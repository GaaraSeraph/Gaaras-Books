# Chapter 1: Awakening in Ash

Dust filled his mouth.

Gaara coughed, and that was the first thing that went wrong.

The cough came out clean. One dry rasp, done, gone. No hook in the chest. No whistle at the top of the breath, no burning behind the sternum that meant the rest of the night was going to be spent sitting upright.

He lay in the dark under a weight of stone and waited for his body to punish him.

It didn't.

Something was pressing down on him, close and heavy, and the air tasted of ash and old rot. Fine. Serious problems, all of them. But underneath the fear sat something stranger, and it took him a stupid moment to name it, because he had not felt it since his twenties.

He felt fine. Not comfortable. Fine as in a body doing what it was told.

He shoved upward. Grit tore across his forearms. His muscles took the load and burned, a deep honest burn, the kind that means work and not damage. No knives in the joints. No heart going off like a car alarm.

He pushed again, harder. Something above him shifted and groaned.

Dust poured down and stung his eyes. He sucked in a breath through his teeth, and it went all the way to the bottom of his lungs.

He almost let go of the slab.

Twenty years of half breaths, of measuring stairs, of the machine on the nightstand hissing all night, and his lungs had just filled up like it cost nothing.

"Fuck my life. Where am I? This office wasn't old enough to fall down."

His voice came out wrong. Too deep, too clear, no gravel in it. He filed it and kept pushing.

Something sharp opened a line across his shoulder. He barely registered it, which was interesting, because he had spent a decade registering everything his body did.

A gap opened in the rubble. He got his shoulder into it, worked one arm through, and then the rest of him tumbled out onto a slope of broken stone.

He lay there breathing.

The last thing he remembered was the tatami mat. Three weeks old, the good kind, and the most expensive thing he still owned, because by then it was also his bed. The flat had gone in spring. He had told exactly two people, and both had assumed he was joking, which told him something about the two people.

So: the office. Lights off, blinds down, mat rolled out between the desk and the wall where nobody could see it through the glass. Lie down at an hour that no longer counted as night. Set an alarm, because there was a call, and after the call another call. And at the end of all of them a number with a minus in front of it that had been gaining on him for two years.

Then the mat. Then dust.

He sat up.

And that was the part that would not sit still in his head later. No crash. No horn, no chest pain, no long slide into a white ceiling with people shouting over him. He had gone to sleep at thirty-eight on a mat in his own office, and he had woken up here.

No death. Just a cut in the tape.

The room was enormous, and it was dead.

Grey stone, half of it fallen in, walls climbing up and stopping in jagged teeth against an open sky the colour of dishwater. Light came through a hole in the roof in a single slanted beam, thick with drifting dust. Splintered beams. Iron brackets, rust-eaten, still clamped into the stone they had been driven through. Ash over everything, soft and even, the way snow lies.

"Okay," he said. "That's not my office."

He got his feet under him and stood up, and the standing worked on the first attempt, which was the most alarming thing that had happened so far.

Then he looked down at himself.

Bare feet on cold stone. A rough undyed shirt tied at the waist, trousers of the same. No phone. No watch.

And under the shirt, a stomach. Flat. Not smaller. Flat, with muscle under his hand where for years there had been a soft heavy swell he could not look at in shop windows.

His nails were clean and pink, with no yellow in them at all.

"No," he said, to nobody.

He went down on one knee beside a puddle in the hollow of a cracked flagstone and looked at his own face.

Nineteen.

Not thirty-eight on a good day. Not thirty-eight after a month of sleep. Nineteen years old, jawline like a cut, skin clear, brown eyes bright and entirely unfamiliar. That was the face from the summer he trained twice a day and moved weight he had no business moving, when a body was a thing you argued with and won.

He touched his cheek. The reflection touched back.

For thoroughness, and because there was nobody around to be dignified in front of, he checked one more thing.

"Well. At least the important upgrades came through."

The laugh cracked in the middle and stopped, because the fear arrived properly, and it arrived cold.

He was not himself. He was not the man who had been quietly disassembled over ten years by his own ambition and his own organs. That man had wanted to help everyone. That man had watched it come apart while lying on his back, listening to a machine breathe for him. The humiliation had been the worst part. Not the pain. The being handled.

That man was gone. Somebody had thrown him away and left this in his place.

"What is this?" he whispered.

The air in front of his face answered.

A panel unfolded out of nothing, a hand's width from his nose. Translucent, faintly lit from inside, hanging there without a projector or a wire or a single good reason.

He tried to wave it off like an insect.

It stayed.

His mind, the only piece of his old life that had come with him, started producing explanations. Light refraction. A stroke. A very expensive holographic rig operated by someone with a hostage plan and poor priorities.

Every one of them died on contact with the fact that he could feel the ash on his tongue.

"I have dreamed about this. It is too real to be a dream."

He reached out. His hand went straight through it, and the text sharpened as his eyes settled on it, like it had been waiting for him to pay attention.

He read it. Then he read it again.

His name, spelled correctly. A level. Hit points, mana points and five attributes, laid out the way ten thousand character sheets had been laid out for him on evenings too tired for anything else.

CON 5.

He looked at that one for a long time.

Five. The lowest thing on the board by a clear margin.

It was correct. That was the obscene part. He could put on strength like other people put on a coat, ten kilos on the bar in a fortnight, while the men beside him ground away for a year and got nowhere. Endurance never came at all. Two sessions a day at nineteen, and he still lost his wind on a flight of stairs and still woke up wrecked after a full night. His body would take load and would not take time.

Then his eyes went one line up, and the whole thing stopped making sense.

STR 11.

Eleven. One better than average, if average was ten.

At nineteen he had pressed a hundred and forty kilos off his chest eight times in a row, and he had been quietly astonished by it himself, because nothing about him had suggested that was in there. The bar had bent. Men who had lifted for a decade had come over to watch and then gone back to their own sets in a slightly worse mood.

Whatever that was, it was not one point better than the man who delivered his post.

So ten was not the average man.

He stood very still in the ash and worked it forward. Ten was a floor, and everything standing on it had stopped being a person some way below. The scale started somewhere above human and ran up from there, and the higher it ran, the more each single point would be hiding.

He had no idea what a bear scored on this thing.

He had no idea what was scratching around in the next room.

"Great," he said. "The bar is not where I left it."

His knees, for the record, were operated on four months after that summer. Both of them. He had walked out of the second surgery with a sheet of printed exercises and a low grinding objection every time he ran. That was the beginning of the long argument his body eventually won.

He looked down. Nineteen-year-old knees, unopened, unscarred.

Then he read the panel again and noticed what was not on it.

No charisma. No luck. No appearance. Whatever this thing was, it had gone through him line by line and had decided that how much people liked him was none of its business.

"Huh," he said. "Points for taste."

Then the rest of it landed, and the cold came back up through his feet.

This was transmigration. Isekai. He had read an embarrassing volume of it at three in the morning, on his phone, on nights when breathing was work and sleeping was worse. A world with rules you could see.

He was in one.

The pain in his shoulder said so. The ash said so. The 5 in his constitution said so, because it meant a system existed and had rated him.

His old life came back all at once, in the wrong order.

The corporate years first, because that was where it started.

Thirty-nine companies. He had counted them once, on a bad night, the way other people count ex-partners. Thirty-five of them corporations, some with names you saw on stadiums. Employee, contractor, consultant, interim. He had seen those buildings from the mailroom floor, from the top of the table, and from the seat where you present to the board and watch six faces decide nothing.

Different logos. Different industries. Different countries, twice.

Same machine. Every single time.

He could have picked any example out of nineteen years. The one that stuck was eighteen months of work, finished, tested, signed off, quietly filed away because two directors were at war over a budget line and the project was born in the wrong department. Nobody looked at it at all. It belonged to the losing side, and losing sides do not ship.

Results were not the currency. Proximity was. He had watched people fail upward past him with a competence he could only describe as artistic. After enough companies to constitute a study, he stopped hoping the next one would be different. He handed in his notice and walked out with a payout and a plan.

The plan even worked, for a while. His own company, his own rules, his own money in it, running for two years the way he had always known it could when nobody was allowed to sabotage it. Then the growth flattened. The costs did not. He cut, put his own money back in, cut again, and told himself each quarter that the next one would turn.

Then the doctors, one after another, the same expression on the way in and a worse one on the way out. Nothing more we can do. Said politely, over a desk, while he nodded like a professional.

Control. That was all he had ever wanted and the exact thing he had never had. He had tried to build something so solid that no illness and no partner and no market could take it off him. It had come down anyway, and his body had come down with it.

And now here he was, standing in a dead building in a dead city, nineteen years old, with a window in the air telling him he was Level 1.

"Level 1," he said, and a dry laugh got out.

It was terrifying.

It was the best thing that had ever happened to him.

Something came up out of his chest, too big to hold, and he let it go.

"I can start again. I CAN START AGAIN!"

Arms out, ash in his hair, blood running down one arm, laughing like an idiot, and the ruin threw it back at him from every direction.

The echo faded. Silence came back in and sat down.

He dragged a forearm across his face and breathed out, long and slow.

"Crippling depression. Borderline disorder. PTSD. ADHD. Too many body illnesses to count."

He listened to how the words sounded out loud, in this voice, in this air.

"None of that matters anymore."

He said it firmly, the way you sign something.

Whether it was true was a question for later.

He thought about what he actually had. No allies. No money. No language, probably. No idea what year it was or whether years were the unit here. One body he had not earned, one window full of numbers, and a lifetime of being let down by everyone he had ever relied on, which had taught him the only lesson he ever fully absorbed.

Do it yourself. Then nobody can drop it.

He turned to the gap in the wall, where the chamber opened into deeper dark.

The air coming through it was cold and smelled of wet earth, and under that of something musky and alive that his brain had no name for and disliked immediately.

Every instinct he owned said go the other way. The other way was a hole in a roof forty feet up.

His bare feet made no sound in the ash. The panel travelled with him, hanging politely at the edge of his vision, lit from inside, patient as a lamp nobody had asked for.

He put his back to the cold stone beside the opening and looked through.

A long chamber, low and wide. Fallen timbers. Broken masonry in drifts. Dust turning in the few beams of light that made it down that far.

Nothing moved.

But something was in there. He could feel it on his skin, the way you feel someone standing behind you in an empty room. Heavy. Patient. Interested.

His hands closed into fists, and he noticed with distant surprise that he was not shaking.

Not this time. Not illness, not circumstance, not whatever was breathing in the dark forty feet away.

Never again.

Then the roar came out of the blackness, and it went through his bones like a struck bell.

# Chapter 2: First Blood, First Skill

The roar ripped through the ruin. The sound hit Gaara in the sternum before it reached his ears, a low pressure that rattled loose grit from the walls.

His head snapped up. Shapes moved in the dark beyond the collapsed support beam. Not one.

Two.

They came out into the weak light together, and his stomach dropped out of him.

Segmented bodies, bulbous and low. Six spiked legs each, folding and unfolding with a dry clicking rhythm. Two more limbs above, thin as whips and barbed along their length, tasting the air in slow sweeps. Chitin the colour of old bruises.

Something in his skull supplied a name he had never learned. Gloom Crawler. It arrived fully formed, the way you remember a word in a dream, and he had no time to be disturbed by it.

"Gosh, ain't you a beauty. I fucking hate insects."

The stench reached him a second later. Decay and something metallic underneath, like a mouthful of old coins. The larger one swung its head toward him, a wet mess of plating and six red eyes, and every one of them found his face at once.

A single click. Wet. Deliberate.

"Please take a shower once in a while, motherfucker."

The old Gaara would already be on the floor. He knew this with total clarity. That body would have locked up, chest cinching shut, heart stuttering into something useless, and it would have died here in about nine seconds without landing a finger on anything.

This body did something else. His weight shifted onto the balls of his feet without being asked. His hands came up. Somewhere under the fear there was a low hum of readiness he had never felt in his life.

The big one lunged.

Barbed limbs tore through the space where his head had been. He was already moving, throwing himself flat and rolling across broken stone, and pain cracked through his knee as it came down on something jagged.

He ignored it. Filed it. Kept moving.

Adrenaline went through him like a lit fuse. The world narrowed and sharpened at the same time. He could hear the individual scrape of each leg on stone. He could feel the shift in the air as the second one flanked him, smaller, faster, keeping low.

Weapon. He needed a weapon. His eyes raked the floor as he scrambled backward.

Collapsed slabs. Iron pins, rusted through, standing out of the stone like broken teeth. Splintered beams. Shards of something dark scattered near a fallen column.

Obsidian.

One piece was as long as his shin, broken to a wicked taper, glinting dull and greasy in the light. His fingers closed around the rough end and the weight of it settled into his palm like it had been waiting.

"Nice. That would definitely beautify you guys."

The big one came again, too fast to think about. He didn't think. He sidestepped and brought the stone up in a clumsy panicked block, and the barbed whip cracked across the flat of it hard enough to numb his arm to the shoulder.

He staggered. Kept his feet.

The obsidian held. It hadn't shattered.

That changed things. That made this a fight instead of an execution.

The smaller Crawler circled wide, chittering low in its throat, red eyes burning. Neither of them rushed him now. They were assessing. Comparing notes, probably, on the softest place to open him up.

"Now. Where is your ass? I would like to stuff it with Blacky."

Focus.

They were fast, but they moved in straight lines. Commit, strike, reset. No feints. And those bodies were plated in sections, which meant seams, which meant the armour ended somewhere.

He backed up and let them come, keeping his feet shuffling, never planting. The big one favoured wide sweeps with its forelimbs, throwing its head high on the follow-through. The small one darted at his ankles, low and mean, then peeled off before he could turn.

Sweat ran into his eyes. His thighs burned. This body was strong, but it had never been taught anything, and strength with no technique burns out fast. He had maybe a minute of this left in him.

So make it count.

The big one reared for another sweep, forelimbs opening wide, head tipping back.

There.

A seam. Just behind the skull, where the plating met the thorax, a band of pale membrane exposed for as long as the head stayed up.

The small one lunged at his ankle. He let it come. Felt barbs open a hot line across his calf and used the pain, pushing off the ground into a leap that had absolutely no technique in it at all.

He brought the obsidian down point first, all his weight behind it, every kilo of a body he had not earned and every year of a life he had wasted poured into one downward thrust.

Crunch.

The sound was wet and awful and immensely satisfying. The point punched through the membrane and grated against something softer underneath. The shriek that came out of the thing went through his teeth.

Black ichor pumped out over his wrist, hot as fresh coffee.

"Nice. Not the ass, but quite effective, I'd say."

Then the current hit him.

It ran up the obsidian and into his arm, and it wasn't pain. It was the opposite of pain. It poured into his chest and lit something up behind his ribs, a bright greedy warmth, and some deep animal part of him wanted it again immediately.

The Crawler convulsed. Its legs hammered the ground in a broken drumroll, folding under it one by one. He ripped the shard free and stumbled back, spraying black.

The red eyes dimmed. Went out.

The body came apart into light, motes of it lifting off the corpse like sparks off a fire and drifting up toward the hole in the roof.

He watched an entire living thing turn into ash and glitter, and he was breathing hard, and he was grinning.

"Does that mean a level up? Now. Second cutie. Come here. I'll dance with you. I can be quite sensual, you know?"

He turned to face it. Somewhere in the last thirty seconds the arithmetic had inverted, and he was no longer the thing being hunted.

The smaller Crawler didn't accept the invitation.

It skittered backward, chitin scraping stone, putting a fallen pillar between them. Red eyes tracked him over the broken edge. Patient. Calculating.

"Oh. You watched that."

Cold understanding settled in his gut. It had seen its bigger sibling die. It had learned. Whatever these things were, they weren't just hungry meat with legs.

His arm still trembled from the kill. The obsidian felt slick now, black ichor coating his grip, and the stuff burned faintly where it touched his skin. Something worse than acid. Something that itched.

He shifted his stance. The Crawler mirrored him.

Left. It moved left. He moved left. It stopped.

"You're a coward. I respect that. I built a whole career on it."

His breath came steady, deep, and that alone was still a miracle he hadn't stopped noticing. Old lungs would have quit three minutes ago. These held.

But steady breath didn't win fights. The thing was faster than him. Lower to the ground, harder to reach, and it had already stopped charging blind. Repeating the same trick would get his throat opened.

So don't repeat it. Change the board.

His eyes swept the chamber. Something colder than panic, and older, clicking over behind his ribs like a machine finding its rhythm. What can cut. What can fall. What can hold weight.

Rubble field to his right, bad footing for a six-legged thing built for speed. Broken pillar between them. And above, a slab of ceiling hanging on two iron pins, rust-eaten and bowed, sagging, groaning whenever the wind shifted through the ruin.

Heavy. Very heavy.

"Alright, gorgeous. Let's get you on your back."

He picked up a fist-sized chunk of masonry with his off hand and threw it past the Crawler, hard, cracking against the far wall.

The creature flinched toward the noise. A quarter second. Half a body length.

He was already moving.

Away from it, into the rubble field, feet slipping on loose stone, deliberately loud, deliberately clumsy. Prey noise. Panic noise. The kind of sound that turns a cautious hunter back into a stupid one.

It worked. Behind him, chitin exploded into motion, limbs hammering the ground in a rapid wet drumbeat, closing fast.

"That's it," he wheezed. "Chase me. Everybody loves the chase part."

Gaara counted. Three strides. Four.

He planted his foot on a canted slab and threw himself sideways under the hanging ceiling piece, rolling through dust, shoulder cracking against stone hard enough to white out his vision.

The Crawler followed him into the gap.

He came up on one knee with the obsidian reversed in his fist and drove it upward, into the rusted pin holding the slab, a full body swing with everything his new shoulders could give.

Metal shrieked. Snapped.

The ceiling came down.

The sound was enormous, a flat concussive crunch that punched the air out of the chamber and threw a wall of grit into his face. Something spattered warm across his forearm. He went down with it, hands over his skull, stone raining across his back.

Then silence, ringing and total.

Then the screaming started.

He raised his head. The slab had caught the Crawler across its hindquarters, pinning it flat against the floor, and the thing was not dead. It was very much not dead. Its front four legs scrabbled uselessly at the stone, gouging pale scratches, dragging its own crushed body a centimetre at a time. Black ichor spread beneath it in a slow glossy pool. The shriek came in pulses, high and wet and endless.

Gaara lay in the dust and listened to it.

His hands were shaking. Not fear. The tremor had a different quality now, a fine electric buzz running from fingertips to elbow, and he understood with perfect clarity that he could simply stay here. Wait. It was dying. It would finish on its own eventually.

The red eyes found him across the floor. Still hungry. Still tracking. Even now.

He got up.

His knee was bleeding. His shoulder felt like someone had driven a nail through the joint. He picked the obsidian out of the rubble, turned it once in his palm to seat the grip, and walked over.

The first one had been panic. Reflex. A body doing arithmetic his mind hadn't agreed to yet.

This was not that.

He crouched by the thrashing head, out of reach of the whipping forelimbs, and waited for the rhythm. Left sweep. Right sweep. The gap between, half a second wide, the same seam of pale membrane behind the skull.

"Shh. Shh." He set the point against it. "Relax for me. It only hurts for the first inch."

He drove the shard in. Leaned his weight onto it. Felt the point grate past chitin, catch, then sink, and he twisted, because something in him knew to twist.

The shrieking stopped.

The corpse dissolved. Light bled out from beneath the slab, motes rising like sparks off a fire, drifting up through the hole in the roof and winking out one by one. The warmth passed through him, and this time the surge didn't tingle. It poured, filling him from the sternum outward, sinking into muscle and bone.

He made it four steps before he threw up.

It came out of him in one violent heave, then another, his whole body wringing itself out. Adrenaline crash. He'd read about it once, in an article he skimmed between meetings, back when death was an abstraction that happened to other people. He knelt in the dust, spat, and laughed, a cracked ugly sound that echoed around the ruined chamber and came back sounding like someone else.

He was alive.

He had never been this alive.

Then the panel returned, and it was not alone.

# Chapter 3: Dead Man's Boots

The grip came out of the nest with a dry sucking sound, like pulling a spoon out of cold porridge.

Gaara sat back on his heels and turned it over.

A knife. A real one, made by a person who had known what they were doing. Steel, thirty centimetres from pommel to point, single edged, with a shallow fuller running most of the way down. The leather wrap on the handle had rotted into black crumbs that came away on his palm, leaving bare tang underneath, and the whole blade wore a coat of rust like orange fur.

Under the rust the edge was still there. He could feel it catch on his thumbnail.

Something happened in his hand when he closed his fingers properly around it. A small correction, barely there, the way a good pen sits differently than a cheap one. His grip shifted a few millimetres without being told to, and his wrist found an angle he had not chosen.

Then the panel came.


[ Dagger Mastery, Rank E ]

Blade assessed.
Field knife, human manufacture. Heavily corroded. Edge geometry intact.
Structural integrity: adequate for soft targets. Avoid bone.


"Avoid bone," he repeated. "Noted. I'll ask them to hold still while I check."

He wiped the blade on his thigh and looked back down at what he had been digging through.

The nest was not just chitin and dirt. Now that he was in it up to his elbows, he could see the rest of it, and the rest of it had been people.

A ribcage, most of a ribcage, picked clean and gone the yellow of old piano keys. A jawbone with three teeth in it. Scraps of cloth so far gone they came apart when he breathed on them. A belt buckle. A boot, then, a metre away, the other one.

He sat there for a while and did not move.

They had come in here the way he had walked in here. Armed better than him, probably. Wearing more than a shirt and a pair of trousers. And they had gone into the dark and something had eaten them and dragged the leftovers home, and the ruin had gone on being quiet and pretty in the slanted light with nothing at all to say about it.

The panel that had congratulated him twenty minutes ago had not mentioned them either.

"Right," he said quietly. "So that's the deal here."

He took the boots.

He hated it while he did it, and he did it anyway, and he was aware of exactly how fast that decision had gone through him. Six weeks ago he had spent four days agonising over whether to let an intern go. Now he was pulling footwear off a stranger's remains and the entire process of deciding had taken less time than it took to lace them.

They were too big. Half a size, maybe more, the leather stiff and cracked across the toe. He shredded a strip off the bottom of his shirt, wadded it into the front of each one, and pulled the laces until his feet stopped swimming.

# Chapter 13: The Cost of Shutting a Door

The gear was under the byre.

Gaara stood in the doorway and looked at the floor he had slept on two nights ago. At the four goats. At Hallvard levering up a flagstone with the ferrule of a stick he did not lean on.

"I slept on it."

"You did."

"You put me in the one building in this village with it under the floor."

"I put you where I could find you," said Hallvard, "and where the thing I did not want found was under a stranger who did not know it was there. If you had gone looking, you would have gone looking somewhere else."

"That is either very good or very lazy and I cannot decide which."

"Both, usually."

Under the stone there was a dry pit lined with slate, and in the pit there was a windlass.

No village made this. Oak drum, iron gudgeons, a pawl and ratchet cut by somebody who understood what a pawl is for, and a coil of chain that had been greased so many times the links were black with it. Four oak levers. A dozen iron wedges. Two blocks and a sling.

"Whose was it?"

Windlass, human manufacture. Oak, iron.

Rated well above intended load.

"No."

"No."

"Yes."

"For nine hours."

"Now you have it."

"Six men," she said. "You are going to have to ask."

"Yes."

"One. Killing them does not solve it. There are four hundred nests in that room and the thing that makes them is behind a line I cannot see across. I could stand in the shallows opening crawlers for a year and get old and rich and change nothing." He held up a finger. "Two. The gate solves the road. We cannot touch the room and I am not going to pretend otherwise, but everything that has died in your fields came down a channel, and a channel is a thing with a door on it."

"And three?" said Marit.

"Three is unpleasant." He looked at Hallvard. "I want to go back in there several times before we shut it."

The old man's head came up.

"Because that room pays," said Gaara. "You know it pays. You have been down there thirty-one years."

"I have never once looked at it that way."

"No, and that is the difference between us, and I would not spend much time deciding which of us it flatters." He leaned forward. "When I killed a six down there yesterday it was worth most of a step to me. I want to hunt in the room I am about to seal."

"I want to use it. There is a difference and it is mostly in the timing."

Marit said, "How many times?"

Nobody said anything for a while. One of the goats came over and investigated the pit and was moved.

"Hallvard," said Gaara. "What level are you?"

"I do not know what that means."

"You do. Marit told you what I see. Numbers over people." He watched the old man's face and got nothing off it, which was itself a data point. "You have killed sixty or seventy of those things over thirty-one years, most of them bigger than you, in the dark, alone. If it counted for you the way it counts for me, you would be the most dangerous man between here and the sea."

"I am a seventy-year-old man with a bad hip," said Hallvard, "who is better with a stick than he should be. That is all I have ever noticed."

"There is nothing over your head. Nothing at all. Everyone else in this village has a name and a number and you have empty air."

"Then I would say," said Hallvard slowly, "that I have been down in that room a great many times. That there is a line cut in the floor of it. And that I have crossed it more often than any living thing."

Gaara went very still.

"Say that again."

"You heard it."

"I want it twice," said Gaara. "Liars decorate."

Hallvard almost smiled.

"I have crossed the line in that floor more often than any living thing," he said, in exactly the same words. "Make of it what you like. I never could."

They washed at the trough behind Solva's house because Solva made them, and because after three days there was not much argument left in either of them.

Gaara got his shirt off and Marit looked at the state of him and said something short and Nordic that the thing in his head did not translate, which he took as a compliment and said so.

"You have no idea what it meant."

"It is what I have decided it meant. I am keeping it."

She washed one-handed and he ended up helping with the strapped arm, which was practical and businesslike and took a great deal longer than practical and businesslike needed to take.

"Marit."

"Mm."

"For the record."

Her good hand stopped.

"The word is for something else," she said.

"No," Gaara agreed. "It is not. I am misusing an agreed signal in a private capacity and I want that noted. I have exactly one thing in this world that is worth anything, and it is that I do not say things that are untrue."

"Say a true thing, then."

"I have been in this world three days," said Gaara. "In that time I have been threatened with an axe, put on trial in a road, and told a woman her son was dead. I watched a man die because I planned for one. Something reached into my chest in the dark and took a piece out. And the only part of any of it I would repeat is the walking."

Marit did not say anything.

"That is the true thing," he said. "I would repeat the walking."

She looked at him for a long moment with water running off her wrist.

"Byre's this way," she said. "You snore, you sleep outside."

"That is what you said the first night."

"It is." She picked up her shirt. "It was not an invitation then."

He did not sleep in the byre.

The arm was the first problem and it was not a small one. She had barbs across the right forearm and up under the arm into the ribs, and she could not put weight on that side. She got about a third of the way out of her shirt before she made a noise she had not wanted to make.

"I am not an invalid."

"You are not an invalid and I am not being noble. I am being slow on purpose and you are in my way." He got the shirt over the strapping without touching it. "Now be quiet a moment. I have not looked at you properly since the road."

"You look at everybody properly. It is the most irritating thing about you."

"I look at everybody for information," said Gaara. "This is not that."

She let him.

He kissed her first, and she had a shape in her head for how that would go, and it was not that shape.

The first one was closed lips and nothing else, and it did not last long. The second was less careful than the first, and not by very much. By the third she had come up off the pillow to meet it, which he noted with enormous satisfaction and did not act on. The fourth had the tip of his tongue in it for about a second, and then did not.

After that it went up. It kept going up all night and it never once went up by more than she had already come forward to take. That was the method entire, and there was nothing else in it. Give her a shade less than she has just asked for, and make her ask again.

It got wild later on. It never got wet. He had views about that which he kept to himself.

He had spent three days being handed information about this world in writing. None of it had covered anything he wanted to know now. There was no number over any of this and no window that would open on it, and the only way to have it was to work it out from scratch.

So he worked it out. The inside of her wrist, above where the strapping started. The base of her throat. The place behind her ear where her jaw met her neck, which shut her good hand hard in the blanket. The back of her knee, which surprised her a great deal more than it surprised him.

He spent longer on her breasts than she had allowed for. She had a commentary running when he started and she did not have it by the end, and she never once conceded either of those things out loud.

"I do not know anything. I am watching you and you keep answering."

"Stop narrating."

"Absolutely not."

He learned the ribs by the sound she made when he came near them, and after that he went round them, and she noticed him going round them and said nothing about it. He kept his weight off her right side for the whole of the night without once mentioning that he was doing it. Then he went lower, and she got a fistful of his hair and held on to it.

"What is that."

"Something nobody in this valley appears to have thought of, which I intend to take up with somebody in the morning." He waited where he was. "One syllable and I go back to what I was doing, and I will not sulk about it."

She did not say the syllable.

"Then you get four words," said Gaara, "and they are the only four you will ever need from me. Slower. Faster. Higher. Lower."

"What am I supposed to do with four words."

"Use them. You will not have to explain yourself and I will not stop to ask."

She came apart twice before she said anything at all about it and once more afterwards, and he kept count, and he was aware that keeping count was an odd thing to be doing.

"Is that a thing where you come from," she said.

"It is."

"Everybody."

"Broadly."

"Then everybody where you come from is very stupid about a great many things," said Marit, "and extremely sensible about one."

Then there was not.

His arms had opinions by the end of it. He ignored them, because the only thing in that room he was interested in was the sound she was making, and he would have gone on until the roof came off.

Nothing she did to him was skilled. Nothing else. No line for this. No number for any of it.

Six men. Four hours. A tunnel.

Forty houses that had stood in their doorways, and a stranger who had told them so in their own road, at volume, with an audience.

It's always the audience.

# Chapter 14: Say It in the Road

He did it wrong first, and in the way he had done it wrong for nineteen years.

The channel. The gate. The eighteen inches. The four hundred nests. The windlass under the floor they were standing on, four men on the bars, two on the wedges, four hours in the dark.

He was clear and brief and put it in front of forty houses like a proposal that had been checked twice.

Then he waited, and they looked at their boots.

"Well?" said Gaara.

Somebody coughed.

"Is he finished?" a man said, further back.

"He is never finished," said Marit, from the byre wall. "He takes his time. And then he takes some more time. And then, when you have entirely given up on him, he gets there."

Somebody laughed. Several people did.

Gaara turned round slowly.

"Nobody has ever accused me of being quick," said Gaara. "They accuse me of other things."

Then, much lower, and only to her:

"And somebody had to go and save her village this morning, so I did not get to finish dinner. Or the midnight snack. Or the second midnight snack. And then she was up and dressed before it was light."

"You were asleep."

"I was recovering. There is a difference and it is mostly in the dignity."

"You will want a word about this later."

"I will want a word about this later."

"You will take your time over it," said Marit, "and then you will take some more."

It bought nine seconds of goodwill, which was all he got, and he knew who had earned them.

Then the heavy man with the boar spear said, "Why should we?"

"Because in the spring it will not be four. And because the pay is one loaf, four hours on a bar in the wet, and my continued company in a confined space." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I have accepted worse offers than that, and I want you to know I regretted most of them."

Nobody moved.

He felt the thing arrive that he had felt in the road at three in the morning, with the smell coming up out of the north. The same thing. The same shape.

The information was right. It had always been right. It was worth nothing in his mouth.

It's always the audience.

"In my old life," he said, mostly to Marit, "I once failed to get eleven people to agree on the colour of a folder. I have no idea why I thought forty would queue up for a tunnel."

"Right," he said. "That is my fault and not yours. Give me an hour."

Hallvard was at his table with his hands flat on it, and he did not ask how it had gone.

"They will not come for me," said Gaara. "They will come for you. Thirty-one years of being the man who is right about things is the only account in this village with anything in it, and I am here to ask you to spend it."

"You do not need to ask. I will stand in the road and tell them to go, and they will go."

"That is beside what I am asking for."

The old man's eyes came up.

"They cannot go down there not knowing," said Gaara. "Four hours in a tunnel with the mouth of that room forty feet away. If it goes wrong it goes wrong in nine seconds, and men who were told the wrong thing do the wrong thing in nine seconds. They have to know what is behind the gate, how long you have been going down, and why it is open."

Hallvard sat very still.

"You want me to say it in the road."

"You could say it for me."

"I could not," said Gaara. "I have one asset in this village and it is that nothing I have said here has been untrue. I am not going to spend it telling your story for you. It has to come out of your mouth, in front of the houses you have been counting for thirty-one years." He straightened up. "Also, I am not doing it because it is yours. That is bookkeeping, and I would rather you did not mistake it for kindness."

"They will take the elder off me," said Hallvard.

"Almost certainly. On the other hand you get a full night's sleep for the first time in thirty-one years, and at your age that is not nothing."

"That is a very poor joke."

"It is the best one available. I looked."

"Aldis's father did eleven years and died of it with nobody knowing. Her uncle did nineteen. I have done thirty-one, and not one year of it has been known, and that was not vanity, it was the design."

"The design has four graves in it now, and a fifth if you count the dogs together. Designs do that. They run for years, then something changes underneath them, and they run one more season on momentum, and then they stop."

Hallvard looked at his own hands.

"You have done this before," he said. "Not the ruin. This."

"I was usually the one holding it," said Gaara. "Nobody told me either, until it was in the road."

The old man got up and picked up the stick, and he did not lean on it.

"Come and hold the door," he said.

It took him four minutes.

He stood in the middle of the paved road with his stick in his hand and said it in order, and he did not decorate any of it.

Gaara stood at the edge of the crowd and understood that he was watching a man do the most expensive thing he would ever do, in flat sentences.

Thirty-one years. Aldis before him. Her uncle before her. Nobody wrote it down, and that was deliberate, and here is why. And in the spring I raised the gate eighteen inches on my own arithmetic. I was right about the rate and wrong about the noticing. The boy who was fishing, the man with the cart, Teodor and Osvald are on that.

He did not say sorry once.

Gaara noticed that, and approved of it, and knew exactly how few people in that road would.

"Thirty-one years of practice," he said, to Marit, without lowering his voice much. "You get very good at the part where you do not apologise. I was better at it than he is and I only had nineteen."

"Give it a year," he said. "They will hate him for a year. Then somebody's roof will come off, and they will want the man who is right about things, and he will be right about the roof, and all of this will be forgiven in about nine minutes."

"You are enjoying this."

"I am appalled by this. I can do both."

It went the way those things go. Silence, and then noise. A woman near the front said the same sentence four times with her hands out. Somebody at the back shouted about the lord. Solva pushed through and stopped dead and said nothing at all.

And in the middle of it Bern said, "Who's going down?"

Which was the whole game, and Gaara moved.

"Six," he said. "Four on the bars, two on the wedges. Everyone gets told everything before they set foot in the channel, and anybody who wants out can be out at the mouth. I will think no worse of him. I will think a great deal worse of a man who changes his mind halfway, because at that point I will be busy."

He was reading the road while he talked, and by now he was not trying to stop. Faces came up with names and numbers on them, and he sorted them without deciding to.

Bern was a 4. The heavy man with the boar spear was a 5. Torv, wide and slow, near the pens, was a 6.

And a woman of about forty behind the smith's gate, who had said nothing all morning and whose name he did not know, came up as a 7.

Seven. The highest thing he had seen in that village.

"You," he said.

She looked at him.

"I do not know your name," said Gaara, "and I am going to ask you first anyway, and I am not going to explain why in the road."

"Ylva."

"Will you come?"

Torv, the wide slow man near the pens, said, "She's a woman."

"Yes," said Gaara. "I did notice. I have been noticing things all week. It is how I found your cellar."

Somebody at the back made a sound.

"She is what I want," said Gaara. "I said six men because the old arrangement said it and I repeated it without thinking. I have been wrong about numbers four times this week and I am not going to be wrong about this one out of politeness."

Ylva looked at him for a moment.

"My husband went down with Aldis," she said. "He came back the first time."

Nobody had told Gaara that. He filed it and did not use it, and was aware that a worse man would have.

"Then you know what the bars are."

"Aye."

Bern came, and Torv, who did not apologise for the remark and came anyway, which Gaara considered the more useful of the two, and a silent man of thirty called Ask.

The heavy man with the boar spear came up close first.

"I still think you have got a mouth on you."

"You are correct. It is the only part of me that has never been injured, and I have been working on that for nineteen years."

That was the entire negotiation.

Five.

He stood in the road with the sun going and did not get a sixth.

"I will take the sixth bar," said Hallvard.

"You are seventy."

"I have taken that bar before."

"You have also opened a gate before, and I would like today to be a day on which we do not revisit your greatest achievements."

"With four men who are now dead of ordinary things. I need you at the wedges, where knowing what you are doing matters more than what you can lift."

Hallvard almost smiled.

"You have got me at the wedges and Ylva on a bar," he said. "You are picking by something."

"Yes," said Gaara.

"Will you tell me by what?"

"No," said Gaara.

The sixth came at full dark and it was Solva, and it took ten minutes and both hands to talk her out of it.

"You are sixty, and I have watched you carry bread with two hands."

"He was my son."

"He was, and he is not down there, and the thing that is down there does not care whose son he was. If you come and it goes wrong, I will spend four hours watching you instead of the mouth of that room. So will Marit. So will Bern. That is how six people become four."

Solva stood in her doorway with the cold lamp on its stone beside the step.

"You will not lie to me," she said, and it came out flat, with no question in it.

"No."

"Then say it plain."

"You would slow us down and it might kill somebody," said Gaara. "That is plain."

Then she went in and came back with a loaf wrapped in cloth, put it in his hands, and shut the door.

Gaara stood in the dark holding bread, which was, on balance, the worst he had felt all week.

It got worse about forty minutes later.

Marit found him at the byre and stopped in the doorway, which told him something before she spoke.

"Ketil's horse is gone," she said. "And Ketil with it."

"Who is Ketil."

"Her dead husband's brother. He was at the back of the road this morning, shouting about the lord. He went east at dusk, and his wife says he took a bag and the good coat."

He put the bread down on the rail very carefully, because the alternative was to throw it.

"How far east."

"Two days to the Wold, where the lord's man sits. Three if the ford is up."

"Of course it is two days. It is always two days." He was looking at the north wall of the byre and not at her. "The gap between a man deciding to ruin something and the ruin arriving is always exactly long enough to be unbearable and never long enough to be useful. It is the only law of nature I fully trust."

He stood in the dark of the byre and did the arithmetic he did not want.

Thirty-one years of an old man saying nothing, and a chain of it going back past Aldis and her uncle and her father. It had all held until a stranger with a clean argument required the truth said out loud in a road.

"I did that," he said.

"He did that."

"Hades has iron in a shed and a name in a book and a lord's officer somewhere east of here with a wax tablet, and I am twenty miles up a hill four days out of five. I need somebody standing in the Wold who knows what a warden looks like when he is about to be difficult, and who can get a message to Gunnar. Somebody who has already learned that yard by heart, because he could not think of anything else to do in it."

"He informed on us."

"He did. And he has been paying for it in a yard, and I do not need him forgiven." Gaara looked at Ketil. "I need him useful. Those are different too."

"He is my husband's brother," said Ylva.

"I know exactly whose brother he is, and so does everybody, and half of Oldstep is going to say I have gone soft in the head." He shrugged, and his shoulder had stopped complaining about it a week ago. "And the other half is going to notice that the man who informed on them now works for them and cannot afford to do it twice, and that is the half I would back."

Ylva stood there for a long moment.

Then she went and stood in front of Ketil, and did not soften her face at all.

"I am not forgiving you," she said.

"I know."

"I am putting you to work. You will do it properly, and you will do it for a year, and at the end of the year we will see where we are." She held out her hand, which Gaara had never once seen her do. "Half of that iron is mine. So half of you is mine, and I want the half that gets up early."

Ketil took her hand.

"You are hiring him," said Torv, later, in the street, in the tone of a man who has been thinking about something for an hour and has not improved it.

"I am."

"He told on us."

"He did, and he was the only man in that road who did the thing his conscience told him to, and his conscience was wrong, and mine has been wrong twice this month." Gaara did not slow down. "I am not going to run a company where the entry requirement is having been right so far. There would be nobody in it, starting with me."

Runa came to the room over Frida's stable that night, and there were no other people in it, because he had paid Frida for that in the afternoon and had not mentioned it to anybody.

"You did not ask me," she said, in the doorway.

"No."

"You told me where you sleep, a week and a half ago, and then you went away and killed something and came back with four carts, and you have not said one word about it since you got here."

"I do not ask twice." He was sitting on the end of the bed with his boots half off. "I said it once in a market. It was true then and it is true now and there is nothing I could add to it except pressure."

Runa shut the door.

"That," she said, "is the most calculated thing anybody has ever done to me."

"Yes."

"You are not even going to argue."

"I would be arguing about the word and not about the fact, and I have watched men do that and it is contemptible." He got the second boot off. "It is calculated. I worked out in about a second and a half that you were somebody who would not be pushed. So I built the only approach that has never once worked on anybody. And I have thought about you in a stone tunnel more than is decent."

She crossed the room and kissed him.

He put it back at the beginning without saying anything about it.

Closed lips, short, nothing else. Then a little more and not much. Then more again, and by the fourth one she was coming forward off her own weight to meet it, and he gave her fractionally less than she had just asked for, and did it again, and again.

She stopped and pulled her head back about six inches.

"You are giving me less than I just asked you for."

"Yes."

"Every time. Deliberately."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because you are going to ask again," said Gaara.

Runa looked at him in the dark with her hand still on the back of his neck. He watched a woman work out in four seconds what nobody in either world had ever caught him doing, and there had been nothing better in either of them.

"You do that to everybody."

"I do that to everybody."

"And nobody has ever said it out loud."

"Nobody has ever noticed," said Gaara, "and I have just found out what that is worth, and it is a very great deal more than I expected."

She got him out of the shirt with a good deal less ceremony than the conversation had suggested.

Later he gave her the four words, in the dark, without explaining any of them.

"That is a very short vocabulary."

"It is a complete one."

"It is four words."

"It is four words," said Gaara. "Everything you might want to say to me while my mouth is otherwise engaged is one of those four. Find me a fifth and I will pay you for it, the way I have paid you for everything else this week."

She did not find a fifth. She used the second one twice and the third one once, and then stopped using any of them, which was the part he was working for. She was not quiet about it and made no attempt to be. Somewhere below them a horse moved in its stall, and neither of them cared.

Afterwards she lay across him with her chin on her arms and looked at him with an expression he could not price at all.

"You will be gone in two days."

"I will."

"And you will not ask me to come with you."

"No," said Gaara. "That is not restraint. I have a village up that road with a hole in the hill in it." He put a hand flat on her back and left it there. "You have a stall, a workshop, and a man you were sensible enough to buy an opinion about before you paid him a wage. I have nothing to put next to any of that. When I do, I will say it once."

"Once."

"Once."

"You are going to be a problem," said Runa, for the third time, and put her head down.

In the morning there were four carts, one shed, and a quarter of a dead city sold at a price that had not moved. There was a debt to a smith that no longer existed. And there was a man in a good coat with a hole in the elbow, standing in the yard behind the Staple at first light, because nobody had told him what time to be there.

Gaara stood in the street and did the arithmetic he could not help doing, and for the first time since the ash it came out on the right side of the line.

Then he looked at the top of the panel, out of habit, the way a man checks a pocket he knows is empty.

# Chapter 31: Send Me a Bill

The north leg had never been walked, and Gaara went up it first, which was not courage and he said so before anybody could get the wrong idea.

"I am the only one who can read what is on the walls and I am the least useful thing in this party in a fight. Those two facts have been true since harvest and I have decided to stop being sensitive about the second one."

"You are not the least useful," said Torv.

"Torv, you split a leg joint like a man taking a door off its hinges and I have watched you do it four times. I am extremely useful with my mouth and everything after that is negotiable."

Sivert put his head against the wall of the tube.

The north leg went up under the fields and then under the outer streets, arched, a rib every forty paces, dry for two hundred years and dry in a way that suggested somebody had wanted it dry. Marit found the reason at the fourth rib. There was a plate in the slot already. It had been there since before Oldstep had a name, and it had held, and the water had gone round it and cut itself another way out.

"Eight hundred years," said Ylva, with a lamp up against it.

"And it is still in its hole doing its one job." Gaara put a hand on it. "There is a version of a good life in that and I have decided not to examine it too closely."

They found the control four hundred paces further up.

It was not a drum. It was a housing set into the stone at the side of the tube, iron and oak, with a spindle and no bars, and there was nothing to turn because the arms had gone eight centuries ago. What there was, was a mark, cut into the oak in the same hand as the mark on the drum in the byre.

Gaara stood in front of it for a while before he touched it.

"Say something," said Marit.

"I am frightened of a piece of wood and I would rather not have that minuted."

Then he put his hand flat on it.

[ SECTIONAL CONTROL, NORTH LEG, THIRD DISTRICT ]

Drawn: 0
Prior draws on this control: 9,806
Bearer of record: Water Office of Vessarim

He read it four times. Then he sat down on the floor of the tube with his back against the stone, in front of six people holding lamps, and did not say anything for long enough that Sivert crouched down next to him.

"It has a name," he said.

"What has."

"The city." He was still looking at it. "It has a name. Somebody kept its water, and wrote his office on the side of a machine. And I have a village up there that calls it the ruin, because there has been nobody alive to call it anything else for eight hundred years."

"Say the name."

"Vessarim."

Nobody in that tube had ever heard the word. He watched it land on six faces and go nowhere at all, and that was somehow the worst part of the afternoon.

"Nine thousand eight hundred and six," he said. "Turns. On one control, in one district, and there were districts, because it says third. And where it should say who, there is a name."

Ylva got it before anybody else, which by now was the pattern.

"The one in the byre says nobody."

"The one in the byre says nobody." Gaara got up off the floor. "Hallvard has fourteen hundred and twelve turns and no name in the book. This has nine thousand eight hundred and six and an office. Somebody used to be somebody, and then everybody stopped being anybody, and that is what happened to this place, and it is not a story about a monster."

"You do not know that."

"I do not know it. It is the first thing I have wanted to be true since I got here. I am aware of what that does to a man's judgement, and I want somebody to hit me if I start arranging facts around it."

"I will do that," said Marit.

"I know you will. It is one of the four things I like most about you and the other three are not appropriate at this distance from a lamp."

They came down at dusk with nothing on the sledges and everybody talking, which had never once happened before.

Sivert came to the byre that night and did not stand in the doorway working up to it, which had been his entire method for a month.

"I want to say what I want."

"Then say it."

"I have been practising it on the way down the hill."

"Sivert," said Gaara, from the straw, "if you have written it out I am going to have to sit down, and I am already sitting down, and I do not know what happens after that."

He said it.

He said it out loud, in order, without stopping in the middle, and it took him about eleven seconds and he got two thirds of the way through before his voice did something, and he finished it anyway.

Gaara did not say a word.

That was the fourth time in this world, and he counted it while it was happening, which he had never once managed before. The reason was not a hand and it was not an order. It was a nineteen year old who had needed six days to ask for two arms and had just asked for considerably more than that in one go, in the dark, in the flattest voice he owned.

"You have gone quiet," said Sivert.

"I have."

"Is that bad?"

"It is the fourth best thing that has ever happened to me and I will not be ranking the other three tonight." He got hold of the front of Sivert's shirt. "Come here and say the middle part again."

He said the middle part again, slower, with one hand flat on Gaara's chest and the other one holding his jaw.

"I want you on your back. I want to take as long as I want over it and I do not want you to be helpful. And I have thought about your mouth every single day since the forge, and I am finished being polite about that."

"Sivert."

"What."

"Nineteen years of my life I spent teaching grown men to ask for what they want in one sentence," said Gaara, "and not one of them ever managed it, and you have just done it lying on straw with your voice going."

"Is that a yes."

"It was a yes at the third word."

It was slower than the loft and there was nobody to be quiet for, and Gaara made a point of that early and often, and got told to shut up, and did not. Sivert had learned in a month exactly how much he could ask for out loud and had settled on all of it. Twice he stopped in the middle of a sentence because his mouth was needed elsewhere, and both times he picked it up afterwards at the same word. Gaara found that funnier than anything that had happened to him in weeks, and said so, at length, into a shoulder.

Afterwards, with the straw and the dark and a hand somewhere it had no business being.

"You are grabbing."

"I am. I have been grabbing since a doorway in a dead city and I am not going to stop. If you want me to stop you will have to say that out loud as well, and I will believe you."

"I did not say stop."

"No," said Gaara. "You said the middle part twice."

Ketil came up the road at noon the next day on a horse that had been ridden properly.

He had left the Wold before first light, which nobody had asked him to do, and done twenty miles in a morning. He got off in the road in front of forty houses that had spent three weeks calling him a name.

He did not go into any house. He came straight to Gaara, in the road, in front of all of them, which Gaara noticed and put away for later.

"You have ridden it in a morning."

"I have."

"Anybody would have taken two days."

"I would like somebody to notice that," said Ketil.

"I noticed before you were off the horse. That is what I hired." Gaara held his hand out for whatever was in the coat. "Now tell me the thing that made a man do twenty miles before breakfast."

It was a strip of parchment, folded twice, with a seal on it that had been broken and a second fold that had not.

"Gunnar came to the Staple yesterday and asked for the man who speaks for Hades, and there was one, and it was me." Ketil said that entirely flat and it cost him something. "He gave me that. He also said, standing up, not sitting down, that he was sorry about it, which I did not understand and thought you would."

Gaara read it twice.

It was eleven lines. It said that the ruin above Oldstep, and the workings of it, and everything raised out of it, were held of the lord. It said an account would be rendered at the quarter. It said the bearer of the roll would attend.

It did not say a single unpleasant word anywhere in it, and it did not have to.

"What does it mean," said Hallvard.

"It means I did this," said Gaara.

Nobody in that road said anything.

"I stood in front of Gunnar and I told him the hole gives nothing back, and every word of that was true, and it is still true." He folded the parchment along the fold that was already there. "And then I took four carts of eight hundred years of ironmongery into the only town in this valley. I sold a quarter of it in an open market, and had a woman tell three separate people that I have a shed. Which was excellent advice, and I took it, and it worked, and the price held."

"So what is wrong."

"A lord does not care about the difference between a hole and a city. He cares that four carts came out of a hill and got sold." He looked up the road at nothing. "I stood him in front of a display that said this is handled and it gives nothing back, and then I spent a fortnight publicly demonstrating that it gives back a great deal. I have been outmanoeuvred by my own conduct and there is not one part of it I can complain about."

"Then we stop selling," said Torv.

"Then we are a village hiding a thing, which is what this valley has been for thirty-one years, and I have watched what that costs and so has everybody standing here." Gaara put the parchment in his shirt. "No. We do the other thing."

"What is the other thing."

"We ask him for a bill."

He said it in Hallvard's front room with Ylva and Marit and Hallvard and Bern, because those were the four who could stop him.

"You have that face on," said Hallvard.

"Is it a face you would like to sit on?"

"I am seventy."

"That was not a no and I want the room to have heard it."

"Get on with it," said Bern.

He laid it out in about four minutes, with a slate and no decoration.

"An account is the worst of the three. An account means a man comes and looks, and he looks at the shed, and then he looks at the hill, and a man who has looked at a hill wants a share of the hill."

"And a share," said Ylva.

"A share is worse than it sounds and better than it looks. A share means a partner. A partner has opinions about who goes up that hill and when and with what. The first time we lose somebody, the partner writes a rule about it, and it will be a stupid rule, and we will die of it."

"Then what."

"A rent." He put the slate down. "A fixed sum, every year, paid in advance, on the ruin and everything in it. He does not audit a rent. He does not put a man in a chair to check a rent. He gets a number on a day and he stops thinking about us, which is the only thing I have ever wanted from anybody in a position of authority."

"He will want more than a share is worth," said Bern.

"He will want more than a share is worth. He will want considerably more, because a man who is being paid to stop thinking about you knows exactly what that is worth to you." Gaara did not soften it. "That is the price and it is the whole of it. We buy him off, in advance, every year, and in exchange nobody stands in our road with a ledger."

Hallvard sat with his hands on the table.

"In advance," he said.

"In advance."

"We have no money."

"We have a shed," said Gaara.

Ylva stood up.

"No."

"Ylva."

"That shed is the only thing standing between us and the price you have just spent a week protecting." She had both hands flat on the table. "You paid a woman three times to tell you not to open it."

"I did. And she was right, and she is still right, and I am going to do it anyway, and I want you to hear the reason in my own words before somebody else gives it to you in worse ones." He stayed sitting down. "The shed holds the price. The price is worth a great deal over five years. And in one quarter a man arrives with a chair and a list. If I have not paid him first he will still be in that chair in five years, and the price will not matter, because he will own the hill it comes out of."

"We will be desperate."

"We will be desperate on purpose, which is a completely different thing, and it is the only one of the two I have ever been good at."

Ylva did not sit down.

"Half of it is mine."

"Half of it is yours and I cannot open the door without you, and I have not once pretended otherwise, and I am not going to talk you round." He put the slate flat. "So say no, and I will go and find the money somewhere worse, and I will not mention this again as long as I live."

The room was quiet for a while.

"What is worse," said Ylva.

"Borrowing it in the Wold at a rate that would make your teeth ache, from men who would then own a piece of me. Which is exactly the thing I have spent this conversation refusing to sell to a lord."

Ylva stood there for a long moment with her hands on the table.

"Open the shed," she said. "And you will write me the number every quarter. Not tell me. Write it, where I can see it, on a board, on the wall, where Bern can see it too."

"Yes."

"Because I am not doing this on a man's word again."

"No," said Gaara. "You are not, and you should not, and I would like you to notice that you have just invented bookkeeping and that I did not have to say one word about it."

"Do not enjoy yourself."

"It is the only wage I take."

Ketil went back at first light with a letter that Gaara wrote three times.

It was six lines. It did not argue with anything in the notice. It thanked the lord's officer for the clarity and agreed that the ruin and its workings were held of the lord. Then it proposed a fixed annual rent in place of an account, payable in advance, the first ready at the quarter.

At the bottom, in the corner, three strokes, badly.

"That is a leaf," said Ketil.

"Everybody says that," said Gaara. "Get on the horse."
