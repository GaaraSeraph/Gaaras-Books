# Chapter 6: Every Plan I Make Is for One

The byre had four goats in it and smelled accordingly.

Marit set the whetstone on the rail by the door and then did not leave.

"Thirty-one years," she said.

"What?"

"You asked me in the road how long Hallvard has been elder, and then you went quiet about it, which is worse than asking twice." She leaned on the doorpost. "Thirty-one years this winter. My mother was a girl."

"Who had it before him?"

"Aldis Halden. My grandfather's sister. She died in the spring and the houses met in the autumn and they gave it to him."

"How long had he been in Oldstep by then?"

Marit thought about that, and the way she thought about it told him she had never once had to.

"Four winters. Five." She shrugged. "He came up the road. There was a winter he wasn't here and then there was a winter he was."

Gaara left his face where it was.

That afternoon, in the road, in front of forty houses, the old man had said that his grandmother lived eighty years in this house.

Both of those could not be true, and one of them had been said to a crowd.

He turned it over and could not make it mean anything yet, because he did not know what my grandmother was worth in this country. It might be blood. It might be the woman whose house he had married into, or the woman who fed him his first winter, or nothing more than what people here call anyone old and gone. He had the discrepancy and not the dictionary, and a man who guesses at a dictionary gets a person wrong.

So he put it next to the blank air over the old man's shoulders and said nothing.

"Thank you for the stone," he said.

"It's Hallvard's stone. He'll want it back." She pushed off the doorpost. "Get the fat off Solva in the morning, for the steel. Don't work it dry."

"He lends me his own stone and puts me in with the livestock. That is a man handling a room, and I have been handled by professionals."

"Go to sleep."

Then she went, and he was alone with four goats.

"You lot smell like a decision I made badly," he told them.

The nearest one looked at him and kept chewing, and the panel opened over its head.

"Of course you are," he said.

So it was not only people. It read whatever stood in front of him, and it had a number ready for a goat in a byre at midnight, and it had still never once told him anything he wanted to know.

Gaara sat on an upturned bucket with his back against the wall and ran the whetstone along the rusted knife the way the skill wanted him to.

That was the strange part. He had never sharpened anything in his life beyond a kitchen knife, badly, twice. But his wrist found the angle and held it, and corrected when it slipped. Rank E, the lowest recorded proficiency, and already better at this than he was.

So the skill covered more than stabbing. Something had improved his hands without being asked, the way something had put a language in his head, and he had nothing to check either of them against.

He was still turning that over when the goats stopped chewing.

All four at once.

Then the smell came under the door. Decay, and something metallic beneath it, like a mouthful of old coins.

He was outside before he had finished deciding to be.

The night was cold and the road was empty. The smell was not. It came up the paved road out of the north, thick and low, moving the way water moves when it has found a channel.

"Get up," he shouted. "Get out of the houses. They're in the road."

The words came out in her language. That part worked. That part had never been the problem.

Doors opened. Of course they did. A man does not stand in a village at three in the morning and shout without waking it, and Oldstep came up out of its beds the way any village does, slowly and badly and with lamps.

"What is it. What's happening."

"Who's that shouting?"

"That's him. That's the one that came out of the ruin."

"What's he saying?"

"He says there's something in the road."

"What's in the road?"

"Ask him what he means, then."

"Don't go out to him. Stay in the door."

"North," Gaara said again. "It is the direction with the monsters in it."

They came to their doorways in their shirts and held their lamps up, and every one of them looked at him instead of at the north end of the road. A woman four doors up said, twice, that she had known this would happen.

"North," Gaara said. "Look north. I am not asking you to believe me. I am asking you to turn your head."

Two of them turned their heads. The rest kept looking at him.

A heavy man came out as far as his own step and no further, with a lamp in one fist.

"You walked out of that place yesterday," he said. "In Teodor's boots. And now here you are in the middle of my road in the dark, telling us what's coming down it."

"Yes."

"Well then."

"I know how it sounds," Gaara said. "Look north anyway."

"He's not wrong to ask," somebody said.

"He's not wrong to ask," the heavy man agreed, and did not move.

"Wonderful," Gaara said. "Everybody agrees and nothing happens. I have missed this more than I can tell you."

Somewhere behind him a child started crying and was hushed and started again.

Gaara stood in the road with the correct information in his mouth and no way at all to spend it. He had put nineteen years into rooms where being right was not the thing that decided, and he had not expected to meet it again here, in a shirt, in the cold, with something coming.

So he stopped spending it and went to get somebody whose word was worth something.

He passed nine open doorways to reach Marit's, and there was a person standing in every one of them, watching him go by.

He hit the door with the flat of his hand until it opened.

He had a reason for choosing that door and it was a number nobody else in this village could see. Level 6. The highest thing in Oldstep by two clear points.

"They're on the road," he said.

She did not ask how he knew. She looked past him into the dark, once, and put her head back inside and made a noise.

There were no words in it. It was long and it went up at the end, and it came out of her like something she had been carrying since she was a child. It went the length of that road.

Then Oldstep moved.

The people who had been standing in their doorways discussing him went back inside and came out with things in their hands. The heavy man set his lamp on the step and came out with a boar spear. Somebody was shouting for the children to go to the Halden house and somebody else was already carrying two of them up the road.

He had told them the truth and they had stood in their doors and talked about it. She had made a sound with no words in it and emptied the village.

He put that away to be angry about later.

Then they came into the light of the first lamp, and there were three of them.

The big one out of the dark at the north end. Segmented, low, the colour of old bruises, six legs folding and unfolding in the dry clicking rhythm he had heard in a dead chamber yesterday morning.

Two smaller ones behind it, quick, keeping to the wall line.

"Three," Gaara said. "You brought friends. I am flattered. I did not think I had made that kind of impression."

"Off the road," he said, louder. "Get behind the pens."

Nobody moved. Then Marit said it, and they moved.

The road was the only thing in this village built by people who knew what they were doing. Straight, walled both sides, narrow at the top where the eaves nearly touched. Bad ground for six legs built to circle.

He took Marit's woodpile apart with his boot, kicked an armful of the dry stuff into the gap between the houses, and struck the firestone into it. It caught, sulked, and went up.

The big one stopped.

"Look at that," Gaara said. "You're shy."

"It doesn't like the fire," said a man at his shoulder. He was about fifty, he had a felling axe, and both his hands were shaking. "Look at it. It doesn't like the fire."

"It doesn't like it once. Stay on my left, and do not go past me."

"Right. Bern. That's my name. In case."

The first came at his ankles, low and mean. He gave it his boot, not his edge, and stepped off the line, and Bern took it across the back with the axe and it went sideways into the wall.

The second came over the top of it. He got the knife up.

Adequate for soft targets. Avoid bone.

So he did not go for the legs or the head. He went for the join under the second limb where the plate ended, and the steel went in and came out and he was already moving.

"Sorry," he said to it. "I know. Nobody has ever been gentle with you and I am not going to be the first."

The thing screamed and kept working. He had known it would.

Then Marit came in on his left with the hatchet up in both hands and put it into a leg joint, and the thing went half down and dragged itself along the wall out of reach.

"Well struck," Gaara said. "And for the record, the timing of this is deliberate, because you have a magnificent ass and I am never going to get a safer moment to mention it."

"Mind your own side."

"I am minding several sides."

Behind him the road was full of people who had come out of their houses with tools.

That was the moment he built the plan. He built it in about two seconds, and it was a good plan, and it had exactly one body in it.

The big one favoured wide sweeps and lifted its head at the end of them. That was the seam, and to get it the thing had to be high and committed, and to be committed it had to think it was through.

So he would let it through.

He put himself half a pace off the centre of the road, opened the space on his right, and let the big one see it.

"There," he said. "Straight through. I am not even going to look at you. Take it. All the way in."

The space behind him was empty. He had checked. He had counted the pens, the doorways, the field gate and the twelve feet of open road, and it was empty.

The big one came.

It took the gap. It went past his shoulder with its head coming up for the turn, and Gaara went with it and put everything he had into the pale membrane behind the skull.

The point went in. He twisted, because something in him knew to twist.

It came apart under him into motes of light, rising, going out one by one over the thatch.

He came up on his knees with his mouth already open, because there was always something to say afterwards and he always said it.

Then he heard the hoe.

The man with the hoe was in the twelve feet of open road, where nothing was supposed to be. He held it the way you hold a tool, then adjusted his hands, and the second small one was already inside the length of it.

Gaara got there late. He had been eleven feet away and busy.

Marit got there first, which is why she was between the wall and the thing when it turned, and why the barbs went across her forearm and up into the ribs.

Bern and two others killed it with axes. It took a while and it was not clean.

The man with the hoe was dead before anyone reached him. He had not made much noise about it.

Gaara got Marit down against the wall and her sleeve open. He put the heel of his hand where the blood came from, and leaned. She swore at him steadily, in a low voice.

That was when the panel came, and it was not for him.

He had looked up, and it had opened over the man with the hoe, the same size and colour it had been over a goat.

He read it with his hand still inside Marit's arm.

He had stood beside that man for the length of a fight and had not asked. Bern had given his name unprompted, in case. This one had come out of a door to help a stranger and had not been offered the chance. Now the thing handed it over, four feet from the body, in the voice it used for livestock.

Nothing else came. No level, no line, nothing at all about the one he had put down in the road.

He did the arithmetic while he knelt there, because that was what he had instead of grief. Two had bought a whole level when he was Level 1. He had three in the ground now and it had bought nothing, so the second level had cost more than the first and the third would cost more than the second.

That was a shape he knew. Every ladder he had ever climbed had been built that way, and the men who built them never wrote the numbers on the wall.

"Later," he said, to nothing in particular.

The panel stayed where it always stayed.
