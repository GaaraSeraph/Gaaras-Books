# Chapter 15: Two to Four Hard Uses

They went in an hour before dawn, eight of them and a sledge, and Gaara spent the walk north being appalling on purpose.

"Nobody has done this before except Hallvard," he said, "and he was younger, and I am told he was better looking, and neither of those is going to be relevant in a tunnel."

"You are not funny," said Torv.

"I am extremely funny. You are frightened, which affects the reception."

Sivert laughed, which was the point, and got looked at for it.

At the mouth of the channel they stopped, and Gaara made them stand in the water for two minutes with the lamps shut, until eight sets of eyes had given up on seeing anything and started listening instead.

"Right," he said. "Rules. You do what Hallvard says about the gear and what I say about everything with legs. If I call a number at you, that number is how much trouble the thing is, and you do not need to know how I know."

"How do you know?" said Bern.

"No."

"That's not an answer."

"It is a very short one." He got the shutter open on the first lamp. "Marit. Tell them what happened last time somebody asked me that."

"He said no," said Marit, "and then he was right about everything for two days, and I stopped asking."

"That is the most flattering thing anybody has said about me in this world."

"It was not intended to flatter you."

"I have decided that it was. I have a very poor memory for corrections."

The channel took them, and the talking stopped, because a stone tube does things to the sound of eight people that nobody enjoys.

He read as he walked.

That was the whole of his usefulness down here and he knew it. The looking, and not much else.

The first one came at four hundred paces, and he had it in the air before anyone else heard it.

He went at the six before it had finished deciding about him.

Being fast was still new enough to be interesting. The thing came round on the sweep and he was already outside it, which had never once happened to him in this world. The seam came up under the plate exactly where his hand expected it, because Dagger Mastery put his wrist where it needed to be.

"There you are," he said to it. "You are very big and you are very stupid and I have had that said about me by better people than you."

Ylva killed hers in three strokes with an axe and a great deal of shoulder, and did not make any noise about it. The heavy man, Vidar, lost his spear to the second five and got it back by stepping on the thing's head, which Gaara found genuinely admirable and said so, loudly, in the middle of everything.

Still twelve.

"Marit," he said, while they were dragging the sledge past the bodies. "It only counts mine."

"Is that bad?"

"It is very bad, and it is also the most honest thing that machine has ever done." He was breathing hard. "I stood in that road and told six people to come down here. If it counted what my people killed I would be up a level by the end of the week and I would learn absolutely nothing about my own hands."

"You sound almost pleased."

"I am appalled. I have explained that I can do both."

The seventh came at the last rib before the gate.

It came from behind them, out of a side slot in the stone that Gaara had walked past twice and read as empty. It was on the sledge before anybody had finished turning round.

"Seven," said Gaara, and something in his voice made every one of them move. "Seven. Off the sledge. Vidar, wall. Sivert, up the channel, now."

Sivert did not go up the channel.

Sivert put his bar into the thing's leg joint, which was the bravest and stupidest thing that happened underground that year. It took the bar, and most of the skin off his forearm, and put him into the wall.

Gaara got there.

He got there faster than he had ever got anywhere, because he had spent nine points the day before and this was the first time it had mattered. He came in under the sweep and put the rusted knife into the seam behind the skull with everything he had.

The steel went in about two inches.

Then it broke.

There was no warning. It had said there would be no warning. He had read the words and gone in anyway, because the lord's man was two days east and there was no version of the next four days with a spare morning in it.

The blade came off at the crack and stayed in the thing.

He was left holding a handle with three inches of rust on it, inside the reach of a Level 7 crawler, with a nineteen-year-old bleeding into the water behind him.

"Yes," said Gaara. "Thank you."

Then he found out what a skill is.

He got the hatchet off Marit's belt because it was the nearest edge in the world, and he went back in with it, and it was like fighting with somebody else's hands.

There was no correction. His wrist went where he sent it and stayed there, wrong, and the angle came off the plate instead of into it. He knew where the seam was, because knowing was his and nobody had taken that. He could not make his arm arrive there.

Rank D governed grip, edge alignment and thrust precision with blades under 40 cm.

A hatchet is not a blade under 40 cm.

He hit it four times and opened nothing, and the fifth time it opened him instead, across the ribs, and he went down in the water and came up with his ears ringing.

Ylva killed it.

She came past him with the axe in both hands and put it into the same joint Sivert had gone for. Twice, and a third time when it turned. Then she stood over it and hit it again until it stopped.

"Seven," she said, breathing hard, looking at Gaara. "You said seven."

"I did."

"Is that a lot?"

"You are the highest thing in your village and you are a seven," said Gaara, from the water. "You have just killed something that was your equal, with a wood axe, in the dark, because a nineteen-year-old would not run when he was told."

Ylva stood there for a moment with that.

Then she said, "That is the first time anybody has told me a number about myself," and went to see to Sivert.

They got him strapped and upright. He had lost a lot of skin and would keep the arm. Hallvard, who had not moved from the gear the entire time, looked at it for four seconds and said it would need washing in wine and would be ugly. That was all the medical opinion available.

"I told you to run," said Gaara.

"You did."

"You are nineteen."

"So are you," said Sivert, with his arm strapped and his face the colour of the water. "Everybody keeps saying it about me and nobody says it about you."

Marit, behind them, made a small noise.

"He has a point," she said. "Sixteen is a man here. Takes a man's work and a man's share and a man's beating if he earns one. You have been calling a grown man a boy for two days and he has been letting you."

Gaara sat with that for a moment.

"Sivert," he said. "I have been rude to you in a way I did not notice, which is the only kind I dislike. You went at a seven with an iron bar when I told you not to. You are also better looking than anybody has any business being at the bottom of a drain. And if I say boy to you again, you have my permission to be extremely unpleasant about it."

"Right," said Sivert, and went slightly red, and did not say anything else for a while.

Marit looked at Gaara.

"What," said Gaara.

"Nothing at all," said Marit. "Carry on."

Gaara sat in the water with his back against dressed stone and his ribs opening and closing in a way he did not like, and looked at what was left in his hand.

Three inches of tang. A handle wrapped in nothing.

He had walked out of a dead chamber with that on the first morning. It had killed two crawlers in a nest and opened the nest afterwards. It had cut a strip off a shirt, gone into a seam in a village road while a man died eleven feet away, and been sharpened on an elder's stone by a man who had never sharpened anything.

He had known for two days that it would break. The thing in his head had told him in writing. He had put it back in his belt carefully, the way you put down something that is going to let you down, and then he had brought it here anyway.

"Blacky," he said.

"What?"

"Nothing. An old friend." He turned the handle over. "This one never got a name. I have been calling it the knife for two weeks, like a man who does not expect a thing to last."

Marit crouched down in the water next to him and did not say anything for a while.

"You are bleeding on your good side," she said eventually.

"I have a good side?"

"You had one."

"Ask me again when I have two arms," said Gaara.

"That is my line."

"It is a very good line. I am appropriating it. You may have anything of mine you like, and I want you to think carefully about that offer, because at the moment it is a broken handle and a shirt with a hole in it."

She helped him up.

Up ahead, forty feet on, the gate stood open eighteen inches on two squared blocks, with cold water coming out of the dark underneath it.

Hallvard was already unshipping the windlass.

"Four hours," said the old man, without turning round. "If nothing else comes."

Gaara looked at the mouth of the room beyond the gate, and at Sivert sitting against the wall being strapped, and at six villagers who were still, against all reasonable expectation, standing up.

Then he looked at the empty sheath on his belt.

"Marit," he said. "When this is finished, we are going to find me a smith. I am going to be extremely tedious about it, and you are going to stand there and listen to me talk about steel for an entire afternoon."

"You will take your time about it."

"I will take my time about it," Gaara agreed, "and then I will take some more."
