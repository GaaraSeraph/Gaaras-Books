The byre had four goats in it and smelled accordingly.

Gaara sat on an upturned bucket with his back against the wall and ran the whetstone along the rusted knife the way the skill wanted him to.

That was the strange part. He had never sharpened anything in his life beyond a kitchen knife, badly, twice. But when he set the stone to the steel his wrist found the angle and held it, and when the angle slipped his hand corrected before he noticed. Rank E, the lowest recorded proficiency, and it was already better at this than he was.

So the skill covered more than stabbing. Care, grip, how to carry it.

He turned that over and did not enjoy all of it. Something had improved his hands without being asked. It had put a language in his head the same way. He was working with one instrument, he could not see inside it, and he had nothing to check it against.

The half hour the elder had granted him had produced three crosses in the swept dirt by the door and not much else. A stream mouth. A ford. A pool under a broken channel. All of it from people who had not been there, about a country he had seen once, from a hill, the previous morning.

He was still looking at it when the goats stopped chewing.

All four at once.

Then the smell came under the door. Decay, and something metallic beneath it, like a mouthful of old coins.

He was outside before he had finished deciding to be.

The night was clear and cold and the road was empty. The smell was not. It came up the paved road out of the north, thick and low, moving the way water moves when it has found a channel.

"Get up," he shouted. "Get out of the houses. They're in the road."

The words came out in her language. That part worked.

A door opened four doors down. A man looked at the stranger standing alone in the road at three in the morning, shouting about monsters, on the day he had walked out of the ruin wearing their dead. He shut the door. A bolt went across.

Another door, further up. A woman with a lamp. She saw his face and took the lamp back inside.

Gaara stood in the middle of the road with the correct information in his mouth and nothing to spend it with.

He had spent nineteen years watching right answers die of who was holding them. He had not expected to meet it here.

He ran to Marit's door, and he went past nine nearer ones to get to it.

He had a reason and it was a number nobody else in this village could see. Level 6. The highest thing in Oldstep by two clear points, asleep behind a plank door while a man with a hoe was deciding whether to come out.

He hit the door with the flat of his hand until it opened.

"They're on the road," he said.

She did not ask how he knew. She looked past him into the dark, once, and put her head back inside and made a noise.

It was not a word. It was long and it went up at the end, and it came out of her like something she had been carrying since she was a child. It went the length of that road and turned the corner at the bottom.

Every door in Oldstep opened.

He had shouted the truth and got two bolts. She had made a sound and got forty houses. He put that away to be angry about later.

Then they came into the light of the first lamp, and there were three of them.

The big one first, out of the dark at the north end where the road came in from the trees. Segmented, low, the colour of old bruises. Six legs folding and unfolding in that dry clicking rhythm he had heard in a dead chamber yesterday morning and had hoped to hear again only on his own terms.

Two smaller ones behind it, quick, keeping to the wall line.

Somebody screamed. Somebody else started praying.

"Off the road," Gaara said, and then louder, "get off the road, get behind the pens."

Nobody moved for a second. Then Marit said it, three words, and they moved.

He used the seconds while they moved, because there would not be many more.

The road was the only thing in this village that had been built by people who knew what they were doing. Straight, walled on both sides by stone and thatch, and narrow enough at the top end that the eaves nearly touched.

Bad ground for six legs built to circle. Good ground for a man with one blade and no plan he liked.

He took Marit's woodpile apart with his boot, kicked an armful of the dry stuff into the gap between the houses, and struck the firestone into it. It caught, sulked, and then went up.

The big one stopped.

They did not like it. That was worth knowing, and it was worth exactly one use.

The fire filled the gap at the top of the road, and there was one way south that was not on fire.

He stood in it.

"Bern," said a man at his shoulder. He was about fifty and he had a felling axe and both his hands were shaking. "That's my name. In case."

"Stay on my left. Do not go past me."

"Right."

The first came at his ankles, low and mean, the way the small ones did. He gave it his boot, not his edge, and stepped off the line, and Bern took it across the back with the axe and it went sideways into the wall.

The second came over it. He got the knife up.

Adequate for soft targets. Avoid bone.

So he did not go for the legs, or the head, or anything that would take the edge off a blade he could not replace. He went for the join under the second limb where the plate ended, and the rusted steel went in and came out and he was already moving.

The thing screamed and kept working. He had known it would.

Behind him the road was full of people who had come out of their houses with tools.

That was the moment he built the plan. He built it in about two seconds, and it was a good plan, and it had exactly one body in it.

The big one favoured wide sweeps and lifted its head at the end of them. That was the seam. To get the seam he needed it high and committed, and to get it high and committed he needed it to think it was through.

So he would let it through.

He put himself half a pace off the centre of the road, opened the space on his right, and let the big one see it.

The space behind him was empty. He had checked. He had counted the pens, the doorways, the field gate and the twelve feet of open road, and it was empty.

The big one came.

It took the gap. It went past his shoulder into the empty road with its head coming up for the turn, and Gaara went with it and put everything he had into the pale membrane behind the skull.

The point went in. He twisted, because something in him knew to twist.

It came apart under him into motes of light, rising, going out one by one over the thatch.

He was still on his knees when he heard the hoe.

The man with the hoe was in the twelve feet of open road, where nothing was supposed to be. He had come out to help. He was holding the hoe the way you hold a tool, and then he adjusted his hands, and the second small one was already inside the length of it.

Gaara got there late. He had been eleven feet away and busy.

Marit got there first, which is why she was between the wall and the thing when it turned, and why the barbs went across her forearm and up under the arm into the ribs.

Bern and two others killed it with axes. It took a while and it was not clean.

The man with the hoe was dead before anyone reached him. He had not made much noise about it.

Gaara got Marit down against the wall and got her sleeve open. He put the heel of his hand into the place under her arm where the blood came from, and leaned. She swore at him steadily, in a low voice.

That was when the panel came.
