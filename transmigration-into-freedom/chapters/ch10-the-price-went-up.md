# Chapter 10: The Price Went Up

Eighteen inches is generous for a crawler.

It was not generous for a man.

Gaara went under the gate on his back with the iron a hand's width above his nose and the knife flat on his chest.

"If it comes down on me," he said, "tell my creditors I died solvent."

"You want me to lie."

"I want you to try it once and see how it suits you."

He came out on the far side, stood up in cold water, and stopped talking.

The tube ended six paces on. Beyond it there was air, and a floor going away into the dark, and a vault somewhere overhead that he could not find the top of.

Marit came out behind him. He put a hand down and got her up one-handed, and she was wet to the shoulder and swearing quietly and he decided not to help with the rest.

"Sorry about the view on the way through," he said. "I was flat on my back and you went second, and I want it on the record that I kept my eyes on the ironwork."

"You are a liar."

"I am not, and you know exactly how much that costs me."

Then the light caught the wall behind her, and neither of them said anything for a while.

There was a stain running round the whole room, level, twenty feet up.

"That's a water line," Marit said.

"That is a water line."

He turned and looked back at the gate he had just crawled under, and at the wear mark cut into the frame by eight hundred years of sitting shut.

"I was wrong," he said. "You do not gate a drain to keep water in. You do it to keep a room full."

The nests started forty paces out.

Mounds of chitin and silt and bone, built up off the floor, going away into the dark in every direction like a town.

All of them empty.

"How many."

"Do not."

"How many."

"Hundreds." He did not look at her. "They were down here the whole time, under the water. Then somebody let the water out, and they walked up the only hole in the room and down the channel and into your fields."

Marit stood with her hatchet in her left hand and looked at the town of empty nests.

"Four people," she said. "Out of all this."

"Four people out of all this is the good news, and I would like you to sit with how bad that makes the rest of it."

They found the line of dead ones a hundred paces further in, and Gaara read them as he walked, because he had learned that trick on the road that morning and had not stopped using it since.

The line stopped.

The groove ran away in both directions and did not curve enough to tell him anything from where he stood.

She went back and looked along it.

"It's a circle," she said. "It's a circle and you're standing on it."

Gaara looked down at the line under his boots.

"It is not the thing." He crouched with his hand flat on the groove. "It has never been the thing. Everything inside this line reads the same, and it reads that whether it is a monster or an empty nest or a handful of dirt. This was never a fence, Marit. It is a blind spot, and somebody cut it into the floor on purpose."

She came and stood at the edge of it and did not cross.

"Something in here doesn't want to be looked at."

"Something in here has arranged not to be." He stood up. "And whoever built this knew exactly how to arrange it, which means the thing in my head is old enough for somebody to have written a rule against it eight hundred years ago."

He did not say the other half.

The other half was an old man with nothing at all over his shoulders. It was still a shape and not yet a thought, and he knew what happens to a man who says a shape out loud too early.

They came out of the dark on the inside of the line, and they came without hurrying.

Three of them. Then five. Then he stopped counting and started moving.

"Behind me. Wall at your back, hatchet up, and do not step over that groove."

"You are standing over it."

"Yes," said Gaara. "Somebody has to be interesting."

The first came at him across open floor, which was the last mistake it ever made. He gave it the boot, took it on the turn, and put the rusted steel into the seam behind the skull.

Something went through him from the sternum outward, and it was warm, and it arrived while there were still two of them on the floor in front of him.

"Not now," he told it.

It did not go away. It never went away.

He killed the seventh with his hands shaking from something that was not fear.

The eighth ran. The ninth ran after it, and then the hall was quiet except for water.

Gaara sat down where he was standing.

"Two," he said. "Then four. Not doubling the total. Doubling the step."

Marit lowered the hatchet.

"Is that good?"

"It is arithmetic, which is better than good, it is useful." He looked up at her and there was blood on his teeth from somewhere. "Two for the first. Four for the second. Eight for the third, if the shape holds. Fourteen kills to be a four, thirty to be a five." He laughed once, and it was ugly. "Your village runs to sixes, Marit. Do you understand what it took the people in your fields to be sixes?"

"Years."

"Lifetimes. And the things in there are sixes already."

He wiped his mouth and did not get up yet.

Four points sat unspent in the corner of his eye and he refused to look at them.

Any answer that arrived this fast, with his hands still shaking, was an answer he had not checked. He would spend them cold or he would not spend them.

"Later," he told the panel.

Then he looked at his own forearm, where the second one had opened him from wrist to elbow, and made an executive decision about the order of business.

"Marit."

"Mm."

"I am going to need your help with my trousers."

The hatchet stopped moving.

"For the wound," he said. "There is a strip of shirt in the pouch, and I have one hand that works and it is holding the other one shut. Whatever you were thinking is available later and in writing."

"That," said Marit, "is a lot of words for a man with a hole in him."

She came over and knelt and cut the strip and tied it, and she did it fast and tight and with no particular tenderness, and he swore at her steadily throughout, and she let him.

"There," she said. "Now, about the trousers."

Gaara stopped talking.

He would think about it a great deal later, in the dark, on a byre floor. In the moment all he managed was to look at her.

Marit tied off the knot and stood up and picked her hatchet back out of the silt.

"Two days," she said. "Two days you have been running your mouth at me, over a ruin and a dead man and my own road, and it turns out all I ever had to do was say one thing back."

"That is unfair."

"It is extremely fair."

"It is accurate," he said, "which is worse, and I would like the record to show that you waited until I was bleeding."

"There is no record."

"There is now. It has a number in it and everything."

She almost smiled. Then she looked past him at the groove in the floor, and whatever was on her face went off it.

Gaara turned round.

The unopened nests started twenty feet inside the line, and they went back into the dark, and there were hundreds of them, and every one of them read the same.

Somewhere a long way in, out past the reach of any light either of them was carrying, water moved. Not much. The amount a thing displaces when it changes position without standing up.

Gaara got to his feet.

"Right," he said. "We have four points, six kills and one hatchet between us, and I am not spending any of it in a room that will not let me read what is in it."

"So we go."

"We go, and we come back with light, gear and somebody who can tell me what that line in the floor is." He was already walking, and his forearm had started to throb in a way that promised a long night. "And on the way home you are going to tell me every man in Oldstep who owns a stick."
