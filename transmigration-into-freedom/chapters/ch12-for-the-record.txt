They came up out of the channel two fields north of Oldstep, into afternoon light that neither of them was ready for.

Gaara sat down on the bank and did the thing he had been refusing to do for six hours.

Nine points, cold, with his hands steady.

Reaction and recovery increased.

It came up through him like cold water going the wrong way, and for a second the field was very loud.

Then he stood up, and the standing happened faster than he had asked for, and he had to put a hand out.

"What did you do?"

"I bought speed." He flexed his hand and watched it arrive where he sent it. "All of it, in one place. I have spent my whole life spreading effort across nine things and being adequate at all of them, and it made me a man with a very good CV and no company."

"I understood one word of that."

"I am going to be quicker," said Gaara. "You will find it insufferable."

"I already find you insufferable."

"Yes, but at this speed."

They walked. She was slower than him now by a distance that was starting to be visible, one arm strapped, and he made a point of not outpacing her and she made a point of not mentioning it.

"Marit."

"Mm."

"For the record."

"There is no record."

"There is now, and I am about to put something in it." He stopped walking, and she stopped, and he made sure she was looking at him. "When we get in there, I am going to say that phrase again. Those three words, in a room, in front of the elder. If you hear them, it means I have decided the next thirty seconds might go badly, and I want your hand on that hatchet before anything happens rather than after."

Marit looked at him for a moment.

"You think he'll come at us."

"I think I do not know what he is, and I have been wrong about numbers three times today." He started walking again. "I would rather agree on a word out here than improvise one in there."

"And if I say it first?"

Gaara considered that.

"Then I will assume you have seen something I have not," he said, "and I will move without asking why. That is the whole point of a word."

"Right." She shifted the hatchet to a better grip. "For the record."

"There is no record."

"There is now," Marit said.

Oldstep at four in the afternoon had a new smell in it, which was earth, because there was a raw stripe of it at the far end where Osvald had gone in that morning.

They came down the paved road, and the doors opened, and the same faces came out and stood in the same places they had stood two nights ago, holding nothing, looking at the two of them.

Somebody said something to somebody else behind a hand.

Gaara stopped in the middle of the road.

"Good afternoon," he said, in a voice that carried. "You are all out of your houses. Excellent. That is one improvement in two days and I want it recognised."

"Gaara."

"No, this is the useful part." He turned as he spoke, so that it went to all of them. "The things that took Osvald did not come down this road. They came under it. There is a stone channel forty feet below where you are standing and it runs the length of your village, and it comes out of a room north of here with about four hundred empty nests in it."

Nobody said anything at all.

"Four hundred," said Gaara. "You have lost four people and two dogs. I have been standing in the place that could have sent four hundred. The only reason it did not is a piece of ironwork that a stranger will now have to go and shut, because on the night it mattered the people who live here held a meeting in their doorways."

The heavy man with the boar spear came a step out of his gate.

"You have got a mouth on you."

"I have got a mouth on me and a map of your cellar," Gaara said. "Bring the mouth up again in a week and see which of the two of us the crawlers cared about."

"That'll do," Marit said.

It did, mostly, because it was her.

Solva was at her door with flour on her forearms as though the last two days had not happened, and she looked at Gaara's shoulder and at the strip of shirt on his forearm and did not ask.

"You are both alive," she said.

"Provisionally."

"Come and eat."

"In an hour." He was already looking up the road, at the last house, at the door that had shut on the night the road split. "There is a conversation I have to have first, and I would like it to happen while everybody is still outside and looking at me."

Solva followed where he was looking and went very quiet.

"Oh," she said.

"Yes," said Gaara. "That is roughly where I am too."

He had put it together somewhere under the fields, and the thing that had done it was not the stick, and it was not the rope, and it was not the empty air over an old man's shoulders.

It was a whetstone.

He had been carrying it in Teodor's pouch for two days. Marit had put it on the rail of the byre and said, in the same breath she said everything, that it was Hallvard's and he would want it back.

A seventy-year-old man who has been the final word in a farming village for thirty-one years, who owns a stone good enough that a stranger going into a ruin is lent it and expected to return it.

Nobody sharpens like that for hay.

The elder's door was open, because it was afternoon. Hallvard sat inside it on a stool with his hands on his knees and his stick leaning against the wall beside him, and he did not look surprised to see either of them.

"You came back," he said.

"Twice, now. It is becoming a habit." Gaara stopped in the doorway. "May we come in and shut this?"

"You may come in." Hallvard did not move. "Shut it if you want to."

Gaara went in. Marit came in behind him and stood where she could see the door and the window at the same time, which he noticed and did not comment on.

He put the whetstone on the table.

"Yours," he said. "I am told you want it back."

Hallvard looked at it.

He did not pick it up.

"Say the rest," he said.

"There is a channel under this village," said Gaara. "There is a room at the top of it with four hundred nests in it and a water line twenty feet up the wall. There is an iron gate on the outflow that was shut for eight hundred years and is now propped open eighteen inches on two squared blocks that somebody carried in. And between the gate and the middle of that room there is a line of dead crawlers going inwards. Sixty or seventy of them, each opened at the seam behind the skull, and not one of them touched afterwards."

The old man's hands stayed on his knees.

"Nobody in this village knows any of that," Gaara said. "I have asked. I asked five of them in your byre with your bread in my hand. I have asked Marit, who has worked the edge of that ruin for eleven years. None of them has ever heard a word of it."

"No," said Hallvard. "They haven't."

"For the record," said Gaara.

Marit's hand went onto the hatchet, unhurried, the way you shift your weight.

And Hallvard's eyes went to her hand, and then to Gaara, and something in his face changed that was not fear and was not anger. He looked, for about a second, extremely tired and extremely amused.

"That is a good arrangement," he said. "Did you settle the word before you came in, or in the road?"

"In the field."

"Better." He nodded slowly. "Sit down, both of you. You will not need it, and I would not tell you to put it away, because I would not put mine away either."

Nobody sat down.

"How long," said Gaara.

"How long what."

"How long have you been going down there and killing them."

The room was quiet enough to hear the road outside, where forty houses were pretending to do something else.

"Thirty-one years," said Hallvard.

Marit made a small sound.

"That is not the answer I was braced for," Gaara said. "I was braced for a season. Possibly two."

"I know what you were braced for."

"Thirty-one years is the whole of your time as elder."

"It is the reason I am elder." Hallvard picked the whetstone up off the table at last and turned it over in his fingers. "Aldis Halden went down there before me, and her uncle before her, and I am told a woman called Ingrith before him, and none of them wrote any of it down, and that was on purpose too."

"Why."

"Because a village that knows sends to the lord," said Hallvard. "And a lord who is told sends men, and men who come look at a hole in the ground with a gate on it and decide what is behind it is worth having." He set the stone down. "It has been shut for eight hundred years, boy. The only thing that has ever opened it is somebody deciding they had a reason."

Gaara stood very still.

"And in spring," he said, "somebody decided they had a reason."

Hallvard did not answer that.

"You raised it," said Gaara.

"Yes."

Marit said his name once, and it came out wrong.

"Eighteen inches," said Hallvard, to the floor. "I worked it out on a slate over two winters. Eighteen inches would let it down slow enough that I would have the shallows by high summer and could reach the middle at last. Nothing in there would notice the water going for a season. Nothing in there has noticed anything in eight hundred years." He put his hands back on his knees. "I was right about the rate. I was wrong about the noticing."

"Four people."

"Four people and two dogs and a man with a hoe." The old man looked up, and his eyes were perfectly dry. "I have counted them. I count them a great deal. I am counting them now while you stand in my house."

"And you said nothing."

"What would you have had me say?" It was the first time either of them had heard him raise his voice, and it was not much, and it was worse for that. "Stand up in my own road and tell forty houses that the thing eating their children came out because the elder opened a door? They would have sent for the lord that afternoon. And then men would have come, and gone down there, and gone through the gate, and I would have swapped four for four hundred."

"So you went down alone," said Gaara, "and told nobody, and took nothing out, and buried your fires so that nobody would know you had sat down."

"Yes."

"And it worked, for thirty-one years, right up until the day it did not, and then it cost other people instead of you."

"Yes," said Hallvard.

Gaara looked at him for a long moment.

Then he pulled the stool out from under the table with his foot, and sat down on it, and put his elbows on his knees.

"I want you to understand," he said, "that I am not sitting down because I forgive you. I am sitting down because I have done exactly what you did, on a smaller scale, in an office, with other people's money. I know precisely how a man gets from a slate in a winter to four graves without ever once making a decision he thought was wrong."

Hallvard said nothing.

"So," said Gaara. "Where is the gear."
