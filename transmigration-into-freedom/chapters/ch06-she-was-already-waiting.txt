The byre had four goats in it and smelled accordingly.

Gaara sat on an upturned bucket with his back against the wall, the rusted knife across his knees, and worked the whetstone along it the way the skill wanted him to.

That was the strange part. He had never sharpened anything in his life beyond a kitchen knife, badly, twice. But when he set the stone to the steel his wrist found an angle and held it, and when the angle slipped his hand corrected before he noticed it had gone wrong. Rank E, the lowest recorded proficiency, and it was already better at this than he was.

Which meant the skill was not only for stabbing. It covered the whole relationship with the blade. Care, grip, how to carry it.

He filed that. It suggested things about the other ranks that he did not have enough information to enjoy yet.

Rust came off in a grey slurry. Underneath, the steel was better than he had any right to expect.

"You'll want to oil that," said the man in the doorway. "Goat fat does. Ask Solva."

His name was Bern, he was about fifty, and he had lost a dog.

"Two of them," he corrected. "Rell and the little one. Down by the stream, first week after the thaw. Found the collars. Nothing else."

"Where on the stream?"

"Where it comes out from under the stones."

Gaara looked up. "Under what stones?"

Bern gestured vaguely north, and left, and that was all he had, so Gaara thanked him and asked him to send in the next one.

They came one at a time over the next hour, which was longer than the half hour he had negotiated, and nobody mentioned that.

Four of them. Five, counting the old man who came to stare.

Out of forty houses, five. He noted that too. It was not that the rest had nothing to say; it was that coming to the byre meant being seen walking to the byre, and the road had split that afternoon and everyone in Oldstep knew exactly which side of it their neighbours had gone home on.

A woman named Halle, whose cousin had the cart. Taken at the ford, in daylight, with the ox screaming loud enough to be heard in the village. They found the cart. They did not find her cousin.

A boy of about sixteen, who had been fishing with the boy who was taken and had run and had been living with that ever since. He talked to his own hands the entire time. The pool below the old channel, he said. Where the water comes down the broken stone.

The last was an old man who had nothing useful and wanted to look at the stranger.

By the time the light was going, Gaara had a stick in his hand and a patch of swept dirt in front of him, and he was drawing.

The village. The road. The ruin, north. The stream. The ford. The pool. The place where the water came out from under the stones.

He drew the aqueduct as two dashes with a gap between them, and he put a cross where each person had been taken, and then he sat back on his heels and looked at what he had made, and the back of his neck went cold.

They were all on one line.

Not scattered around the village. Not spread along the tree line where you would expect an animal to hunt. Every single one of them sat within a hundred paces of the water that came out of the ruin, and the line of it ran straight back north, under the broken aqueduct, and into the city.

"Nobody's put these together," he said.

Marit was leaning against the doorpost. She had been there some time.

"Put what together?"

"This." He tapped the crosses with the stick. "Everyone here knows one of these. You know the wood and the tree line. Bern knows his dogs. Halle knows the ford. The boy knows the pool. Every one of them has a piece and nobody has ever laid them next to each other."

"We know they're by the water," she said. "Everyone knows that."

"Yes. But you say it like it's the weather." He stood up, and his knee complained, and he ignored it. "It isn't weather. It's a direction. They're not spreading out from the ruin in a circle, they're running down one channel and stopping where the channel stops. That's not hunting behaviour. That's a road."

Marit came over and looked down at the dirt for a long moment.

"You did that in an hour," she said.

"I did it in an hour because I've never met any of these people. You've known them your whole lives. Nobody sits down with all of it at once because nobody can stand to. Halle's cousin isn't a cross on the ground to her." He shrugged. "It's the one thing I'm good at. It's not a nice thing to be good at."

"What does it mean?"

"It means when I go in tomorrow, I don't wander. I follow the water backward until I find where it comes from, and whatever changed in spring, it changed there."

She was quiet.

"Teodor's isn't on your map," she said.

"No. He went in on purpose. He's the only one who wasn't taken."

He had not said it gently, and he watched her take it, and she took it standing up.

At the far end of the byre, in the shadow past the goats, Hallvard was standing with his stick, and Gaara had no idea how long he had been there either. The old man looked at the drawing in the dirt for a while.

Then he turned and went out without saying anything at all, and Gaara found that more interesting than anything he could have said.

Solva came at full dark with the bread.

Three flat loaves wrapped in cloth, a lump of hard cheese, and a small clay pot with a stopper, which turned out to be fat.

"For the steel," she said. "Bern said."

"Thank you."

She stood there a moment in the doorway with her hands empty, and he could see her deciding whether to say something, and then deciding not to, and he let her not say it.

"First light," she said instead. "There's mist in the low ground until the sun's over the trees. Don't set out in it. You'll walk past the ford and not know."

"Understood."

She went. He ate half of one loaf and made himself stop.

Then he sat in the dark with four goats and did the arithmetic on his own life for a while, which was a thing he had promised himself he had given up.

The panel hung where it always hung.

Level 2. HP 165. CON 8. A skill that could sharpen a knife better than he could.

Two days ago he had been thirty-eight years old, in debt, sleeping on a mat behind his own desk. Now he was nineteen, fed, armed with a rusty knife, and about to walk into a ruin that had eaten four people, on behalf of a village that had voted roughly half and half on whether he was the cause.

He thought about that until he found the thing that was actually bothering him, which was not the ruin.

It was the empty air over Hallvard's shoulders.

He had looked at maybe twenty people today and every one of them had come up with a name and a number. Bern was a 4. Solva was a 3. The boy who had run was a 2 and Gaara had made a point of not looking at him twice.

He turned his head and looked at the nearest goat.

"Of course you are," he said.

The goat continued chewing.

So it was not restricted to people. It read whatever was in front of him and it worked on livestock, and it had failed on exactly one thing today, and that thing was the man who ran this village.

There were three explanations he could think of and he did not like the taste of any of them. That Hallvard was something the system could not read. That Hallvard was something the system had been told not to show him. Or that the panel was not showing him what was there, but what somebody wanted him to see.

He lay back in the straw with his hand on the knife and stared up at the black roof.

"You can put a language in my head," he said quietly, to nothing. "So what else have you put in there."

The panel did not answer. It never answered. It only reported.

He slept badly and woke in the grey before dawn with a goat standing on his ankle.

Mist in the low ground, exactly as promised, lying in the hollows in long white bands. Cold that got into the shoulders. He rolled the food into the cloth, tied it to Teodor's belt, checked the knife, checked the boots, and walked out through the village while it was still asleep.

Marit was standing at the north gate.

Hatchet at her hip. A pack on her back. A coil of rope over one shoulder and a second, shorter axe pushed through a loop at the side, and she was chewing something, and she looked at him with no expression whatsoever.

Gaara stopped.

"No," he said.

"Morning."

"Marit."

"It's my road too."

"It's a ruin with a hole in the bottom of it and nothing in there is going to care that you can cut wood."

"You've been in it once," she said. "For about six hours. I've worked the edge of it for eleven years. I know where the ground gives. I know where the wall is thin enough to get out through if something's behind you." She shifted the rope on her shoulder. "You know where two dead crawlers are."

"I work alone."

"Yes," she said. "I heard. You told Hallvard, you told Solva, and last night you told the goats." She started walking. "You'll want to keep up, the mist lifts in an hour."

He stood there for a second with a whole speech in his mouth.

It was a good speech. He had been giving versions of it for fifteen years, to co-founders, to investors, to a woman who had eventually stopped asking. Help is unreliable. Other people's failures become your failures. Carry it yourself and nobody can put it down.

And every version of it had been true, and every version of it had ended with him alone on a mat behind a desk at four in the morning owing money to people he had never met.

The thought arrived sideways and unwelcome, and he decided not to look at it directly.

"If it goes wrong in there," he said, catching up, "I'm not going to be able to get both of us out."

"No," she agreed. "You're not."

"That doesn't concern you?"

"It concerns me a great deal." She stepped over a rut in the road without looking down at it. "I'm coming anyway. Teodor's in there."

They walked north into the mist, and the ruin came up out of it slowly, one broken wall at a time.
