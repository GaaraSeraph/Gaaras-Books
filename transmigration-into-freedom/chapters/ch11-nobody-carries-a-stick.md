# Chapter 11: Nobody Carries a Stick

They got about ninety paces towards the gate before the room objected.

"How is the arm?" Gaara said.

"Attached."

"That is the answer I give people, and I want you to know it is deeply unsatisfying from the other side."

He was walking with the knife low and his own forearm strapped in a strip of his own shirt, and he had stopped being able to tell which of the aches were new.

"Also," he said, "and I raise this purely as a matter of household management. If you say one more thing like the thing you said about the trousers, while I am carrying an open wound and a rusty knife, I will fall over. And you will have to explain to your village how their stranger died."

"Noted."

"That is some way short of agreeing."

"No," Marit said. "It isn't."

Then the dark ahead of them moved, and he stopped enjoying himself.

They came out of the nest field on the inside of the line, so he could not read one of them until it crossed the groove. He was fighting blind, and he knew exactly how blind.

"Back to back," he said. "Do not step over that line. If I go over it, do not come and get me."

"You are giving orders again."

"I am giving one order, and it is the same one, and if I have to give it a third time I would like it to be in writing."

The first came over the groove and turned into a number as it did.

Gloom Crawler
Level 5

"You are a five," Gaara said. "That is unfortunate for one of us."

He took it at the seam and it took him four seconds and a strip off his shoulder.

Kills: 8

The second came in behind it and he did not have time to read it. He went low, gave it the boot, let it turn, and put the steel in behind the skull while it was still deciding.

Kills: 9

"Left," Marit said.

"Yours."

She had it, one-armed, and the hatchet went into the joint and stuck, and the thing dragged her three feet before Gaara got there and finished it.

"That was not clean," he said.

"It was not."

"No, I am admiring it. I was raised on people who did clean work and produced nothing."

Kills: 10

Then the big one came, and it came over the line at a speed that made everything before it look like a rehearsal.

He read it in the half second while it crossed.

Gloom Crawler
Level 6

"Marit. Wall."

"What are you doing?"

"Being interesting."

It was a foot longer than the one that had come down her road, and the barbed limbs went further, and he lost the first exchange entirely and only kept his face because he went over backwards into silt.

The second exchange he lost as well.

The third he won. A Level 6 crawler in a drained cistern still lifts its head at the end of a wide sweep, because that is what they do, and a man who has been told that once never needs telling twice.

He went up under the plate with everything he had left in his legs.

Kills: 11

The panel went white at the edges, and he had time to think that this was wrong, before the rest of it arrived.

Nine. Four from the last one, which he had not touched, and five from this.

He sat down in the silt beside the thing he had killed and looked at that for a while.

"That is wrong," he said.

"You are bleeding out of your shoulder."

"Yes, and it is also wrong." He put the back of his wrist against his eyes. "Fourteen. I said fourteen. I stood in this room four hours ago and told you fourteen kills for a four, and I had the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was clean."

Marit sat down next to him, which she had not done before.

"And?"

"Eleven." He laughed, and it hurt. "I was wrong three times in one day about the same number, which is a record even for me."

She waited. She had got good at waiting.

"Two," Gaara said. "Then four. I had two points and I drew a line through them, which is what you do when you are tired and want to feel clever. Two points make any line you like." He turned his hand over. "The count was never the price. Look at what I killed."

"Crawlers."

"A five. A five. Something I did not read. A five. And a six, just now, when I was a three." He was talking faster. "In your road I killed a small one when I was a two and it was worth almost nothing. Down here I killed a six and it was worth most of a level on its own. It is not counting corpses, Marit. It is weighing them."

Marit thought about that with her hatchet across her knees.

"So killing something bigger than you is worth more."

"Killing something bigger than me is worth an enormous amount, and that is the ugliest sentence I have said in this world." He got up, badly. "Because it means the fast way is the way that gets you killed, and the reason nobody in your village is a ten is not that they are lazy. It is that everybody who tried is dead."

"Hallvard's a seventy-year-old man who's never left the valley."

"Hallvard does not read at all," said Gaara, "and we are not doing that one yet."

They went the last of it fast, because two more came and neither of them wanted the arithmetic.

At the tube mouth he stopped and looked back once. At the groove in the floor, and the town of unopened nests beyond it, and the dark past that, where the water had moved earlier without anything standing up out of it.

Then he pulled the panel forward, because there had been something else in the corner of it since the crawler went down, and he had been ignoring it the way you ignore a knock at a bad time.

"Oh," said Gaara. "Now you tell me."

He looked at the rusted knife in his hand, the way he had looked at it on the first morning, and the window opened wider than it ever had.

Field knife, human manufacture. Heavily corroded.
Edge geometry intact. Spine cracked from tang to fuller, propagating.
Remaining structural integrity: two to four hard uses.
This blade will fail without warning.

He stood in a foot of cold water and read it twice.

"What," Marit said.

"It says my knife is going to break."

"When?"

"It says two to four hard uses, which is a very polite way of saying the next one." He turned it over. The crack ran up out of the tang and into the fuller, and now that a thing in his head had told him where to look he could see it, and he could not stop seeing it. "It knew this the whole time. It has been telling me adequate for soft targets since the first morning and it never once mentioned the crack, and today it got a better window and now it will."

"Is that the system being cruel?"

"That is the system being a system," he said. "It shows you what you have earned the right to see. Which means everything I have ever been told by that thing was the small print of what it actually knows, and I have been making decisions on the summary."

He put the knife back in his belt, carefully, the way you put down something that is going to let you down.

They went under the gate one at a time. Marit went first, and Gaara made a point of studying the ironwork with great professional interest, and told her so. She told him what he could do with the ironwork. It was the most cheerful either of them had been in eight hours.

Out in the channel, in the moving water, with daylight somewhere a long way south of them, he asked it.

"Marit. Who in Oldstep carries a stick?"

She stopped walking.

He had expected her to think about it. He had expected a list, four or five old men, somebody's uncle, and an afternoon of ruling them out.

She did not think about it at all.

"Nobody carries a stick," Marit said.

"Somebody does. I have his footprints."

"You are not listening." She turned round in the water to face him, and her voice was flat in a way he had not heard from her before, not in the road with a hatchet up, not over Teodor's boots. "Old men lean on them. That is what a stick is. My grandfather had one and he put his weight on it and you could hear him coming across the yard."

Gaara said nothing.

"The mark in that silt was not deep," she said. "You told me that yourself. You knelt down and you told me it never took any weight at all."

"Yes."

"So it wasn't a stick." She started walking again, south, down the channel, towards the village and the road and the houses. "It was a man walking about carrying one."

He followed her.

Behind them the water came out of the dark under an eight-hundred-year-old gate that somebody had propped open with two squared blocks, and it ran the same way they were going, and it got there first.
