# Chapter 17: Who Gets the Kill

He had said he would leave in the morning. In the morning he stood in the road with the pack on and did not go.

"You said," said Solva.

"I did say."

"You said it to my face."

"I did, and it was true when I said it, and then I slept for six hours, which is the most dangerous thing I do." He looked east down the road, at nothing. "Four days was the walking. Two there and two back. It was the only honest number I had at midnight and I handed it over as though it meant something."

"It does not?"

"It is the floor. Nothing has ever happened at the floor." He got the pack off his shoulder and set it on the wall. "Rulf has gone to tell a lord's man that a stranger opened a gate. A lord's man does not keep a horse saddled for that. He keeps a ledger, and a superior, and an enormous appetite for not being blamed. He will write it down. Then he will wait to see whether it goes away."

"How long," said Marit.

"Two weeks. Possibly three. And I am giving you that as a guess and not as a fact, because I have been wrong twice this week and both times somebody else paid the bill."

"Then go," said Solva. "You have two weeks."

"That is the problem with two weeks," said Gaara. "Four days I could have run from. Two weeks I have to do something with."

Hallvard was at the top of the road with his stick, and had been for a while, and said nothing.

"You," said Gaara. "For thirty-one years there has been exactly one person in this valley who can walk into that ruin and walk back out. The gate does not change that. It moved it, from under the ground to on top of it, and it left you standing in it, and you are seventy, and I have met your knees."

"My knees are my business."

"Your knees are the whole valley's business and that is the entire point I am making."

He picked the pack back up and put it inside Solva's door, which was the most committal thing he did all week.

"Get Ylva," he said. "Get Torv, Bern, Ask and Vidar. Not Sivert, and I want somebody to tell him that in a kind voice, because if he hears it from me he will do something with one arm that I will have to write a letter about."

He had checked the number on the walk up, because he had been checking it since he woke.

All of it, back overnight, without him doing anything at all. Whatever the drum had taken it had taken as rent, exactly as he had told six people in the water that it would, and he had been guessing when he said it and had let them think otherwise.

Nobody had ever been past the tree line.

That was the thing he had not understood until they were standing in it. Every soul in Oldstep had been to the edge, and Hallvard had gone into a hole, and Gaara had come out of a chamber and walked down a channel, and none of that was the city.

The city was streets.

They came up a paved avenue wide enough for four carts, with a root the thickness of a horse coming through it at an angle. Doorways stood open on both sides at the height of a first floor, because the ground floors were gone under forty feet of leaf. The aqueduct crossed the sky ahead of them on its legs with the bite out of it. Torv stopped walking and had to be spoken to twice.

"Right," said Gaara. "Nobody touches anything, nobody goes through a doorway, and nobody dies, and if you can only manage two of those, pick the last one."

Bern went into the wall doing exactly what he had been taught.

That was the part Gaara could not put down afterwards. There was no version of it where anybody had been careless. He had told them to take a thing at a wall so that it could only come from one side. It was good advice and he had used it himself twice. Bern set his back against eight hundred years of dressed stone and did it correctly.

The stone had been holding a lintel up with a root through it.

It came down on his leg from the knee, and the noise it made was not loud.

They got it off him in ninety seconds, which was fast, and it did not help much. The leg had opened along the shin and the bone was doing something a bone should not do. Bern did not shout. He said Ask's name twice and then apologised to Vidar for something none of them ever worked out.

"Door," said Gaara. "Off that frame. Two of you, and Ylva at his head, and nobody carries him by the leg."

They were four minutes into getting him onto it when the roof came down on the other side of the street.

"That is not a thing you fight. Ylva, the door. Get him down the avenue. Vidar, walk backwards and do not turn round. Marit."

"Aye."

"You are with me and you are on my left and you do not take it. You get its head round."

She got its head round.

She did it with the axe and a great deal of noise, and one appalling half second where it came for her and she stood exactly still. That is the hardest thing a person can be taught to do, and nobody had taught her.

Gaara went in under it with eighteen inches of wedge iron.

He had no blade and no skill that applied and he knew precisely where the seam was, and he put the iron into it four times, and the fourth one went through into something that mattered. It came down on his shoulder on the way and put him into the road, and he got up because Marit was still standing there.

He stood in the avenue of a dead city with his shoulder going numb and read that twice.

One kill. A nine, taken at four, and it had been worth more than the eleven before it put together. The rule he had worked out in a tunnel held, and it held generously, and there was a version of the next month that consisted of nothing but finding things far too large for him and being extremely careful.

Forty feet down the road, four people were carrying Bern on a door.

"Say it," said Marit, when they were on the hill.

"I was going to. You have taken all the pleasure out of it."

"Say it anyway."

"Bern is on a door because I told him to take it at a wall." He was walking on the downhill side, watching the ground. "Then a thing came off a roof and I killed it, and I was paid for killing it, more than I have been paid for anything since I arrived in this world. Both of those are true and they happened forty feet apart, and there is a way of saying them in one sentence that makes me a man who bought a level with a leg."

"Nobody thinks that."

"I think it. I have been doing arithmetic on people since I was twenty-two, and I am not going to stop today on the grounds that the answer is unpleasant."

"Is it the true one?"

"No," said Gaara. "It is only the neatest."
