The wall of the Wold had been started and not finished, and Gaara stopped in the road to look at it for long enough that Ylva went on twenty paces without him.

Good coursed stone for about ninety feet. Then a butt end, squared off properly rather than broken, with the tooling still crisp on it. Then a ditch and a bank and a hedge, going round the rest of the town the way a rope goes round a bale.

"Somebody laid out a wall for a place four times this size," he said, when he caught up. "Then they stopped, on an ordinary morning, in the middle of a good week. The last stone they cut is finished on the face nobody will ever see."

"It has been like that my whole life," said Ylva.

"That is what I said."

There were more people in the first street than lived in Oldstep, and he read every one of them, because he had never once been able to stop.

Sivert had stopped again and was looking at a woman in a doorway with her sleeves rolled.

"Yes," said Gaara.

"I did not say anything."

"You did not have to. You have the face of a man who has just found out there are more people in the world." He did not slow down. "I had that face as well. I was thirty-eight and in a far worse position to do anything about it."

"I am carrying your pack."

"You are carrying Marit's pack, which is a different and sadder sentence."

A woman with two buckets was a 4. A boy under a cart was a 2. The man shouting at the boy was a 5, and the two carters behind him were a 3 and a 6. None of it meant anything at all. Oldstep had been a village of exactly those numbers, and he had spent a week believing that was the world.

Then a man came out of a doorway with a bowl in his hand and leaned on the wall to eat, and did not look at anybody, and was bored.

"I have just been made aware," said Gaara, to nobody, "that I have spent a week being the cleverest man in a shed."

"Ylva. In your whole life, what is the highest thing you have seen."

"I want you to hold on to that." He was watching a man weigh out nails four doors down, a 3, entirely ordinary. "Because I am about to tell you where you sit in this town, and the answer is nowhere very high, and I would rather you had it from me on a street than from somebody in a hall."

She took it the way she took numbers.

"How bad."

"You are an eight. There is a man on that barrel who does nothing all day but be sixteen. Nobody in that valley has ever seen a soldier, and I did not know that was a thing you could not have seen."

Sivert, who had not spoken since the ford, said, "What am I."

"You are a two, and you were a two when you put an iron bar into a Level 7's leg joint, and I have thought about that on and off for a week." Gaara did not slow down. "So I would not put very much on the number if I were you."

They found the hall at midday and he made himself walk past it.

That was the hardest thing he did all day and he knew exactly why he was doing it. A man who goes into an institution cold, and asks it how it works, has told everybody in the room what he is. He had watched people do that for nineteen years, and he had hired some of them, and he had never once respected one.

So instead he asked what a bed cost.

A coin bought three nights in a common room, on straw, with nothing to eat. A coin bought one night behind a door that bolted. A hot meal was half of one.

He stood in the street and did the sum, and did not like it, and did it again in case liking it had been the problem.

Eleven coins. A month of sleeping badly. Ten days of sleeping properly. And a blade at Ordulf's was four goats, which was forty-four coins, which was four times everything he owned and had been for six days.

"Straw," he said. "All three of us. And we eat once a day and we eat the cheap thing, and I want to be extremely clear that this is not me being hard, it is me being able to count."

"I have slept on straw since I was born," said Ylva.

"Then you will be better at it than me and I will complain more, and I would like that entered somewhere as the agreed division of labour."

The woman behind the counter had a ledger stick and a wrist full of keys and had looked at exactly one of them, which was him.

"Three of you," she said.

"Three of us, and I would like to say before anything else that you have not once looked at what we are carrying, which means you priced us off our boots in about a second." Gaara put his elbows on the counter. "That is a considerable skill and nobody has ever told you so, because the people who notice it are the people it is being done to."

"Frida," she said, after a moment.

"Gaara. And I am going to be in this town for some days, and I would like it known that I have already decided you are the best-run thing in it."

"There is one room."

"There is one room," Gaara repeated, and did not look at either of the other two, and took slightly too long about not looking. "Three of us. Straw. Yes. That is fine. That is entirely fine and I am not going to say the next thing."

"Say it," said Ylva.

"No. I am on my best behaviour in a town where I have no friends and eleven coins, and I want you both to remember this restraint later, at length, and out loud."

The place where the Roll men ate was two doors from the hall and had no name on it, which told him it did not need one.

He got in the corner with his back to the wall and a bowl he did not want, and he did what he had come to do, which was nothing.

Sivert lasted forty minutes.

"A man who was very bad at his job and extremely good at keeping it."

He watched a company come in off a job and count out silver on a table. He watched who counted, and who watched the counting, and who did not bother to watch, and that told him the shape of them inside four minutes. He watched a man arrive alone, get looked at by three tables and served by none of them. He read every man in the room and wrote none of it down, because a man writing in a corner is a man everybody is looking at.

And he found what he had come to find, which was somebody with a problem.

An hour later he stood up, put a hand on the back of Gaara's chair on the way past, and said one word, and the word was fine.

The roll house was one room and it was the plainest building in the Wold.

No banner, no crossed anything on the wall, no man at the door. A counter, a bench for waiting on, and behind the counter a wall of shelves, and on the shelves the only books he had seen since he arrived in this world.

He stood in the doorway a second longer than he meant to.

Because he had it, all of it, from the name and the shelves together, and it arrived the way these things always arrived, complete and unearned and slightly embarrassing.

The thing was a list. That was all it was. A company was warranted because it was written here. It worked in another town because a copy of this went there. A man who broke a contract stopped being able to feed himself anywhere, because somebody put a line through him in a room like this one. Every ounce of it lived in the writing.

Which meant that whatever the men in the yard thought was happening, the person who mattered in this institution was not a captain.

Eight days ago he had come out of a hole in the ground in a dead city with no language, no name he could give anybody, and a rusted knife. He leaned over the counter and spelled his own name into the only book there was.

"Badly. It is going extremely badly."
