# Chapter 29: Put It on Mine

The fourth trip was the easiest of the four and took them a day and a half, and the only thing that happened on it was work.

They knew the street. They knew which wall. They knew that Ask stopping meant go, and that Marit saying one word meant put it down, and that Torv would try to take a leg joint on his own if nobody was looking, so somebody was always looking. Two crawlers came at them at the bottom of the third street and were dead in under a minute, and Gaara got neither of them and did not want them.

They came down at dusk with both sledges over the axles and a third load roped onto the frame of a door.

"Four of four," said Gaara, in the road, to everybody.

"You said that in the street," said Ylva. "Twice."

"I am going to say it for about a week. I have never finished anything in my life. I have left three jobs and two flats in the middle of a sentence. I have just come to the end of a thing I said I would do, and I would like all of you to be enormously impressed for approximately one minute."

Nobody was impressed. Bern counted the party twice, which was the only ceremony that had ever mattered in that road, and then went back to his elm.

Ylva made the plate the next day.

It was flat and it was two feet by three and it was made out of a dead city, hammer welded out of strap hinge and door plate and eleven of the nails that were all the same length. It took her most of a day and she would not let anybody else near the fire.

"You said you could not make a blade."

"This is not a blade." She did not look up. "This is a very flat thing that sits in a hole and does nothing."

"That is also how my last performance review described me."

"Then it will be a good plate," said Ylva.

He went down for the drum himself, with Hallvard, because Hallvard would not have let it be anybody else.

The channel had found its way in the end, the way Hallvard said it would. The water was three inches over the sill instead of climbing, and it had cut itself a new line somewhere out in the dark, and the tube was cold and quiet and it stank of nothing at all.

The drum stood where they had left it in the water at the gate.

Oak the width of a man, iron gudgeons, a pawl and ratchet cut by somebody who had understood exactly what a pawl was for. The chain was black and still greased. Eight hundred years, and the only machine still working in this valley was the one that sent the bill.

Gaara put his hand flat on the mark.

He had done that once before with a twelve in the box and got a quantity and nothing else. He did it now with twenty-seven, and stood there, and the thing in his head took about four seconds and then gave him three lines instead of one.

He read it twice. Then he read it a third time, and something went down the back of his neck that had nothing to do with the water.

"What," said Hallvard.

"Give me a moment."

"You have gone a colour."

"I have gone several." He did not take his hand off the oak. "Hallvard. How many times have you turned this drum."

"I have not counted."

"Yes you have. You have counted everything you have ever done on a slate and wiped it, and you know exactly. You are going to tell me, because I am about to tell you something and I would rather you heard your own number first."

Hallvard stood in three inches of water in the dark and did not answer for a while.

"Twice a month in the good years," he said. "Less after my knees. Up and down is the two of it. It would be seven hundred trips, near enough. It would be fourteen hundred turns."

"Fourteen hundred and twelve."

The old man's face did something Gaara had not seen on it before and could not name.

"You cannot know that."

"I do not know it. It knows it." He turned his hand over on the mark. "This thing has been keeping a book on you for thirty-one years. Every time you and five neighbours got on those bars it wrote the number down, and it has never once lost count. There is not a soul alive who can read it, except a man who arrived in the spring with a pair of eyes he did not ask for."

"Then say what it says."

Gaara said what it said. All three lines, in order, without arranging them.

Hallvard was quiet for a long time.

"Bearer of record," he said eventually. "None."

"Yes."

"What does it mean."

"It means that whatever this thing was built into, and whatever kept accounts for the people who built it, has been drawing on somebody for thirty-one years and does not have his name." Gaara heard his own voice do something and let it. "It has your number and it has your work and it has taken whatever it takes, and where it should say who, there is nothing. You have been paying into a book that does not know you exist."

"You are not telling me all of it."

"No," said Gaara. "Because the rest is arithmetic and I have not checked it and you have had enough for one afternoon."

"Say it anyway."

"When you stand in front of me there is nothing over your head. Not a small number. Nothing at all, the same nothing this drum has where your name should be." He took his hand off the oak. "You have killed sixty or seventy things above your own weight over thirty-one years. By everything I understand about how this works, you should be the most dangerous man between here and the sea, and you read as empty air. I have had those two facts in my head for weeks and never once put them next to each other out loud. I have just done it, and I do not like the shape of it."

The water moved slightly around both of them.

"I thought it was for nothing," said Hallvard.

"What."

"Thirty-one years. Aldis before me and her uncle before her and none of us wrote a word of it down, and I have been lying awake since the road thinking that it went nowhere and was for nothing." He put his own hand on the drum, next to where Gaara's had been. "And you have just told me that something has been keeping the account the whole time."

Gaara looked at him.

"That is the part you have taken out of this."

"That is the part I am keeping."

"I am going to resent that for years," said Gaara. "Nineteen years I spent learning to read a book like that one, and you have taken a better thing out of three lines of it than I ever took out of any of them."

"I can read a slate."

"You can do arithmetic on a slate, which is not the same thing and is in some ways worse, and I say that as a man who could never manage either without a headache."

They carried the drum four hundred paces up the channel and it took them most of the afternoon and six people.

Sivert took the low end on his own for the last hundred of it, because there was a step in the floor and nobody else could get under it.

"I would like it on the record," said Gaara, to the roof of a drain, "that I have seen a great deal in two worlds and that was comfortably the best of it."

"Turn round and walk," said Ylva.

"I am walking. I am walking and thinking about it. The two are entirely compatible."

The south leg was dry, and it ran under the fields and under the old paved road, and it had a rib every forty paces with a slot cut either side of it. Gaara had walked that leg twice with a lamp. He had known what it was since the first time he counted the ribs, and he had done nothing about it, because until there was a plate there was nothing to do.

There was a plate now.

It went into the slot at the fourth rib the way a thing goes into a hole cut for it, which is to say with three people swearing and one person telling them how. Then they got the chain on and set the blocks and put four on the bars.

"You do not need the drum for this," said Vidar. "It slides."

"It slides. It does not seat, and it does not hold, and I would like it to do both." Gaara got his hands on the mark and left them there. "And I want to see what it charges."

"There it is," said Gaara.

"It is drinking it." He was watching the panel and not the chain. "Same as the gate. It is not the bars doing this, or it is not only the bars. Something is coming out of me and going into a plate, and I would very much like to know where the plate is putting it."

They turned for an hour and a half.

At the end of it the plate was seated in the slot at the fourth rib. The south leg of a built drain that ran under forty houses was shut for the first time in eight hundred years. Gaara was sitting against the wall with his legs out in front of him.

"You have gone grey," said Marit.

"I have gone cheap." He got his head back against the stone. "Sixteen out of sixty and I can feel every one of the missing ones, and by tomorrow morning it will be sixty again, because mine comes back. That is the entire thing. That is the whole of what I found out today and it is worth more than the gate."

"Say it in a way I can use."

"Hallvard has been paying this for thirty-one years out of a purse that does not refill." He looked up the dark of the tube. "I have one that does. He is a man being bled and I am a renewable supply, and from this afternoon the difference between those two is the only thing standing between him and whatever the last thirty-one years have already cost him."

Hallvard was at the bar and had not let go of it.

"I have turned that drum since before you were born."

"You have."

"And I will turn it again."

"You will not," said Gaara, "and I am not going to be polite about this one, because I have been polite about your knees and your gate and your slate and I have got nowhere at all. Any turn of that drum from today is mine. When it has to move, I am on the mark with my hands on it and the six of you can have the bars. Whatever it takes, it takes out of the one man in this valley who gets it back."

"That is not yours to decide."

"No. It is yours." He did not get up. "So decide it, and if you decide the other way I will stand at the bar next to you and say nothing, and you will hear about it for the rest of your life."

Hallvard let go of the bar.

"Put it on yours, then," he said.

"Put it on mine."

They told the village that night, because there was no version of six people carrying an oak windlass under the fields that forty houses were not going to ask about.

It went the way those things go. Halle's wife said the same sentence three times with her hands out. A man at the back wanted to know whether the plate could be got out again, and was told yes, and asked twice more anyway. Somebody said that his father had always said there was something under the low field, and everybody ignored him, and he was right.

And Solva stood in the road and worked out what it meant about the night the three of them came down it, and said so before anybody else got there, and then the road went quiet.

"It came under us," she said.

"Yes."

"It came under my house."

"It came under most of them," said Gaara, "and it has been able to since before this village had a name, and the only reason it has not done it since is that a thing with six legs cannot open a door. Tonight it cannot get to the door."

He did not soften any of it and nobody asked him to.

Later, much later, when the road had emptied and the arguing had moved indoors where it belonged, Marit came and stood in front of him with a lamp.

"You said sixteen out of sixty."

"I did."

"Is it back."

"Most of it."

"Then it is the schedule," said Marit, and took the lamp back into Halle's back room, and he followed it, because he had been following it for about four hours.

The room had the beam across the underside of the roof and it had space. He had spent two days in a stone tube being billed by a machine, and there was nothing on this earth he wanted less than to be careful.

He was not careful. Neither was she.

Her heels went up at the rafters and he had her held there with the whole of his weight behind it. It was the least clever thing either of them had done all week and by some distance the best. She did not use one of the four words. She had gone past the four words some time ago and found something better, which was saying his name in a register she kept for absolutely nothing else. He worked for it, and got it three times before the lamp went out.

Afterwards, with the beam a black line above them and Marit's leg across his and going nowhere, she said it into the dark.

"You gave away sixteen out of sixty in a hole in the ground."

"Forty-four out of sixty. I have sixteen left."

"And you did it so an old man does not have to."

"I did it because it comes back," said Gaara. "Everything else about it is his business."

Marit did not say anything for a while.

"You have got very good," she said, "at doing a decent thing and then filing it under something else."

"It is where I keep them," said Gaara. "It is a very old drawer and the lock is broken."
