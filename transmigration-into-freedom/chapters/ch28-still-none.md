# Chapter 28: Still None

Ordulf came himself.

He came on foot, because he did not keep a horse and would not borrow one, and it took him two days. He arrived in the road at Oldstep at the end of the second one, with a long parcel under his arm and an expression that could have stripped bark.

"I have not been out of the Wold in nine years," he said.

"And you have chosen a village with one road, no inn, and a smith who is a woman with better hands than yours."

"I know what Ylva's hands are. I taught the man who taught her." He put the parcel on the wall. "Do not do that thing you do. I am too old and I have walked too far."

"You came to look at Bern."

"I came to deliver a blade."

"You could have sent it up with a carter for two coins and a bad lunch." Gaara did not move off the wall. "You have spent four days and both knees instead. Everybody comes to look at Bern. He is thinking of charging."

"I am not," said Bern, from the bench.

"You should."

Ordulf went and looked at Bern. It took about a quarter of an hour and most of it was Bern talking about elm. At no point did either of them say one word about a leg. When Ordulf came back up the road his face had changed, and Gaara looked at it once and left it alone.

He unwrapped the parcel on the wall in front of half the village.

It was two feet of blade with a spine that went the whole way, and a short simple grip wrapped in cord, and it was not beautiful. Nobody had spent an hour of the eleven days on anything a person could admire. Every one of them had gone into the part that goes into things.

Marit picked it up before anybody offered it to her.

"It is lighter than I thought."

"Everybody says that," said Gaara. "It is a recurring theme in my life and I have made my peace with it."

Ylva put her face in her hands. Ordulf did not react at all, which was somehow worse.

"What is it called," said Sivert.

"Invoice."

"That," said Ordulf, "is a disgraceful name for eleven days of my life."

"It is eleven days of your life, four goats, and a debt I have not paid yet." Gaara took it off Marit and turned it over once. "I would like all three of those going into the next thing I hit. A man who names a blade after a virtue is telling you what he wishes he was. I would rather be reminded what I owe."

They went up on the third trip two mornings later, and this time they went up to find something.

He said it at the top of the rise where the streets started, with Invoice on his hip and Brick still in his belt, because he was not sentimental about a thing that had worked twice.

"Anything under me is scenery. Anything level with me is work. Anything above me is money." He waited for Torv to say it and Torv did not, which was progress. "This is the first job I have ever had where the frightening part and the profitable part are the same part, and I want all of you to understand how enormously that has improved my temper."

"You have been unbearable for two days," said Ylva.

"That is the improvement."

They found it at the cistern at the top of the third street, and it was a Roofwalker, and it was not a nine and it was not an eleven.

"No," said Marit.

"Yes."

"You said above us. You did not say that."

"I know what I said." He was already looking at the street and not at the thing, which was still four hundred feet away and had not seen them. "Everybody get behind the corner and let me be quiet for one minute, and I have never asked any of you for that before and I will not make a habit of it."

They got behind the corner. He was quiet for about forty seconds.

Then he told them what a rib slot was.

The stone tube under the fields had a rib every forty paces and a slot cut either side of every rib. The same people who cut those had built this city. There was a slot exactly like it at the mouth of the cistern channel, because a man who gates a drain in sections gates everything in sections. There was no gate in it any more. There had been one, eight hundred years ago, and it had gone the way of everything else.

They had one. It was in Bern's yard at the bottom of the hill, half an inch thick and the size of a table. It had taken most of an hour to get off the stone and a full day to get down.

"You want to carry the sluice gate back up the hill," said Vidar.

"I want six of you to carry the sluice gate back up the hill. I want the other three to sit here and watch a fourteen and count how often it goes down to the water. It does go to the water, and everything that goes to water goes to it on a schedule."

"And then."

"And then we drop eight hundred years of ironmongery on it in a slot that was cut to hold eight hundred years of ironmongery," said Gaara, "and I would like it minuted that this is the first idea I have had in this world that my old life was any use for at all."

It took them a day and a half.

The thing about the plan, and he said this afterwards more than once, was that no part of it was clever. Six people carried a gate up a hill. Three people sat in a doorway and counted. Ask counted better than any of them and could not say the number out loud, so Marit sat with him and said it for him. What they got was four. The fourteen went down to the cistern mouth four times between first light and noon, and came back up the same channel every time, because it was the only channel.

The gate went into the slot at the top of it with two ropes on and nine people on the ropes.

Gaara stood in the channel mouth on the wrong side of it and made a noise.

"You are the most expensive object in this street," he said.

He said it the way a man says a thing across a table. It filled the channel wall to wall and it was coming at him at the speed of something that has never once had to hurry.

"And I would like you to hold still for four more seconds."

It did not hold still. It came.

He got out through the gap under the gate on his stomach, with about a foot and a half to spare and no dignity of any kind. The ropes went. Half a ton of dressed iron came down into a slot cut for it in the reign of somebody nobody remembers, and it did not kill the thing.

It held it.

That was the entire idea and it was still appalling to watch. The Roofwalker came into the gate at full weight three times and the gate did not move, because it had been made by people who expected water to try that. And on the fourth it stopped and started working at the edge with something at the front of it, patiently, the way a man works at a nail.

"Now," said Gaara.

Nine people went in at a thing that could not turn round.

It took twenty minutes and it was not brave and it was not a fight, and Torv was sick afterwards against a wall and nobody said anything about it. Sivert put the good axe into the same joint four times because it was the joint they knew. Ylva stood on the gate itself with the hammer, out of reach, and hit the same place on the head of it thirty or forty times with no expression at all.

Gaara went in under the gate at the end with two feet of Ordulf's blade and found the seam on the first attempt.

Invoice went in past the guard and did not stop where Brick would have stopped.

He sat down on the wrong side of the gate with his back against it and read that with his hands shaking, which they had not done after the eleven.

"Seven," he said.

"One kill," said Ylva.

"One kill four levels above me, taken with a piece of municipal water management." He got his breath. "The rule holds and it holds obscenely. That is worth more than everything the nine of us have done since harvest."

"Say the numbers," said Marit, "because you always do, and if you do not say them I will assume something is wrong with you."

"Fifteen points."

"That is the most you have ever had."

Nothing got faster and nothing got warmer. What happened was that the street got quiet in a way the street was not, and then he was holding all of it at once. The count of the ribs. Which of the nine were breathing wrong. The angle of the slot and why it was that angle and not another. Ylva's three piles, and what each of them weighed, and what each was worth in goats and in coins and in days of a man's work. All of it at once, and none of it in order.

He sat very still for about half a minute.

"Well," he said.

"What."

"I have spent my whole life being pleased with myself for something." He put his hand flat on the iron beside him. "And I have just found out I was doing it on a twelve."

"Is it good," said Sivert.

"It is not good and it is not bad. It is loud." He shut his eyes. "And I will tell you the one part of it that is an unambiguous improvement. I have just gone back over something I did in a forge with no window in it, and I have found two errors, and I would like the chance to correct them."

"That is not what a forge is for," said Ylva.

"It is what that forge was for."

Sivert put his head against the gate and left it there.

Gaara shut his eyes again. "I can hear myself thinking about four things and I chose none of them. Talk to me about something unimportant for a few minutes while I find the handle."

Sivert talked to him about elm for a few minutes.

It helped, and Gaara filed the fact that it helped, and then filed the fact that he had filed it, and then made himself stop.

Later, on the way down, with the sledges going ahead and his arm across his body again, he went through the panel line by line the way he had not done since the first morning.

Level 7. Sixteen kills. Two skills. A body he had bought in three separate transactions and could now, on a good day, rely on.

And at the top of it, where it had said the same word since the first morning in the ash:

Class: None

"It still says None," he said.

"Says what," said Marit.

"Class. It has a line for it and the line has never had anything in it." He was almost laughing and it was not a good laugh. "I have a company registered in a guild book in the Wold. I have nine people who do what I say in a dead city. I have a village on a lord's roll, a doctrine nailed to a byre, and a blade named after a debt. The system has looked at every bit of that and written the word None, which is the most accurate performance review I have ever been given in either world."

"It will say something eventually."

"It will say something when I do whatever the thing is." He shifted the strap on his arm. "And that is the part that frightens me, because I have been assuming for seven levels that I am the man doing all this. It has been sitting there the whole time with an empty box, waiting to tell me what I actually am."

They came down at dusk.

Ordulf had gone the morning they went up, and had left without saying goodbye to anybody, which Bern said was the friendliest thing he had ever done. There was bread, and there was a great deal of arguing about the gate. It was still up the hill in its slot and it was going to stay there, and Ylva declared it the most expensive thing anybody in Oldstep had ever thrown away.

"It is not thrown away. It is deployed."

"It is nine goats in a hole in a wall."

"It is nine goats in a hole in a wall that killed a fourteen," said Gaara. "It will be there when we come back, and there is a fourth trip. I have finally found a use for a thing that does nothing at all except stand still and refuse to move, and I would like that noted, because I have been that thing at four separate companies."

He got a hand on Marit going past her at the fire, without stopping and without particular subtlety, in the middle of a sentence about slot depth.

She let it happen for about a second and a half and then took his wrist and moved it, and did not stop him talking about slot depth either.

"Later," she said.

"That is not a no."

"It is a schedule." She went and sat down next to Ylva. "You have four things in your head and you told us all so, in the street, at length. Go and put them down somewhere."

He went and sat on the wall in the last of the light and put them down one at a time, which took longer than it should have because the new one kept picking them back up.

The gate. The fourth trip. The eight hundred years of iron in Ylva's three piles and what it would fetch in a town that had just heard the word Hades for the first time.

And a wax tablet, going east on a horse, with the name of a company written on it in somebody else's hand.
