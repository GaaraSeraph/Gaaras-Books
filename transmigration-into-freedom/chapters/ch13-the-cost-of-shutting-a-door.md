# Chapter 13: The Cost of Shutting a Door

The gear was under the byre.

Gaara stood in the doorway and looked at the floor he had slept on two nights ago. At the four goats. At Hallvard levering up a flagstone with the ferrule of a stick he did not lean on.

"I slept on it."

"You did."

"You put me in the one building in this village with it under the floor."

"I put you where I could find you," said Hallvard, "and where the thing I did not want found was under a stranger who did not know it was there. If you had gone looking, you would have gone looking somewhere else."

"That is either very good or very lazy and I cannot decide which."

"Both, usually."

Under the stone there was a dry pit lined with slate, and in the pit there was a windlass.

No village made this. Oak drum, iron gudgeons, a pawl and ratchet cut by somebody who understood what a pawl is for, and a coil of chain that had been greased so many times the links were black with it. Four oak levers. A dozen iron wedges. Two blocks and a sling.

Gaara went down on one knee at the edge of the pit and did not touch any of it.

"Whose was it?"

"Aldis Halden's, when it was hers. It was old then." Hallvard set the flagstone aside. "There is a mark on the drum. I have never known what it says."

There was a mark on the drum. Gaara looked at it, and the thing in his head looked at it with him and returned exactly nothing, and he had a moment of genuine cold about that which he decided not to explain.

He put his hand on the chain instead.

Windlass, human manufacture. Oak, iron.
Condition: sound. Chain sound. Pawl worn, functional.
Rated well above intended load.

"There," he said. "It reads. It reads because I have hands on it and because somebody once decided that counted." He stood up. "Right. Talk me through raising it."

"Four men on the bars," said Hallvard. "Two to set the wedges. It took us nine hours."

"Us."

"Aldis, before she died. Two men from this village who are also dead now, of ordinary things." He said it without weight. "I have not had four men since. That is why the gate has been open since spring and not since the day after spring."

Gaara stood in the byre and worked it, and the answer arrived and it was not one he liked.

"You cannot drop it," he said.

"No."

"Because those two blocks are carrying eight hundred years of iron, and blocks under load do not come out by being hit. You have to lift the gate an inch to free them and then let it down, and lifting it means the windlass, and the windlass means the bars." He rubbed his face with the back of his wrist. "You cannot shut that door with fewer men than it took to open it."

"No."

"And it has to stand in the channel. In the water. On the far side of the mouth, where the only road in and out is the road they use."

"Yes."

"For nine hours."

"It will be less," said Hallvard, "coming down. Perhaps four."

"Four hours," said Gaara, "of six men standing in a stone tube that a thing the size of a pig uses as a corridor, in the dark, at night, because you cannot do it in daylight without half the valley watching."

"Now you have it."

Marit had been leaning in the byre door the whole time with her arm strapped across her body and her hatchet loose in her left hand, and she spoke for the first time.

"Six men," she said. "You are going to have to ask."

"Yes."

"In that road. Where you told them this afternoon that they held a meeting in their doorways."

"Yes," said Gaara.

"That was clever of you."

"It was extremely satisfying of me," said Gaara, "which is not the same thing, and I have been aware of the difference since about four o'clock."

He sat down on the upturned bucket he had sharpened a knife on, in a byre with four goats and a hole in the floor, and did the thing he had been putting off since the gate.

"Let us do the arithmetic out loud," he said, "because I have been wrong three times this week and I would like witnesses."

Hallvard sat on the flagstone. Marit did not sit.

"One. Killing them does not solve it. There are four hundred nests in that room and the thing that makes them is behind a line I cannot see across. I could stand in the shallows opening crawlers for a year and get old and rich and change nothing." He held up a finger. "Two. The gate solves the road. We cannot touch the room and I am not going to pretend otherwise, but everything that has died in your fields came down a channel, and a channel is a thing with a door on it."

"And three?" said Marit.

"Three is unpleasant." He looked at Hallvard. "I want to go back in there several times before we shut it."

The old man's head came up.

"Because that room pays," said Gaara. "You know it pays. You have been down there thirty-one years."

"I have never once looked at it that way."

"No, and that is the difference between us, and I would not spend much time deciding which of us it flatters." He leaned forward. "When I killed a six down there yesterday it was worth most of a step to me. There is nothing in this valley that will pay like that, and I am going to need to be worth more than I am before somebody, somewhere, asks me a question I cannot answer with a rusty knife. So yes. I want to hunt in the room I am about to seal."

"You want to farm it," said Hallvard.

"I want to use it. There is a difference and it is mostly in the timing."

Marit said, "How many times?"

"Three. Four. Whatever it takes to get me high enough that I am not the weakest thing standing in that tube for four hours while six of your neighbours turn a drum."

Nobody said anything for a while. One of the goats came over and investigated the pit and was moved.

"Hallvard," said Gaara. "What level are you?"

"I do not know what that means."

"You do. Marit told you what I see. Numbers over people." He watched the old man's face and got nothing off it, which was itself a data point. "You have killed sixty or seventy of those things over thirty-one years, most of them bigger than you, in the dark, alone. If it counted for you the way it counts for me, you would be the most dangerous man between here and the sea."

"I am a seventy-year-old man with a bad hip," said Hallvard, "who is better with a stick than he should be. That is all I have ever noticed."

"There is nothing over your head. Nothing at all. Everyone else in this village has a name and a number and you have empty air."

"Then I would say," said Hallvard slowly, "that I have been down in that room a great many times. That there is a line cut in the floor of it. And that I have crossed it more often than any living thing."

Gaara went very still.

"Say that again."

"You heard it."

"I want it twice," said Gaara. "Liars decorate."

Hallvard almost smiled.

"I have crossed the line in that floor more often than any living thing," he said, in exactly the same words. "Make of it what you like. I never could."

They washed at the trough behind Solva's house because Solva made them, and because after three days there was not much argument left in either of them.

Gaara got his shirt off and Marit looked at the state of him and said something short and Nordic that the thing in his head did not translate, which he took as a compliment and said so.

"You have no idea what it meant."

"It is what I have decided it meant. I am keeping it."

She washed one-handed and he ended up helping with the strapped arm, which was practical and businesslike and took a great deal longer than practical and businesslike needed to take.

"Marit."

"Mm."

"For the record."

Her good hand stopped.

"The word is for something else," she said.

"No," Gaara agreed. "It is not. I am misusing an agreed signal in a private capacity and I want that noted. I have exactly one thing in this world that is worth anything, and it is that I do not say things that are untrue."

"Say a true thing, then."

"I have been in this world three days," said Gaara. "In that time I have been threatened with an axe, put on trial in a road, and told a woman her son was dead. I watched a man die because I planned for one. Something reached into my chest in the dark and took a piece out. And the only part of any of it I would repeat is the walking."

Marit did not say anything.

"That is the true thing," he said. "I would repeat the walking."

She looked at him for a long moment with water running off her wrist.

"Byre's this way," she said. "You snore, you sleep outside."

"That is what you said the first night."

"It is." She picked up her shirt. "It was not an invitation then."

He did not sleep in the byre.

There was a bed under the eaves of the Halden house, with a good wool blanket and a window that did not shut properly, and a draught off it that neither of them noticed for some time.

The arm was the first problem and it was not a small one. She had barbs across the right forearm and up under the arm into the ribs, and she could not put weight on that side. She got about a third of the way out of her shirt before she made a noise she had not wanted to make.

"Stop," said Gaara. "Sit. Let me."

"I am not an invalid."

"You are not an invalid and I am not being noble. I am being slow on purpose and you are in my way." He got the shirt over the strapping without touching it. "Now be quiet a moment. I have not looked at you properly since the road."

"You look at everybody properly. It is the most irritating thing about you."

"I look at everybody for information," said Gaara. "This is not that."

She let him.

He kissed her first, and she had a shape in her head for how that would go, and it was not that shape.

The first one was closed lips and nothing else, and it did not last long. The second was less careful than the first, and not by very much. By the third she had come up off the pillow to meet it, which he noted with enormous satisfaction and did not act on. The fourth had the tip of his tongue in it for about a second, and then did not.

After that it went up. It kept going up all night and it never once went up by more than she had already come forward to take. That was the method entire, and there was nothing else in it. Give her a shade less than she has just asked for, and make her ask again.

It got wild later on. It never got wet. He had views about that which he kept to himself.

He had spent three days being handed information about this world in writing. None of it had covered anything he wanted to know now. There was no number over any of this and no window that would open on it, and the only way to have it was to work it out from scratch.

So he worked it out. The inside of her wrist, above where the strapping started. The base of her throat. The place behind her ear where her jaw met her neck, which shut her good hand hard in the blanket. The back of her knee, which surprised her a great deal more than it surprised him.

He spent longer on her breasts than she had allowed for. She had a commentary running when he started and she did not have it by the end, and she never once conceded either of those things out loud.

"How do you know that," she said.

"I do not know anything. I am watching you and you keep answering."

"Stop narrating."

"Absolutely not."

He learned the ribs by the sound she made when he came near them, and after that he went round them, and she noticed him going round them and said nothing about it. He kept his weight off her right side for the whole of the night without once mentioning that he was doing it. That was the only tenderness he had that he trusted, because it did not require him to say anything and could not be misheard as a promise.

Then he went lower, and she got a fistful of his hair and held on to it.

"What is that."

"Something nobody in this valley appears to have thought of, which I intend to take up with somebody in the morning." He waited where he was. "One syllable and I go back to what I was doing, and I will not sulk about it."

She did not say the syllable.

"Then you get four words," said Gaara, "and they are the only four you will ever need from me. Slower. Faster. Higher. Lower."

"What am I supposed to do with four words."

"Use them. You will not have to explain yourself and I will not stop to ask."

Her hand came out of his hair and lay flat on the back of his head instead, which he took as the contract being agreed.

It was some time before she used one.

When she did it was slower, said into the dark, in nothing at all like the voice she used for testing people. He did as he was told inside half a second. She noticed that he did. She got through the other three before she had finished with him, and the last one she said twice.

She came apart twice before she said anything at all about it and once more afterwards, and he kept count, and he was aware that keeping count was an odd thing to be doing.

He had put nineteen years of work into things and found out years later whether any of it had landed, on the occasions when he found out at all. Three days ago a window had opened in his head and started telling him what a thing had been worth in the same second he did it. This did the same and did it faster, and he wanted the number, and he was not sorry about wanting it.

Afterwards she lay with her good arm over her eyes and got her breathing back.

"Is that a thing where you come from," she said.

"It is."

"Everybody."

"Broadly."

"Then everybody where you come from is very stupid about a great many things," said Marit, "and extremely sensible about one."

There was a considerable amount of laughing at the wrong moments, some of it about the strapped arm and most of it about him.

Then there was not.



She got him onto his back, because she could manage that one-handed. She told him what she wanted in the flat voice she used for rope and ground and other things she knew about. Then she told him again, harder, and he did what he was told for a considerable length of time.

Nothing she did to him was skilled. Nobody in these forty houses had ever been taught any of it, and there was nobody living who could have done the teaching, and it mattered about as much as the weather in another country. What she had instead was that she wanted it, out loud, and was not apologising to anybody for any part of it. His arms had opinions by the end of it. He ignored them, because the only thing in that room he was interested in was the sound she was making, and he would have gone on until the roof came off.

At one point she told him to stop talking, and he did, which had now happened exactly twice in his life and both times in this village.

Afterwards he did not get up and he did not go anywhere, and it turned out that she had been braced for both. He got the blanket over her and got himself round the side of her that had nothing wrong with it, and stayed there. She was rigid about it for perhaps a minute. Then she was not.

Afterwards he lay in the dark with the panel hanging at the edge of his vision the way it always hung there, and he looked at it, and it said what it always said.

Level 4. HP 220. Kills 11.

Nothing else. No line for this. No number for any of it.

"Good," he said, out loud, to the thing in his head. "Keep it that way."

Marit, without opening her eyes, said, "Who are you talking to?"

"The bookkeeper."

"Tell him to go home."

He woke in the grey with the window rattling and a shoulder that had stiffened into something with opinions, and lay still for a while, working.

Six men. Four hours. A tunnel.

Forty houses that had stood in their doorways, and a stranger who had told them so in their own road, at volume, with an audience.

It's always the audience.

He got up carefully, and got dressed, and stood at the bad window looking down the length of Oldstep at the raw stripe of earth at the far end where Osvald was.

"You are going to go and ask them," Marit said, behind him.

"I am going to go and ask them."

"They will say no."

"Most of them will say no," Gaara agreed. "I need six."
