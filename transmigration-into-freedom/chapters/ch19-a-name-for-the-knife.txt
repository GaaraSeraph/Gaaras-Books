He asked four people for something to write on and got four different kinds of nothing.

Hallvard had the slate, and the slate was the slate. It had been wiped every evening for thirty-one years and it would be wiped tonight. Solva had wax on a board for counting salt and wanted it back before dark. Marit said there was parchment in the Wold if he had money, and then remembered what he had, and did not finish the sentence.

The fourth was Torv, who thought about it seriously and said that a man who needed to remember a thing generally remembered it.

So he went round the back of Bern's house and stole eleven roofing shingles.

They were split oak, thin as a finger, flat on one face and rough on the other, and there was a stack of them against every wall in Oldstep because a roof is a thing that eats them. He took a burnt stick out of the fire. He sat on the trough wall with the sun not properly up and wrote the first page of anything in this world.

It went badly for an hour. He knew what the letters were and his hand did not, and the charcoal broke twice, and he spelled his own doctrine wrong in three places and only caught two.

Then he had it, and he read it back to himself under his breath, and it was five lines long and it was the ugliest object he had ever been proud of.

One. Take it at a wall. A wall does half your work and never gets tired.

Two. Do not chase it. Everything down there is faster than you and none of it is cleverer.

Three. If a number is called, that is how much trouble it is. Believe the number over your own eyes.

Four. Nobody goes in front alone.

Five. Feet before hands. A man who is standing can do anything. A man who is falling can do one thing badly.

He looked at it for a while.

"Right," he said, to a village that was still mostly asleep. "Now find out if it is any good."

He handed them out after breakfast, and the whole thing came apart inside four minutes.

Torv turned his shingle over twice, looking for the side with the writing on it. He held it out at arm's length and squinted. Then he put it down on the bench very carefully and looked at the ground.

"I do not read," he said.

Gaara did not say anything for a moment, which for him was a considerable event.

"Who here does."

Marit put her hand up. Hallvard did not bother. Ylva shook her head once. Vidar said his mother had, which was not an answer to the question and was the whole answer to the question. Bern said he could read a tally mark and a mason's number and nothing that used letters. Sivert said no and looked cross about it. Ask said nothing, which by now meant no.

Two out of nine, and one of the two was seventy years old and one of them was staying behind.

"I have spent an hour," said Gaara, "writing a thing for people who cannot read it. In my last life I would have had somebody's job for that, and the somebody would have been me."

"You could read it out," said Marit.

"Then it is a speech, and I have made a great many speeches, and none of them survived me leaving the room." He picked Torv's shingle back up and turned it over. "No. It has to work when I am not there. That is the entire point of it and I built it wrong at the first step."

"Read it out anyway," said Ylva. "And then we will tell you what is wrong with it."

So he read it out.

Ylva got the first one, which he should have seen coming, because she was the only person there who had killed something bigger than herself twice.

"Four is wrong," she said.

"Go on."

"Nobody goes in front alone." She had her arms folded. "In the street you went in front alone. Under the gate you went in front alone. You have done it every single time and you wrote down that we should not."

"Because you should not."

"Then write that. You wrote a rule for everybody and you are not in it." She did not raise her voice at any point. "If a rule has you outside it, it is not a rule. It is a favour you are doing us."

Vidar said the shingles would go soft in wet weather and ought to be waxed. It was the least interesting thing anybody said all morning and Gaara wrote it on the back of his hand, because it was also correct, and because a product that dissolves in autumn is not a product.

And then Bern.

They had carried him out into the road on a bench because he had said he would crawl otherwise. He had one leg and a colour like porridge and he had been listening to the whole of it with his eyes shut.

"Five," said Bern.

"Feet before hands."

"I had my feet." He opened his eyes. "You shouted at me about my feet in that street and you thanked me for them. I had my feet and I did the thing exactly the way you said and I am sitting on a bench with one of them."

Nobody in the road moved.

"Say the rest of it," said Gaara.

"Your page tells a man how to win. There is not one word on it about what happens when it goes wrong." Bern shifted his weight and went grey doing it. "It goes wrong. That is the part I know about now and you do not. You have never once had it go wrong."

"I have had it go wrong twice and both times it went wrong to somebody else."

"Then you still do not know," said Bern. "Write down when to stop. Write down who says stop and whether I am allowed to say it. I did not know if I was allowed to say it."

Gaara stood in the road holding a stack of bad shingles and understood that he had just been handed the only note that mattered by a man he had crippled.

"That is going on the front," he said. "Before all five. And it is going to have your name on it, and you are going to be extremely annoying about that for the rest of your life."

"I intend to be."

The forge was behind a gate on the north side and the gate had been shut since before he arrived.

He had walked past it eleven times. He had read the woman standing at it as a seven on his second day and had never once looked at what she was standing in front of. That went on the list of things he had been too pleased with himself to see.

Ylva opened it for him without being asked why.

Anvil, bellows, a slack tub with an inch of green water in it. And racks, and on the racks a quantity of stock that stopped him in the doorway, because an anvil is only an anvil and a full rack in a village is a fortune standing in the dark.

"Whose was this."

"Rulf's." She went past him and put her hand flat on the anvil, briefly, the way you touch a horse before you walk behind it. "He went down with Aldis and came back. Then he went down again."

"How long has it been cold."

"Nine years."

"Can you work it."

"I can weld a share and set a tyre and put an edge on a scythe. I stood at that bellows for twenty years." She turned round. "I cannot make you a blade. Do not ask me to, because I will say yes and it will break in something's neck and then you will be Bern."

"Then who."

"Ordulf in the Wold. He is not a good man and he is a very good smith." She said it flat, in the same voice she used for numbers. "Four goats."

"I have one goat. In theory. I have never seen the goat."

"Then you have no blade."

He walked the length of the racks and back and looked at what was on them. Then he stood still in the middle of a cold workshop and did the arithmetic he had been doing since he was twenty-two.

Bar stock. Rod. A crate of broken shares. Everything a forge holds and nothing that could be turned into a weapon, because a village forge is fed by a village and a village has no steel.

"Ylva. How much of what is on these racks came out of the ground round here."

"None of it. It comes up the road from the Wold and it costs what it costs."

"And there is a city an hour up the hill with eight hundred years of worked iron lying in it."

She looked at him.

"Everybody knows that," she said. "There has never been a year when everybody did not know that. Rulf knew it. It is why he went the second time."

"So the metal is not the problem and it never was." He put both hands on the edge of the bench. "The problem is that the only people who can walk in there and come out again are standing in this shed. And one of us is the highest number in this valley and the other one talks too much."

"You want to bring it out."

"I want to bring it out and I want you to work it and I want you to keep half. That is not generosity, it is that I cannot carry it, cannot forge it, and cannot sell it, so half of nothing is worse than half of something." He straightened up. "And I want the first thing off that anvil to be mine."

Ylva was quiet for long enough that he heard the bellows leather creak in the cold.

"Ordulf will want paying in stock," she said. "Not coin. Nobody in the Wold has seen good stock in ten years and he will take it over money and hate himself for it."

"That is what I was hoping you would say."

"And I am coming with you."

He had a sentence ready and did not use it.

"I am forty," said Ylva. "I have stood behind this gate since I was twenty. Last week a man I had never met told me what I was, and it was the first time anybody had ever told me a number about myself, and I have not slept properly since." She shut the gate behind them both. "I am not asking. I am telling you in front of the anvil so you cannot be charming about it."

"I was not going to be charming about it."

"You are always going to be charming about it."

He found Marit at the byre at dusk and did not get halfway through the first sentence.

"No," she said.

"You have no idea what I was going to say."

"I have every idea what you were going to say. You have been building up to it since the road." She was sitting on the step with her forearms on her knees and she did not look up. "A man lost a leg in my hands yesterday and I have to take six people back into that place next week without you. No. Not tonight."

"Right."

"That is not about you."

"I had worked that out, and I want it noted that I worked it out in under a second, which is a personal record." He sat down on the step beside her, at a distance, and looked out at the same nothing she was looking at. "Do you want me to go away or do you want me to sit here and talk rubbish until you fall asleep."

"The second one."

So he did that instead, for about an hour, badly.

Afterwards he sat on the trough wall on his own with the wedge iron across his knees.

Eighteen inches of soft iron, square in section, no edge, no point, no balance, borrowed from a seventy year old man and never given back. It had killed a five on a windlass and a nine in a street. The world had looked at it and at him and issued a rank for the pair of them.

He had let a piece of obsidian go without a name and then given it one too late. He had carried a dead man's knife for four days and never named it at all, because he had not expected it to last, and it had not.

"You are going to be melted into the tang of something better," he said to the bar, "and you will not be there when it happens. So I am doing this first."

He turned it over once.

"Brick," he said. "Because that is what you swing like, and because you have never once let me down, and I want that on the record in front of a trough."
