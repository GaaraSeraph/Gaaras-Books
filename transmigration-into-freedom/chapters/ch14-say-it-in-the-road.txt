He did it wrong first, and he did it wrong in the way he had done it wrong for nineteen years.

He got them into the road after the morning milking and gave them the facts in order.

The channel. The gate. The eighteen inches. The four hundred nests. The windlass under the floor they were standing on, four men on the bars, two on the wedges, four hours in the dark.

He was clear and brief and did not decorate any of it, and he put it in front of forty houses like a proposal that had been checked twice.

Then he waited, and they looked at their boots.

"Well?" said Gaara.

Somebody coughed.

"Is he finished?" a man said, further back.

"He is never finished," said Marit, from the byre wall. "He takes his time. And then he takes some more time. And then, when you have entirely given up on him, he gets there."

Somebody laughed. Several people did.

Gaara turned round slowly.

"Nobody has ever accused me of being quick," said Gaara. "They accuse me of other things."

"You will want a word about this later."

"I will want a word about this later."

"You will take your time over it," said Marit, "and then you will take some more."

It bought nine seconds of goodwill, which was all he got, and he knew exactly who had earned them.

Then the heavy man with the boar spear said, "Why should we?"

"Because in the spring it will not be four."

"You say."

"I say," Gaara agreed. "You have known me three days, two of them I spent insulting you, and I am asking you to stand in a hole in the dark for four hours on my arithmetic." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Put like that I would not come either. The pay is one loaf, four hours on a bar in the wet, and my continued company in a confined space. I have accepted worse offers than that, and I want you to know I regretted most of them."

Nobody moved.

He stood on the step and felt the thing arrive that he had felt in the road at three in the morning, with the smell coming up out of the north. The same thing. The same shape.

The information was right. It had always been right. It was worth nothing in his mouth.

It's always the audience.

"In my old life," he said, mostly to Marit, "I once failed to get eleven people to agree on the colour of a folder. I have no idea why I thought forty would queue up for a tunnel."

"Right," he said. "That is my fault and not yours. Give me an hour."

Hallvard was at his table with his hands flat on it. He had been listening through the open door, because he did not ask how it had gone.

Gaara shut the door and stayed standing.

"They will not come for me," he said.

"No."

"They will come for you."

"Some of them."

"All six." Gaara put his knuckles on the table. "You have had thirty-one years of being the man who is right about things. That is the only account in this village with anything in it, and I am here to ask you to spend it."

"You do not need to ask," said Hallvard. "I will stand in the road and tell them to go, and they will go."

"That is beside what I am asking for."

The old man's eyes came up.

"They cannot go down there not knowing," said Gaara. "Four hours in a tunnel, in the dark, with the mouth of that room forty feet away. If it goes wrong it will go wrong in nine seconds, and men who were told the wrong thing do the wrong thing in nine seconds. They have to know what is behind the gate, how long you have been going down there, and why it is open."

Hallvard sat very still.

"You want me to say it in the road."

"I want you to say it in the road."

"You could say it for me."

"I could not," said Gaara. "I have one asset in this village and it is that nothing I have said here has been untrue. I am not going to spend it telling your story for you. It has to come out of your mouth, in front of the houses you have been counting for thirty-one years." He straightened up. "Also, I am not going to do it because it is yours. That is not kindness. It is bookkeeping."

The room was quiet for a long moment.

"They will take the elder off me," said Hallvard.

"Almost certainly. On the other hand you will get a full night's sleep for the first time in thirty-one years, and at your age that is not nothing."

"That is a very poor joke."

"It is the best one available. I looked."

"Aldis's father did eleven years and died of it with nobody knowing. Her uncle did nineteen. I have done thirty-one, and not one year of it has ever been known, and that was not vanity, it was the design."

"I understand the design," said Gaara. "The design has four graves in it now, and a fifth if you count the dogs together, and it has run out. Designs do that. They run for years, then something changes underneath them, and they run one more season on momentum, and then they stop."

Hallvard looked at his own hands.

"You have done this before," he said. "Not the ruin. This."

"I have been in the room when a thing that worked for eleven years stopped working," said Gaara. "I was usually holding it. Nobody told me either, until it was in the road."

The old man got up, slowly. He picked up the stick, and he did not lean on it.

"Come and hold the door," he said.

It took him about four minutes.

He stood in the middle of the paved road with his stick in his hand and said it in order, and he did not decorate any of it.

Gaara stood at the edge of the crowd and understood that he was watching a man do the most expensive thing he would ever do, in flat sentences.

Thirty-one years. Aldis before him. Her uncle before her. Nobody wrote it down, and that was deliberate, and here is why. And in the spring I raised the gate eighteen inches on my own arithmetic. I was right about the rate and I was wrong about the noticing. The boy who was fishing, and the man with the cart, and Teodor, and Osvald are on that.

He did not say sorry once.

Gaara noticed that, and approved of it, and knew exactly how few people in that road would.

It went the way those things go. Silence, and then noise. A woman near the front said the same sentence four times with her hands out. Somebody at the back shouted about the lord. Solva pushed through and stopped dead and said nothing at all.

And in the middle of it, Bern said, "Who's going down?"

Which was the whole game, and Gaara moved.

"Six," he said. "Four on the bars, two on the wedges. Everyone who comes gets told everything before they set foot in the channel, and anybody who wants out can be out at the mouth. I will think no worse of him. I will think a great deal worse of a man who changes his mind halfway, because at that point I will be busy."

He was already reading the road while he talked, and by now he was not trying to stop. Faces came up with names and numbers on them, and he sorted them without deciding to.

The heavy man with the boar spear was a 5, which surprised him.

Bern was a 4.

A wide slow man near the pens was a 6, which surprised him considerably more.

And a woman of about forty behind the smith's gate, who had said nothing all morning and whose name he did not know, came up as a 7.

Seven. The highest thing he had seen in that village.

"You," he said.

She looked at him.

"I do not know your name," said Gaara, "and I am going to ask you first anyway, and I am not going to explain why in the road."

"Ylva," she said.

"Will you come?"

Torv, the wide slow man near the pens, said, "She's a woman."

"Yes," said Gaara. "I did notice. I have been noticing things all week. It is how I found your cellar."

Somebody at the back made a sound.

"She is what I want," said Gaara. "I said six men because that is what the old arrangement said and I repeated it without thinking. I have been wrong about numbers four times this week. I am not going to be wrong about this one out of politeness."

Ylva looked at him for a moment.

"My husband went down with Aldis," she said. "He came back the first time."

Nobody had told Gaara that. He filed it, and he did not use it, and he was aware that a worse man would have.

"Then you know what the bars are," he said. "Will you come?"

"Aye."

He got Bern. He got Torv, who did not apologise for the remark and came anyway, which Gaara considered the more useful of the two. He got a silent man of thirty called Ask. And he got the heavy man with the boar spear, who came up close first and said, quietly, "I still think you have got a mouth on you."

"You are correct. It is the only part of me that has never been injured, and I have been working on that for nineteen years."

That was the entire negotiation.

Five.

He stood in the road for a while with the sun going and did not get a sixth.

"I will take the sixth bar," said Hallvard.

"You are seventy."

"I have taken that bar before."

"With four men who are now dead of ordinary things," said Gaara. "I need you at the wedges. That is the part where knowing what you are doing matters more than what you can lift. And if the gate comes down wrong, I will take that loss on a man who can pull, not on the only man who has ever done it."

Hallvard almost smiled.

"You have got me at the wedges and Ylva on a bar," he said. "You are picking by something."

"Yes," said Gaara.

"Will you tell me by what?"

"No," said Gaara.

The sixth came at full dark and it was Solva, and it took ten minutes and both hands to talk her out of it.

"You are sixty," he said, "and I have watched you carry bread with two hands."

"He was my son."

"He was, and he is not down there, and the thing that is down there does not care whose son he was." He held on to it the way you hold a rope. "If you come and it goes wrong, I will spend four hours watching you instead of the mouth of that room. So will Marit. So will Bern. That is how six people become four."

Solva stood in her doorway with the cold lamp still on its stone beside the step.

"You will not lie to me," she said, and it came out flat, with no question in it.

"No."

"Then say it plain."

"You would slow us down and it might kill somebody," said Gaara. "That is plain."

She looked at him a long moment. Then she went in and came back with a loaf wrapped in cloth, put it in his hands, and shut the door.

Gaara stood in the dark holding bread, which was, on balance, the worst he had felt all week.

It got worse about forty minutes later.

Marit found him at the byre and did not come all the way in, which told him something before she spoke.

"Rulf's horse is gone," she said. "And Rulf with it."

"Who is Rulf."

"Ylva's brother-in-law. He was at the back of the road this morning, shouting about the lord. He went east at dusk, and his wife says he took a bag and the good coat."

Gaara put the bread down on the rail very carefully, because the alternative was to throw it.

"How far east."

"Two days to the Wold, where the lord's man sits. Three if the ford is up."

"Of course it is two days. It is always two days." He was looking at the north wall of the byre and not at her. "The gap between a man deciding to ruin something and the ruin turning up is always exactly long enough to be unbearable and never long enough to be useful. I have watched it happen on three continents and it is the only law of nature I fully trust."

He stood in the dark of the byre and did the arithmetic he did not want.

Thirty-one years of an old man saying nothing, and a chain of it going back past Aldis and her uncle and her father. The whole structure had held until this morning, when a stranger with a clean argument required the truth to be said out loud in a road.

"I did that," he said.

"He did that."

"He did that because I made him." Gaara rubbed his face with both hands. "And I would do it again, because six men in a tunnel on a lie is how you get six dead men, and I am not sorry, and none of that changes the horse."

"Two days there," said Marit. "Two days back, if they come."

"If they come they will come with six men and a serjeant and they will look at a gate that is eighteen inches open and they will not shut it." He was already moving. "They will widen it. That is what men do with a door that somebody else is guarding. They will want to know what is worth eight hundred years of ironwork."

"So we have four days."

"We have four days to be finished and gone, and I have just spent an evening explaining to a woman of sixty why she is too slow." He got his coat off the nail. "Get Hallvard. Get the six. Nobody sleeps late, and I want it noted that in my case that is a genuine sacrifice and I am making it for your neighbours."

Marit did not move yet.

"You could have let him keep it," she said. "The secret. You could have taken the men on his word and never told them why."

"Yes."

"Would that have been so bad?"

"It would have worked," said Gaara. "It would have worked beautifully, right up until the nine seconds, and then it would have killed somebody, and I would have known why and nobody else would." He pulled the door open. "Also I would have had to lie, and then I would have been a man in this village who lies, and that is the only thing I own."

They got the sixth in the morning.

It was Halle's boy, nineteen, who had helped axe the small one in the road on the night Osvald died. He came to the trough before anyone was awake and said he wanted a bar.

Gaara looked at him.

Level 2.

The lowest he had taken, half the weight of Torv, and nineteen, which was a number he had opinions about.

"Why," he said.

"Because I was there and I did it and I am not doing that again," said the boy, meaning something he did not have the words for and which Gaara understood immediately.

"Right," said Gaara. "You are on a bar next to Torv, you will do exactly what he does, and if I say the word you go up the channel and you do not stop and you do not look round."

"What's the word?"

"For the record."

Behind him, at the byre door, Marit made a noise into her sleeve.

"Do not ask her what it means," said Gaara, without turning round. "She will tell you, and then I will have to leave the valley."
