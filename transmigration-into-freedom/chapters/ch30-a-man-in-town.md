# Chapter 30: A Man in Town

They took the metal down in four carts and it took them three days instead of two, because a cart is not a man and does not care what a man is in a hurry about.

"Twenty miles," said Gaara, on the second afternoon, to nobody who had asked. "On a road that was built properly by people who assumed somebody else would keep it up. I have never in my life felt closer to my old employer."

Marit had stayed. That had been settled in about four seconds and neither of them had enjoyed it, and she had put it in the flattest voice she owned.

"Somebody holds the valley."

"Somebody does."

"And it is not going to be the man who has to be in a town."

So it was Ylva, and Sivert, and Torv, and Vidar, and four carts, and eight hundred years of a dead city coming down a road at the pace of an ox.

Ordulf took his first, in his own yard, and weighed it in front of them because he weighed everything in front of everybody.

"This is more than a blade."

"It is."

"By some distance."

"Then take the rest as credit against the next thing I want," said Gaara, "which will be worse, and I will want it sooner than is reasonable, and I would like the account already open when I ask."

Ordulf grunted and wrote something on the back of his own door with a nail, which appeared to be his ledger.

Sivert carried the last of it in on his own because the yard gate was too narrow for the cart and because nobody was going to stop him.

"You have got him carrying," said Ordulf.

"I have got him doing a considerable number of things," said Gaara, "and every single one of them is an improvement on the alternative, which was a nineteen year old at the bottom of a drain being told he was too big to be quick."

Ylva made a noise. Ordulf did not, which by now Gaara recognised as the man laughing.

That was the first debt he had cleared in this world. He noticed the exact moment it happened and had to stand in a smith's yard doing nothing for a second. In the last world he had carried one thing for eleven years and never got to the bottom of it.

Runa was at the same stall with the same tray of horn and one of brass, and she watched four carts of iron come up the street and did not move the stall.

"You are going to ruin the price," she said, when he got to her.

"Say that again more slowly."

"There is one town, and it has three smiths in it and a shop that buys scrap for the weight, and you have brought them a year of stock in an afternoon." She turned a button over. "Whatever it is worth this morning, it is worth half of that by the time the fourth cart is unloaded. And the day after that, everybody in the Touch knows there is more of it up a hill, so it stays at half."

Gaara stood there.

"I am going to pay you for that."

"You are."

"And I want it on the record that this is the third time I have paid you to tell me a thing I should have got to on my own. I am keeping a running total, out of pure spite." He got the coin out. "What is the answer."

"Sell a quarter of it and put the rest in a locked shed, and tell three people you have a shed."

"And the price."

"Holds," said Runa. "Because a man with a shed is not desperate, and everybody in this town can smell desperate through a wall."

He looked at her for slightly too long.

"You are going to be a problem," he said.

"I have already told you that," said Runa. "You are just slow."

They sold a quarter of it and rented a shed off Frida, who wanted to talk about boards.

She had sold eleven of them. Two carters had argued about a load in her front room and gone and got the board off the counter without her suggesting it. She reported this as though it were a scandal, and Gaara made her say it twice.

"They fetched it themselves."

"They fetched it themselves."

"Frida," he said, "in nineteen years I never once got anybody to fetch a form. I had to have them printed on a different colour of paper and I still had to stand over people."

"I do not know what half of that means."

"Neither does anybody. It is the great comfort of my life."

It was Ylva who saw Ketil.

She saw him across the yard behind the Staple, where men waiting for work stand about in a way that pretends not to be standing about. She stopped in the middle of a sentence about brackets and did not finish it.

He had the good coat still. It had gone through at one elbow.

Gaara got out of the way and let it be hers. Then he stopped letting it be hers, because the two of them had stood in a yard saying nothing at all for a minute, and nobody was going to be helped by a fourth.

"I have imagined this for three weeks," said Ketil.

"Whatever you imagined, it had a great deal more shouting in it." Gaara came over and stood where he could be seen. "I can still do the shouting, if it would be useful. I am extremely good at it and I have never once meant a single word."

Ketil looked at him the way a man looks at a thing he has decided in advance to hate and cannot get started on.

"You know what I did."

"You took a horse and you rode east and you told a lord's man that a stranger opened a gate and four people are dead," said Gaara. "Yes. I know what you did, and I knew it the same night. I have had three weeks to work up something to say to you about it and I have got nothing at all. The thing that put you on that horse was me telling forty houses the truth in the road."

"That is not the same."

"It is exactly the same and it is the only reason I am not enjoying this." He sat down on a mounting block. "I refused to recruit men on half a story. You went east because you had the whole one. If I get to be proud of the first half, I do not get to be surprised by the second. Working that out took me most of a week and put me in a foul mood, and I would like it minuted."

Ketil's face had not moved.

"They will be gone," he said.

"What."

"Oldstep. I have been three weeks in a town waiting to hear it." His voice went somewhere unpleasant. "A lord's man came back from up there four days ago and I could not make myself ask him. I have stood in that yard every morning for three weeks and I could not make myself ask a man a question."

Gaara looked at Ylva. Ylva did not help him at all.

"Sit down," said Gaara.

"I will stand."

"Sit down, because I am going to talk for two minutes and I would rather you were not on your feet for it."

Ketil sat down.

Gaara told him what had happened. Not arranged, not softened, and not in the order that made it comfortable. The gate is shut. Bern lost a leg below the knee and is fed and working and his name is on the front of the thing they read out. Nine people go into the city and nine come back and it has now happened four times. Gunnar came, and looked, and wrote Oldstep onto a roll.

"On a roll."

"With the count of the houses and who speaks for it and what comes out of the hill."

"Hallvard would never."

"Hallvard stood in his own doorway and gave him the count of the houses himself," said Gaara. "In a voice with nothing in it, which is how he does everything."

Ketil put both hands over his face and left them there. Ylva looked at the wall of the Staple. Gaara looked at the middle distance and let a man have four minutes in a yard.

"Then it worked," said Ketil eventually, from behind his hands.

"It is on a roll and it will be taxed and it will be asked for men, and that is the price, and I got it as cheap as I could get it." Gaara stood up. "But yes. It worked, and the reason it worked is that a lord's man came to look at us instead of us going to look for him, and the reason he came is that you got on a horse."

"That is not why I got on the horse."

"No," said Gaara. "It is what came of it. Those are different and I would never insult a man by mixing them up."

Ylva spoke for the first time in a quarter of an hour.

"You did not come back."

"I could not."

"You could have come back and stood in the road and had it said to you." She had her arms folded. "Hallvard did. Hallvard did it about the gate, in front of everybody, in four minutes, and he expected to lose the eldership and said so first. You went east and then you stood in a yard for three weeks."

Ketil did not answer.

"That is the part," said Ylva. "Not the horse. Everybody in that village understood the horse by the second day."

Gaara said nothing at all, which he was getting slightly better at.

"I want him," he said, after a while.

Ylva turned round.

"You want what."

"I want a man in this town." He said it to both of them. "Hades has iron in a shed and a name in a book and a lord's officer somewhere east of here with a wax tablet, and I am twenty miles up a hill four days out of five. I need somebody standing in the Wold who knows what a warden looks like when he is about to be difficult, and who can get a message to Gunnar. Somebody who has already learned that yard by heart, because he could not think of anything else to do in it."

"He informed on us."

"He did. And he has been paying for it in a yard, and I do not need him forgiven." Gaara looked at Ketil. "I need him useful. Those are different too."

"He is my husband's brother," said Ylva.

"I know exactly whose brother he is, and so does everybody, and half of Oldstep is going to say I have gone soft in the head." He shrugged, and his shoulder had stopped complaining about it a week ago. "And the other half is going to notice that the man who informed on them now works for them and cannot afford to do it twice, and that is the half I would back."

Ylva stood there for a long moment.

Then she went and stood in front of Ketil, and did not soften her face at all.

"I am not forgiving you," she said.

"I know."

"I am putting you to work. You will do it properly, and you will do it for a year, and at the end of the year we will see where we are." She held out her hand, which Gaara had never once seen her do. "Half of that iron is mine. So half of you is mine, and I want the half that gets up early."

Ketil took her hand.

"You are hiring him," said Torv, later, in the street, in the tone of a man who has been thinking about something for an hour and has not improved it.

"I am."

"He told on us."

"He did, and he was the only man in that road who did the thing his conscience told him to, and his conscience was wrong, and mine has been wrong twice this month." Gaara did not slow down. "I am not going to run a company where the entry requirement is having been right so far. There would be nobody in it, starting with me."

Runa came to the room over Frida's stable that night, and there were no other people in it, because he had paid Frida for that in the afternoon and had not mentioned it to anybody.

"You did not ask me," she said, in the doorway.

"No."

"You told me where you sleep, a week and a half ago, and then you went away and killed something and came back with four carts, and you have not said one word about it since you got here."

"I do not ask twice." He was sitting on the end of the bed with his boots half off. "I said it once in a market. It was true then and it is true now and there is nothing I could add to it except pressure."

Runa shut the door.

"That," she said, "is the most calculated thing anybody has ever done to me."

"Yes."

"You are not even going to argue."

"I would be arguing about the word and not about the fact, and I have watched men do that and it is contemptible." He got the second boot off. "It is calculated. I worked out in about a second and a half that you were somebody who would not be pushed. So I built the only approach that has never once worked on anybody. And I have thought about you in a stone tunnel more than is decent."

She crossed the room and kissed him.

He put it back at the beginning without saying anything about it.

Closed lips, short, nothing else. Then a little more and not much. Then more again, and by the fourth one she was coming forward off her own weight to meet it, and he gave her fractionally less than she had just asked for, and did it again, and again.

She stopped and pulled her head back about six inches.

"You are giving me less than I just asked you for."

"Yes."

"Every time. Deliberately."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because you are going to ask again," said Gaara.

Runa looked at him in the dark with her hand still on the back of his neck. He watched a woman work out in four seconds what nobody in either world had ever caught him doing, and there had been nothing better in either of them.

"You do that to everybody."

"I do that to everybody."

"And nobody has ever said it out loud."

"Nobody has ever noticed," said Gaara, "and I have just found out what that is worth, and it is a very great deal more than I expected."

She got him out of the shirt with a good deal less ceremony than the conversation had suggested.

Later he gave her the four words, in the dark, without explaining any of them.

"That is a very short vocabulary."

"It is a complete one."

"It is four words."

"It is four words," said Gaara. "Everything you might want to say to me while my mouth is otherwise engaged is one of those four. Find me a fifth and I will pay you for it, the way I have paid you for everything else this week."

She did not find a fifth. She used the second one twice and the third one once, and then stopped using any of them, which was the part he was working for. She was not quiet about it and made no attempt to be. Somewhere below them a horse moved in its stall, and neither of them cared.

Afterwards she lay across him with her chin on her arms and looked at him with an expression he could not price at all.

"You will be gone in two days."

"I will."

"And you will not ask me to come with you."

"No," said Gaara. "That is not restraint. I have a village up that road with a hole in the hill in it." He put a hand flat on her back and left it there. "You have a stall, a workshop, and a man you were sensible enough to buy an opinion about before you paid him a wage. I have nothing to put next to any of that. When I do, I will say it once."

"Once."

"Once."

"You are going to be a problem," said Runa, for the third time, and put her head down.

In the morning there were four carts, one shed, and a quarter of a dead city sold at a price that had not moved. There was a debt to a smith that no longer existed. And there was a man in a good coat with a hole in the elbow, standing in the yard behind the Staple at first light, because nobody had told him what time to be there.

Gaara stood in the street and did the arithmetic he could not help doing, and for the first time since the ash it came out on the right side of the line.

Then he looked at the top of the panel, out of habit, the way a man checks a pocket he knows is empty.

"One of these days," he said.
