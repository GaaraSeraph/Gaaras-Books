# Chapter 14: Say It in the Road

He did it wrong first, and he did it wrong in the way he had done it wrong for nineteen years.

He got them into the road after the morning milking, forty houses' worth, and he stood on the step of the byre and gave them the facts in order.

The channel. The gate. The eighteen inches. The four hundred nests. The windlass under the floor they were standing near, the four men on the bars, the two on the wedges, the four hours in the dark.

He was clear. He was brief. He did not decorate any of it.

He put the whole thing in front of them like a proposal that had been checked twice, and he finished, and he waited, and forty houses looked at their own boots.

"Well?" said Gaara.

Somebody coughed.

"Is he finished?" a man said, further back.

"He is never finished," said Marit, from the byre wall. "He takes his time. And then he takes some more time. And then, when you have entirely given up on him, he gets there."

Somebody laughed. Several people did.

Gaara turned round slowly.

"Nobody has ever accused me of being quick," said Gaara. "They accuse me of other things."

"You will want a word about this later."

"I will want a word about this later."

"You will take your time over it," said Marit, "and then you will take some more."

It bought him about nine seconds of goodwill, and it was the only nine seconds he got, and he was aware of exactly who had earned them.

Then the heavy man with the boar spear said, "Why should we?"

"Because in the spring it will not be four."

"You say."

"I say," Gaara agreed. "And you have known me three days, and two of them I spent insulting you, and I am now asking you to stand in a hole in the dark for four hours on my arithmetic." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Put like that I would not come either. The pay is one loaf, four hours on a bar in the wet, and my continued company in a confined space. I have accepted worse offers than that, and I want you to know I regretted most of them."

Nobody moved.

He stood on the step and felt the thing arrive that he had felt in the road at three in the morning, with the smell coming up out of the north. The same thing. The same shape.

The information was right. It had always been right. It was worth nothing in his mouth.

It's always the audience.

"In my old life," he said, mostly to Marit, "I once failed to get eleven people to agree on the colour of a folder. I have no idea why I thought forty would queue up for a tunnel."

"Right," he said. "That is my fault and not yours. Give me an hour."

Hallvard was at his table with his hands flat on it, and he had clearly been listening to the whole thing through the open door, because he did not ask how it had gone.

Gaara shut the door and stayed standing.

"They will not come for me," he said.

"No."

"They will come for you."

"Some of them."

"All six." Gaara put his knuckles on the table. "You have had thirty-one years of being the man who is right about things. That is the only account in this village with anything in it, and I am here to ask you to spend it."

"You do not need to ask," said Hallvard. "I will stand in the road and tell them to go, and they will go."

"That is beside what I am asking for."

The old man's eyes came up.

"They cannot go down there not knowing," said Gaara. "Four hours in a tunnel, in the dark, on a windlass, with the mouth of the room forty feet away. If it goes wrong, it will go wrong in about nine seconds, and men who have been told the wrong thing will do the wrong thing in nine seconds. They have to know what is behind that gate. They have to know how long you have been going down there. And they have to know why it is open."

Hallvard sat very still.

"You want me to say it in the road."

"I want you to say it in the road."

"You could say it for me."

"I could not," said Gaara. "I have one asset in this village and it is that nothing I have said here has been untrue. I am not going to spend it telling your story for you and letting them hear it as a stranger's accusation. It has to come out of your mouth, in your voice, in front of the houses you have been counting for thirty-one years." He straightened up. "Also, I am not going to do it because it is yours. That is not kindness. It is bookkeeping."

The room was quiet for a long moment.

"They will take the elder off me," said Hallvard.

"Almost certainly. On the other hand you will get a full night's sleep for the first time in thirty-one years, and at your age that is not nothing."

"That is a very poor joke."

"It is the best one available. I looked."

"Aldis's father did this for eleven years and died of it with nobody knowing. Her uncle did nineteen. I have done thirty-one. There has never been a single year of it that anybody has known, and that was not vanity, it was the whole design."

"I understand the design," said Gaara. "The design has four graves in it now, and a fifth if you count the dogs together, and it has run out. Designs do that. They run for years and then a thing changes underneath them and they run for one more season on momentum and then they stop, and the only question left is who is standing next to them when they stop."

Hallvard looked at his own hands.

"You have done this before," he said. "Not the ruin. This."

"I have been in the room when a thing that worked for eleven years stopped working, yes," said Gaara. "I was usually the one holding it. Nobody ever told me either, until it was in the road."

The old man got up, slowly, the way he had got up in front of the whole village three nights ago.

He picked up the stick, and he did not lean on it.

"Come and hold the door," he said.

It took him about four minutes.

He stood in the middle of the paved road with his stick in his hand and said it in order, and he did not decorate any of it.

Gaara stood at the edge of the crowd and understood that he was watching a man do the most expensive thing he would ever do, in flat ordinary sentences.

Thirty-one years. Aldis before him. Her uncle before her. Nobody wrote it down, and that was deliberate, and here is why. And in the spring I raised the gate eighteen inches on my own arithmetic. I was right about the rate and I was wrong about the noticing. The boy who was fishing, and the man with the cart, and Teodor, and Osvald are on that.

He did not say sorry once.

Gaara noticed that, and approved of it, and knew exactly how few people in that road would.

It went the way those things go. There was silence, and then there was noise. A woman near the front said the same sentence four times in a row with her hands out. Somebody at the back shouted about the lord. Solva pushed through to the front and stopped dead when she got there and said nothing at all.

And in the middle of it, Bern said, "Who's going down?"

Which was the whole game, and Gaara moved.

"Six," he said. "Four on the bars, two on the wedges. Anyone who comes gets told everything before they set foot in the channel, and anybody who wants out can be out at the mouth and I will not think worse of him."

He was already reading the road while he talked.

He could not help it, and by now he was not trying to. Faces came up and gave him names and numbers, the way they had on the first afternoon, and he sorted them without deciding to.

The heavy man with the boar spear was a 5, which surprised him.

Bern was a 4.

A wide, slow man near the pens was a 6, which surprised him considerably more.

And a woman of about forty standing behind the smith's gate, who had said nothing the entire time and whose name he did not know, came up as a 7.

Seven. The highest thing he had seen in that village.

"You," he said.

She looked at him.

"I do not know your name," said Gaara, "and I am going to ask you first anyway, and I would rather not explain why in the road."

"Ylva," she said.

"Ylva. Will you come?"

The wide slow man near the pens said, "She's a woman."

"Yes," said Gaara. "I did notice. I have been noticing things all week. It is how I found your cellar."

Somebody at the back made a sound.

"She is what I want," said Gaara. "I said six men because that is what the old arrangement said and I repeated it without thinking. I have been wrong about numbers four times this week. I am not going to be wrong about this one out of politeness."

Ylva went on looking at him for a moment.

"My husband went down with Aldis," she said. "He came back the first time."

Nobody had told Gaara that. He filed it, and he did not use it, and he was aware that a worse man would have.

"Then you know what the bars are," he said. "Will you come?"

"Aye."

He got Bern. He got the wide slow man, whose name was Torv and who did not apologise for the woman remark and did come, which Gaara considered the more useful of the two options. He got a man of thirty called Ask who said nothing at all and just stepped forward. He got the heavy man with the boar spear, who came up close first and said, quietly, "I still think you have got a mouth on you."

"You are correct. It is the only part of me that has never been injured, and I have been working on that for nineteen years."

That was the entire negotiation.

Five.

He stood in the road for a while with the sun going and did not get a sixth.

"I will take the sixth bar," said Hallvard.

"You are seventy."

"I have taken that bar before."

"You have taken that bar with four men who are now dead of ordinary things," said Gaara, "and I need you at the wedges. The wedges are the part where knowing what you are doing matters more than what you can lift. And if that gate comes down wrong, I will take the loss on a man who can pull before I take it on the only man who has ever done this."

Hallvard almost smiled.

"You have got me at the wedges and Ylva on a bar," he said. "You are picking by something."

"Yes," said Gaara.

"Will you tell me by what?"

"No," said Gaara.

They found the sixth at full dark, and it was Solva, and it took Gaara ten minutes and both hands to talk her out of it.

"You are sixty," he said, "and I have watched you carry bread with two hands."

"He was my son."

"He was, and he is not down there, and the thing that is down there does not care whose son he was." He held on to the argument the way you hold a rope. "Solva. If you come and it goes wrong, I will spend the four hours watching you instead of the mouth of that room, and so will Marit, and so will Bern, and that is how six people become four."

Solva stood in her doorway with the cold lamp still on its stone beside the step.

"You will not lie to me," she said, and it came out flat, with no question in it.

"No."

"Then say it plain."

"You would slow us down and it might kill somebody," said Gaara. "That is plain."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she went inside and came back with a loaf wrapped in cloth, and put it in his hands, and shut the door.

Gaara stood in the dark holding bread, which was, on balance, the worst he had felt all week.

They got the sixth in the morning.

It was Halle's boy, nineteen years old, who had helped kill the small one in the road with an axe on the night Osvald died. He came up to Gaara at the trough before anyone else was awake and said he wanted the bar.

Gaara looked at him.

Level 2.

The lowest number he had taken. Half the weight of Torv. Nineteen, which was a number he had opinions about.

"Why," he said.

"Because I was there and I did it and I am not doing that again," said the boy, meaning something he did not have the words for and which Gaara understood immediately.

"Right," said Gaara. "You are on a bar next to Torv, you will do exactly what he does, and if I say the word you go up the channel and you do not stop and you do not look round."

"What's the word?"

"For the record."

Behind him, at the byre door, Marit made a noise into her sleeve.

"Do not ask her what it means," said Gaara, without turning round. "She will tell you, and then I will have to leave the valley."
