# Chapter 1: The first thing that arrives
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.5 · EN

## Day Five Hundred and Ninety-Two · Tuesday 18 May

For fourteen days there has been nothing to build.

He still gets up at the same hour and reads the same four papers, and he still goes through the shipping notices and the corporate filings the way he has since the March before last.

It has not been for anything for fourteen days. He has not missed a morning.

The card came to the front door at twenty past ten.

Mrs Seo took it in the hall and brought it up herself.

She has done that once before, on a Tuesday in May of the year before, about a telephone call. She said at the time that she had been answering that telephone for nine years and that nobody had ever asked for him.

Georgij put the pen down.

"There is a card," said Mrs Seo. "It has your name on the front of it and it has it correctly, and it does not have hers on it anywhere."

She did not come into the room.

"The man who brought it was not from a firm that carries things. A man from one of those has a book and a bag and four more to do before twelve, and he had none of it." She put the card down on the corner of the desk where things go. "He did not ask me who I was before he handed it over."

"He would have had to ask somebody that."

"He did not ask me," said Mrs Seo. "And he was not looking at the house the way a man looks at a house he has not seen before."

She went back down and did not say anything else about it.

The card is printed and it is not expensive, and one line of it is in ink.

It gives a name, with the character in front of it that only ever goes in front of a dead man's.

It gives Wednesday the twenty-sixth of May at eleven in the morning, a temple in Jongno, and a hall inside the temple. It says what the day is. The day is the forty-ninth.

The forty-ninth day is counted from the death and the death is the first of them. Choi Dae-ho died on the eighth of April.

It is a rite that a family holds. Somebody has to book the hall and pay for it and stand at the front of it. If there is no family, the person who does those things has taken the family's place.

It is the sort of card a stationer keeps a box of.

At the bottom, where a card of that sort says who is asking, there is one name.

There is no office under it and no address and no telephone number, and there does not need to be. That name has been in the newspapers of this country for two years, in the sort of paragraph that does not explain who the man is, because whoever is reading it is assumed to know.

The name is Gong Doo-hyun. He is the Prosecutor General.

He turned it over. There is nothing on the back at all.

Georgij read it twice and then went and got a notebook, and it was not the current one.

He keeps them. There are four now, and the first is nearly full of things that came to nothing.

He found the page for the twenty-seventh of May last year in under a minute, because every page in there has the date at the top of it and because he knew roughly where it would be.

*Choi Dae-ho. 59. Former prosecutor, out early and nobody says why.*

He wrote that in a garden in Hannam-dong with a glass in his other hand, because Park Sang-hoon had said it in that order and at that speed.

He has been back to that page a great many times since.

Never once for that line.

He put the notebook down on the card and left it there.

There is a man who says why.

He is going to be standing at the front of a hall in Jongno on Wednesday of next week.

And he has sent a card to a house that is on no list, addressed to a man whose name has no public connection to it. The name exists on paper in the Republic of Korea. Not one piece gives anybody this door.

He looked the temple up, because that costs nothing.

It has two halls. The large one is the one that gets photographed, and it is where a man in that office would be expected to hold anything at all.

The card gives the other one. It is behind the large one and it takes about two hundred, and two hundred is a number a man can go down with a pen.

Georgij looked at that for a while and then wrote one line and put the date on it.

*Everybody in that room will be somebody he wanted in it.*

He telephoned Baek Jun-ho at half past eleven and asked one question.

"The office in Jung-gu that forwards," he said. "When they told you he had died, say who told them."

There was a pause on the line of about three seconds.

"I did not ask." Baek said it straight out. "I have thought about that four times since. I had the girl on the telephone for two minutes and she would have told me."

"You had no reason to ask."

"I had every reason and I did not have the wit." He was not making it smaller. "Say what has happened."

Georgij told him what had come to the door and what was on it, and read him the name at the bottom, and did not say anything about what he thought it meant.

Baek Jun-ho did not answer for a while.

"I have been in this trade for thirty-two years," he said. "I have never once been on the same sheet of paper as that man and I never expected to be."

"You are not on it. I am."

"You are on it because he wrote you on it." Something had gone out of his voice. "I am going to say this badly. I wrote to Choi Dae-ho four times and every one of them went through that office, and the last came back marked in a clerk's hand. I have been quietly pleased with myself for writing to him since February."

"Yes."

"And now a man has taken the family's place, and the name at the bottom is his own, not an estate's and not a firm's. The office told me there was no address for anybody at all. I put that in a note on the twentieth of April, and I have read it since and not seen what it left unanswered."

Georgij did not telephone Park Sang-hoon.

Sang-hoon asked in three places in April and heard nothing back, and he does not usually ask anywhere. Going at it again now would tell him that something had arrived, and he would have the rest of it inside a day, and then two men would be carrying it instead of one.

There is a day for that, and this is not it.

* * *

The tray went up at seven the way it has gone up at seven for ten years.

He went down at about eight and told her all of it in order.

Neither of them had said anything about the Sunday and neither of them was going to, and it was in that room the whole time.

"Say the name again," said Annie.

"Gong Doo-hyun."

She put the pen down and left her hand on it.

"Twice in four years somebody who could have moved him has tried to move him, and he is still sitting in that office." Annie had not looked at the card. "One of the two was in this house in the spring of last year."

"I did not know that."

"You did not need it until this evening."

The clock in the hall did the thing it does at the quarter.

"Say what you think it is."

"Three things, and none of the three is written on it." Georgij did not sit down. "The first is that Choi Dae-ho is dead. That is the first time anybody outside this house has said so to us. Until this morning we had it from a girl on a telephone in April who did not know what she was saying."

Annie waited.

"The second is that a man took the family's place." He had his hands behind his back. "Baek was told there was no address for anybody at all. Somebody still booked a hall and put his own name at the bottom of a card, and that is not a thing you do for a man you have had dinner with."

"And the third."

"The third is that it is not an invitation. A man who wants me in a room does not need my name on the front of it. He needs the address, and he had it, and he has let me see that he had it."

The card was still on the corner of the desk where it had been since half past ten.

Annie did not answer for a while.

"There is a fourth and you have not said it," she said.

"No."

"Then I will say it." She had not moved in the chair. "You cannot refuse it. If you go, he gets an hour of looking at you. If you do not go, he gets the fact that you understood the card, and the second is worth more to him and costs him nothing."

"Yes."

"You had that at about half past ten this morning and you have carried it round the house all day, and you brought me the other three first because they are the ones I can do anything about."

She turned the card round on the desk with one finger so that it faced her, and read it, which she had not done until then.

Georgij stood where he was.

"May I go to it?"

"You are asking because of the thirtieth of April."

"I am asking because I do not know the price, and the part of it I do have belongs in this room."

Annie looked at him for a moment.

"I could tell you not to go. I have thought about it for as long as you have been standing there and I am not going to." She did not soften any of it. "A man who wants you in a room and cannot have you finds out what he is dealing with some other way, and I hear about that afterwards. I would rather it happened at eleven in the morning in a hall with two hundred people in it."

"There are going to be men in that hall who have read people for a living for thirty years," said Georgij. "I am going to walk into it wearing the one thing in this country that answers the question before anybody puts it."

"Yes."

"Better that he has it in the first four seconds than that he spends a fortnight getting it wrong."

The machine in the room at the back ran for four minutes and stopped.

Annie picked the pen back up, which is what she does when a thing has been decided.

"Go on the Wednesday," she said. "Come back here and tell me all of it in order, including the parts you have decided are not important."

"Yes, Mistress."

"And Georgij." She was writing again. "You are not to be the last man out of that hall."

* * *

On the eighth of February a letter went out of an office in Yeouido, and Georgij had said in that office what it was.

*There is nothing in the world with his name on it except what he signed himself. That letter is going to be the first thing that arrives at him.*

It went to a solicitor's office in Jung-gu that forwards. It was forwarded.

Choi Dae-ho was dead on the eighth of April.

The card is on the corner of the desk where Mrs Seo put it, and it has his own name on the front of it, in a hand he does not know.
