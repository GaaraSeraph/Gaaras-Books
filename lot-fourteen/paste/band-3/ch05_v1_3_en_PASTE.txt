Book Three · Chapter 5: Only hers
Lot Fourteen · Version 1.3 · EN

Day Six Hundred and Seventy-Two · Friday 6 August

Mr Hwang telephoned the house at twenty past seven in the evening, which he has done four times in eleven months, and all four were about a date.

"There is something out of order in my column," he said. "I do not know what it is and I would rather not say it on a telephone, because I would have to say it twice and the second time it would sound like an accusation."

"May I come tomorrow?"

"Come this evening if you are able. I am here until ten, and it is not going to get tidier overnight."

———

He went out at ten past eight with Jang driving.

The bridge is twelve minutes if there is nothing on it, and there is always something on it. Georgij did not say anything for the length of it.

The terminal lights come up on the right about four minutes before the exit. There are a great many of them, and every one is a crane or a stack or a gate.

The whole of it belongs to a man of seventy-nine who telephoned once in April, and asked one question, and rang off.

———

The terminal is on Yeongjong and the office is at the end of the shed.

It is one room with a window that faces the wrong way, a desk, a second desk that has never had anybody at it, and a wall of box files that were there before he came.

Mr Hwang has been in it since the fifteenth of March and he has not put one thing on the walls.

He keeps the books of a terminal that has one clerk and does not need two, and he does it in six hours, and after six he does Georgij's work in the same room.

There is a kettle on the second desk and one cup beside it.

He is sixty-seven. He comes at seven and goes at ten, six days a week, and Woo has never once asked him what he does after six.

———

He had four pages out and squared before Georgij was through the door.

"I want to say first what this is not," said Mr Hwang. "It is not a complaint and it is not a finding, and if I were still where I was I would not have written it down."

"Say what it is."

"It is untidy." He put one finger on the top page. "I have kept a column for thirty-one years and I know what it looks like when it is behaving, and this one has not been behaving since the second week of May."

Georgij did not sit down.

———

The berth allocations for a terminal are a list and a clock.

A ship is given a window. If the window moves, everything behind it moves, and the movement is recorded because the port authority requires it to be, and nobody ever reads it because a moved window is the most ordinary thing in the world.

Mr Hwang reads it, because it is his column.

"Between the second week of May and the end of July there were eighty-one arrivals against berths on this terminal," he said. "Nineteen of them moved by more than four hours."

"And."

"Fourteen of the nineteen were hers."

———

A berth window moves because somebody at a terminal decides it does.

A ship comes in late and the one behind it has to go somewhere. A crane is down for an hour. A pilot is on another job. A vessel with a reefer load goes first because a reefer load is perishable and everybody agrees that it should.

Every one of those is true on the day. Every one of them is somebody’s judgement, and a judgement is not a document.

Nineteen of them in a quarter is a busy summer.

Fourteen going the same way is not a thing anybody has to explain, because nobody is going to ask.

———

Georgij put both hands on the back of the chair he had not sat down in.

"Say what share of the eighty-one is hers."

"About a fifth." Mr Hwang did not look up from the page. "That is the part I have been sitting with since Tuesday. A fifth of the traffic and three quarters of the movement, and a quarter is long enough that it is not weather."

"It is not weather."

"No. I did not say it was."

He turned the second page round and did not push it across.

———

Georgij read it standing up, and it took him about ninety seconds, and then he read it again for a different reason.

The first time he was looking for the pattern, and it is there and Mr Hwang has already found it.

The second time he was looking at which fourteen.

Her cargo is not one thing. Some of it is feedstock with three weeks of slack in it, and some of it is a precursor that goes into a reactor on a Thursday and cannot go in on a Friday.

Eleven of the fourteen are the second sort.

———

There is a clause under every one of those contracts with a number in it.

The number is not there to be reasonable.

———

"Mr Hwang."

"Yes."

"How many of the nineteen went past eight hours."

"None." He said it without having to look. "The longest is seven and a half, and there are four at seven, and I noticed that before I noticed anything else and I could not tell you why."

"Because eight is the line."

Mr Hwang looked up then.

"Eight hours is where the form changes," said Georgij. "Under eight it is a note. Over eight it is a report with a name on it, and somebody has to sign it, and it goes in a file that stays."

"Yes."

"Nineteen times, and not once over."

———

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

Somewhere down the shed a machine that lifts things put something down.

———

"I would like to say the thing I did not want to say on the telephone," said Mr Hwang.

"Say it."

"Weather does not know where a threshold is." He put his hand flat on the page. "And weather does not know which of a woman's ships has a penalty on it and which one has three weeks."

Georgij did not answer that for a while.

"No," he said. "It does not."

———

The second one is worse than the first and both of them knew it in the same second.

To move the right fourteen out of eighty-one, a person has to know which fourteen matter.

That is not in a shipping schedule. It is in the supply contracts, and the supply contracts are between her company and her customers, and there are four people in that building who have read all of them.

Georgij did not say any of that out loud in a room with a window in it.

———

"What do you want me to do?" said Mr Hwang.

"Nothing that changes. Keep the column the way you have kept it since March." Georgij put the page back exactly where it had been. "If you start looking, it will be in the way you look, and somebody is reading this terminal as carefully as you are."

"That had not occurred to me."

"It occurred to me about forty seconds ago and it should have occurred to me on the road."

Mr Hwang squared the four pages and did not put them away.

"There is a copy of that column in this office and one at the authority and none anywhere else," he said. "The one here is in my hand and it goes in the drawer at the end of every week, and I am the only person who has opened that drawer since March."

"Keep doing that."

"I was going to."

———

At the door Georgij stopped.

"May I ask you something that is not about this?"

"You may."

"Do you like it here?"

Mr Hwang thought about it properly, which took him about four seconds.

"It is a small column and it is honest," he said. "I did thirty-one years of a large one and I do not know how much of that was honest. I am not going to find out now, because the house is gone and there is nobody left to ask."

He put the pen in the tray where it goes.

"This one I can get to the bottom of by half past four."

———

Georgij was on the bridge at half past nine.

He telephoned nobody and he wrote nothing down until the car was off it.

Then he wrote four lines, and the fourth one is a question, and it is the only one with a mark on the end.

Eighty-one. Nineteen. Fourteen. Eleven with a clause.

None over eight.

Not weather.

Who has read her contracts.

———

Annie was still at the desk at half past ten, which she is not on a Friday.

He told her all of it in order and it took eleven minutes.

She let the whole of it come out before she said anything, and then she said the thing he had been waiting eleven minutes for somebody else to say.

"Four people in that building have read all of them," said Annie. "I am one, Mr Chae is one, and the other two have been with me since before you were in this country."

"Yes."

"So it is not out of that building."

"No."

"Then say where else they are."

———

Georgij had it and had had it since the bridge.

"They are in the counterparty's copy," he said. "Every one of those contracts exists twice. The other half of it sits with whoever is on the other side, and there are nineteen of those, and not one of them has any reason to keep it quiet."

Annie put the pen down.

"That is a much bigger room than the one I was standing in."

"It is. And it is the first thing all evening that is good news."

"Say why it is good news."

"Because a man who read one contract could be anybody." Georgij did not move. "A man who read enough of them to pick eleven out of eighty-one has been at this for months, and he has been at it in a way that leaves a shape." He did not move. "I have spent two years reading shapes."

———

Annie looked at the window for a moment, which she does perhaps once a month.

"There is a reactor at Yeosu that has been cold twice since June," she said. "I was told both times that it was a valve."

"Was it a valve?"

"It was a valve both times." She said it flatly. "A valve fails when a plant runs hot because a charge came in late and somebody made the time up."

Georgij did not say anything.

"I have been paying for that twice a month since May and calling it maintenance," said Annie. "Go on."
