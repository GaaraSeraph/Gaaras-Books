Chapter 17: I have never put it down
Lot Fourteen · Version 12.5 · EN

Day 46 · Tuesday 18 November

He gave her the evening on the expressway, in the order he had built during the ten minutes.

Sang-hoon had had him checked before he came through the door. He had said so at twelve minutes and had named the two men who told him. One was Hong. The other he described instead of naming: a man from Land and Infrastructure who talked to his people about other things.

"Kang," said Annie.

"Yes."

The car went under a bridge. The lights crossed her face and left it.

"Then everything you gave him on the telephone was in Yeouido inside a week."

"I have to assume it was."

* * *

"He kept the report. I get it back on Thursday." Georgij looked out at the expressway going past. "He read the first page, then the second, then the first again, and nothing happened to his face.

"Twenty minutes later we were talking about the ceiling at the Grand Hotel. He asked me about slot fees on a lane that is on page seven, and then whether a board that has stopped meeting can still authorise a disposal, which is page fourteen. He did not look at the pages for either of them."

"He has bitten."

"Yes."

Georgij was smiling. He knew he was doing it and he did not take it off.

He owned a great many smiles and could have told you what every one of them was for.

The wide one that goes all the way up and arrives a quarter of a second late, which is what makes people believe it, built in a house in Daejeon when he was twenty-one. The one held precisely as warm as the one it answers and not a degree warmer. The one you let go out for a second, which is rarer than any smile and worth more. The warm and entirely pleasant one he had put on a woman in the back of her own car on the way out of Gangnam, about forty minutes after she bought him. And the one you keep off your face on purpose, in front of somebody who is still deciding what you are.

Every one of them curated for a situation, aimed at a face, pointed at a result. He could have named the house each came out of and roughly what it had cost to learn.

Two of them in seventeen years had been his own. One had gone to Mr Hong at the gala, for a third name that had cost him something to say.

This was the other one, and it was the second time in twelve days. Over her desk it had come up slowly, from somewhere behind the courtesy, and had taken its time.

It did not take its time now.

"He read it twice because it is good work. There is not one untrue word in it anywhere." The smile did not go anywhere. "He has taken a bite out of the apple, and he is carrying the rest of it around in his coat until Thursday. He will spend the first month telling people he found it himself."

"When does he move?"

"Four to six weeks. Half that board wants out badly enough to sign anything, and will sign cheaply, and that is the half he will sit down with."

"And then."

"Then he is bound. He will have paid for four routes and bought a company that cannot authorise anything afterwards, including selling him the rest of it." He sat back. "He asked me about a disposal by a board that has stopped meeting. He is already standing in front of that wall. He will go over it anyway, because he has decided he wants what is behind it."

* * *

"There is a third thing and he gave it to me without knowing he had it in his hand."

Annie turned her head.

"He told me how long it takes to turn a family. Two years. Eighteen months if somebody frightens them for him." Georgij let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. "And then he said that fear is the cheapest thing on the market, because it is always available, and because it never has to be bought from the person who supplies it."

She was quiet for the length of two lamp posts.

"Say the guess."

"Sang-hoon put the container into that shipment, and he did not do it to slow them down. Customs have been in that building a month and they have not stopped one lorry leaving that yard.

"What a month of customs does is take the customers off them, and put a man in uniform in the corridor every morning where the whole family can see him. That is not a delay. That is the frightening, and the Republic of Korea is doing it for him, on its own time, and he has not paid anybody a won for it."

A sign went over the car and the light came down through it and went away.

"You already know there is no majority for him to buy, and you know Ye-rin has held those six for twenty years. What she cannot do is hold them through a second quarter of this. There is a Tuesday morning in February when a nephew with four per cent telephones her and says he is not waiting, and she has nothing to tell him that is better than what he is afraid of."

"Then it is February," said Annie. "Not March and not the spring."

"February is when we lose her. It is not when we move." Georgij looked at the back of Mr Pyo's head. "He signs for the routes in December. For the quarter after that he is short of money and can do nothing about it, and she holds through January. Those two things are true at the same time for about four weeks."

"January," said Annie.

"That is what I would work to."

She looked at him for rather longer than she usually did.

"You have been enjoying yourself since Yeouido."

Georgij did not answer straight away.

"Yes," he said.

"Be pleased with it in this car and nowhere else. You got it because a man was showing off to somebody he had decided was nobody." She turned back to the window. "If you look pleased in a room, he stops showing off."

* * *

"The last one is not from tonight," said Georgij. "Yesterday afternoon I pulled the second section on Hana's sixteen thousand square metres. It is on your desk. She told me to pull it, at your table, in front of you."

"Say what is in it."

"Three charges. Together they come to a little over two thirds of what that land is worth at today's designation. Two are old and dull. The third was registered in March, three months after the designation was published, and it is against the uplift."

"So she is not rich," said Annie. "She is leveraged."

"On the exact thing she is asking us to protect, which is the best reason we have to believe every word she said that evening. She cannot afford for the designation to move and she cannot afford for this to fail, and nobody can outbid not being ruined."

"And the other side of it."

"Then it is a question of who holds the paper." He said it flatly. "A charge is an asset. It is bought and it is sold. A regional bank in Incheon will let a position go at a discount to anybody who telephones in a bad quarter, and nobody there will ask what he wants it for.

"Somebody buys three charges over a couple of months, and a letter comes in the spring, and the person who can call in two thirds of her land is somebody she has never met. Buying positions in things is what a private equity company in Hong Kong exists for."

"Has anybody started?"

"Not as of yesterday. Not one of the three has moved." He turned his hand over. "I am telling you the shape of a door. I am not telling you that anybody is standing in it."

"I will tell her myself," said Annie. "By telephone, after Thursday, and not in writing anywhere."

"Yes, Mistress."

* * *

That was all of it.

He had come out of that building with four things and he had had them in order before the ten minutes were up. A man who works the way he works does not open his mouth on anything he has not laid out first.

So he stopped, and did not start again, and let the road have it.

He was aware, somewhere underneath that, that he had been sitting with one hand flat on the seat between them since Yeouido, and that he had not once measured the distance to anything.

Beside him, Annie put her hand on the door.

There was a small motor noise from the front of the car, and the panel behind Mr Pyo's head came up, and the sound of the road changed, and three people became two.

She took her hand off the switch and put it back in her lap.

She had never once touched it. Not on any drive he had taken with her, and not on the night of the gala, when he had given her fourteen minutes of a ballroom on this same road.

* * *

"Mistress. About the quid pro quo."

"Go on," said Annie.

"Eleven houses. Seventeen years."

There was nothing in his voice, at any point in it.

"In every one of them, the first thing I did when I came through the door was find the place where it would hurt. Not the locks and not the exits, those are reflexes. The place. I found it every time, and I used it whenever I needed it, and in some of those houses I needed it a great deal."

A pause.

"I have not looked once since I came here."

And then, before she could say anything:

"I did not decide to stop. It stopped being the first thing I did in a room and I did not notice it stopping. I have been in your house six weeks and I found out about it tonight, in this car, on the way home from doing it to somebody else."

"And now?"

"I enjoy this."

He had meant to stop there.

"The first time I called you Mistress it was a courtesy with an edge on it. It was not true."

He looked straight ahead at the back of the panel.

Beneath the linen at his throat there was a band of steel, and it had been there since the second house. It had made the word accurate from the first day, and it had never once needed him to mean it.

"It has been true for some time. I did not notice that either."

His hand stayed where it was.

"I stopped looking and I started meaning it. Neither of them was my doing."

He turned his head. She was already looking at him, and he understood that she had been for some time.

"They were yours."

* * *

She did not answer.

The cypresses started, and the headlights went along them and came back, and the car took the bend at the speed of a car that has been up this drive four thousand times.

Nobody said anything else at all.

* * *

The car stopped. Mr Pyo came round and opened her door and did not look at anybody, which was correct.

Georgij stood on the gravel for a moment and worked out what he had not done.

He had not reached for the switch.

He had never reached for it because he had never once had anything to put behind it. Everything he had ever said in a car had been work, including the night of the gala, when he had told her that he did not know where his people came from. That had been work as well. It had been a report on a mistake, and mistakes belong to whoever paid for them.

He had nothing of his own to keep, so he had never once had to clear a room for himself.

Somebody else had heard the difference before he did.

* * *

The hall light was on and Mrs Seo had gone to bed. The stone shelf was where it always was and the shallow bowl was on it and there was nothing beside the bowl.

Annie went up the stairs without saying anything, and he went up after her, because she had not sent him anywhere else.

On the first floor the corridor ran east to west and crossed the one that went back into the north side of the house, and there was a chair at the crossing.

She stopped there and did not go west.

* * *

"You told me a thing in that car," she said. "I am going to tell you one, and then I am going to bed."

"Yes, Mistress."

"There are three angles on this floor that no lens reaches. I put all three of them on a drawing myself and I argued with a professional for three days to keep them." She was looking at the chair and not at him. "I sit in that one. Most nights, for an hour, with my shoes off."

Georgij did not say anything.

"There are fourteen men on this estate whose whole job is to know where everybody is. Not one of them comes through my front door after ten, because I said years ago that I would not live in a bank." Nothing moved in her face anywhere. "The cameras do not go to bed. That is the only square metre of my own house where nobody can watch me sit, and I have never once gone a week without needing it."

She looked away from the chair.

"Eleven houses. In every one of them you found a weapon and you used it."

A pause.

"In this one you put it down and did not notice you had put it down."

Annie put two fingers on the back of the chair and left them there.

"I have never put it down. Not one night." Her hand stayed where it was. "And this is my house."

Georgij did not move from where he was standing.

"On my fourth day I gave Mr Jang nine sheets with every weakness in this building written on them," he said. "Every angle in the house except three."

Annie said nothing.

"I told him I was holding three back. I told him they were yours and that he could ask you for them. He has never asked."

"I know," said Annie. "He came to me with it in his first week."

Then she picked nothing up and moved nothing and went west along the corridor, and the door at the far end closed.

* * *

Georgij stood at the crossing in the dark.

He had counted the metre and a half of dead floor under every camera in this house on his first night, and had been rather pleased with himself about it, and had gone to bed.

She had just handed him the place where it would hurt. In her own house, out loud, half an hour after he had told her he no longer went looking for it. She had known exactly what she was handing over, because she had drawn the angle it stood in.

And she had known since October what he had left off Jang's paper. She had carried it for six weeks without a word, and had chosen tonight to say so.

She had not answered what he said in the car. She had put something of the same weight beside it and left it there.

He went along the east corridor to his own room.

He did not turn round and look at the chair.
