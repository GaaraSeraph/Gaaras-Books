Book Two · Chapter 13: What it costs him
Lot Fourteen · Version 1.5 · EN

Day One Hundred and Eighty-Eight · Thursday 9 April

Jang came on the Wednesday, two days early, with a folder and no heading written on anything in it.

He does not put headings on things. He laid it out on the table in the small room in the order he wanted it read, and he stood while Georgij read it, and he did not talk over any of it.

Six firms.

One of them has three trucks and a son at a university in Australia and seven months left on a loan he took against the yard. He is not afraid of losing the lane. He is afraid of a term beginning in September.

One has nine trucks, no debt at all, and a partner who has wanted out since his wife died and is being held in by nothing except that there is nobody to sell to.

One is two brothers who do not speak to each other and have not since 2019, and both of them talk to the younger one's wife, who does the invoicing.

One has been quietly moving work off that lane since February and has not told the association, which under the constitution he is not obliged to do and under twenty years of habit certainly is.

One is a man of forty who took the firm over from his father in the autumn. He has spent six months finding out what his father was actually like, and he has not said one word at a meeting since he arrived.

And one is Nam Byung-hee, who has four trucks and a nephew.

The last sheet was the one Georgij had asked for and it had five names on it in a column.

Who each of them would follow into a room. Not who they like. Who they would look at first if somebody in that room asked a question nobody wanted to answer.

Four of the five names were the same name, and it was the man with nine trucks and no debt, and it was not Nam Byung-hee.

"That is better than I asked for," said Georgij.

"You asked for what they are afraid of, which is the hard half, and then you asked who they would follow, which is the half people get wrong." Jang did not sit down. "They are not the same thing and I have watched men treat them as if they were, in four houses, over thirty years."

"There is one more," he said, "and you did not ask for it and I am giving it to you because it will save you a week."

"Go on."

"None of the six has met your employer or anybody who works for her. Not at a function, not at a port, not once." Jang put his hands behind his back. "There is no photograph of any of them next to anybody out of that house. I had that checked twice, because if I am wrong about it you will find out at the worst possible moment."

"And the man outside the yard."

"Since Monday. Nothing yet. He has a reason to be on that road and he is being paid by somebody in Busan who thinks he is doing something else, which is the arrangement I would want if it were me."

———

The terminal at Yeongjong is ten months old and looks it, in the way a thing looks new when the man who built it will not have anything on it repainted before it has earned the paint.

Chairman Woo came out to the car himself, which he did in November as well.

"The twentieth of November," he said. "That is the last time you stood on this ground. You telephoned in December and you did not come. Now come in and look at the second berth, they finished it in February and nobody has been out to see it."

They looked at the second berth for twenty minutes and Georgij asked four questions about it, and all four were real.

That is the part he could not have faked and did not have to.

"Now," said Woo, in the office at the end of the shed with the sea on three sides of it, "you want something. You have wanted something since you got out of the car and you have been extremely well-mannered about it for a quarter of an hour."

"Yes."

"Say it."

"There is an association of six independent shippers on one lane out of Ulsan," said Georgij. "Five of them are going to need work from the middle of this month and they are going to need it from somebody who does not care what the sixth one thinks. I would like it to be you."

Woo did not answer for a moment.

"Coastal feeder," he said. "Ulsan up to Incheon and back."

"Yes."

"About a fifth of my southeast leg goes through a broker in Busan who takes twelve per cent for arranging what he does not own. I have been irritated about him since 2021 and have never done anything, because the alternative was six telephone calls a week." He put one hand flat on the desk. "You are telling me the six telephone calls are about to be one."

"Yes."

"And the rate."

"At the floor, for the first quarter. They have had nothing since January and they have three months of it behind them."

Woo sat back and looked at him.

"You have not asked me for anything," he said.

"Chairman."

"That is not a complaint, it is a description. You have brought me a firm's worth of capacity on a leg where I am being fleeced, at the bottom of the market. You have arranged it so that I am better off on the day I say yes." He said it without any warmth in it at all, which is unusual for him. "You could have come here and asked me for a favour. I would have given it to you and I would have been pleased to."

"I know."

"You are not going to let me pay you back."

There it was, and there is only one thing a man can do with that if he has never said anything untrue.

"No," said Georgij.

Woo did not say anything for a while.

"Some men would call that a very cold way to treat somebody who likes them."

"Yes."

"Are you going to tell me it is not."

"No."

Georgij did not move from where he was standing.

"You are the only man in this country who has ever talked to me for three hours without wanting anything at the end of it," he said. "I am not going to spend that on a lane. If I ever have to spend it, it will be on something worth what it is worth, and I would sooner come out here four more times and never spend it at all."

"That is either the most honest thing anybody has said to me this year or it is the most efficient," said Woo, "and I have been in this trade since I was twenty-seven and I cannot tell which, and that is a very rare condition for me."

"It is both. They are not in competition."

"Now tell me what it is for," said Woo, "because five firms do not need work in the middle of April unless somebody has decided that the sixth one is not going to have any."

Georgij told him.

He did not arrange it and he did not put it in an order designed to land. A woman on that lane wrote to the house he works in, in March, on association letterhead, signed as chair, about a private matter of her own that she had no business touching. He is taking her firm, her chairmanship and her standing, in that order, over the next three weeks, and it will be done by her own members and they will be right to do it.

He used the word finished. He did not use any of the softer ones and there are four or five available.

Woo listened to the whole of it without moving.

"And her people."

"Forty-one of them. Forty of them will be working for the other five inside a fortnight, and better paid than they were in December. I am not telling you that to make it sound better. It is the mechanism." Georgij came to the last one. "The forty-first is her nephew and he will be placed by name, at the nine-truck firm, and I will know it has happened because I will ask."

"And her."

"Her nothing."

The sea did something against the piles under the second berth and neither of them looked at it.

"I am seventy-eight," said Woo. "I have finished four men in my life and I remember the names of all four and two of them deserved it. So do not stand in my office looking as though you expect me to be shocked, because it is discourteous and it is also wrong."

"I did not expect you to be shocked. I expected you to want to hear it from me and not in October from somebody else."

"Yes." Woo pulled a pad towards him. "Seven years."

"Chairman?"

"Seven years, the same as the anchor cargo, the same paper, and I will have it drawn by the same man. If I am going to put my name on that lane I am not going to do it for one quarter and then find out in August that somebody else has bought them with a better rate." He was writing while he said it. "You came here to ask me for something you thought I owed you. What you are getting instead is a customer, and I would like that on the record, because I know exactly what you are doing and I am going to let you do it."

Georgij did not say anything for a moment.

"Then you still have the three hours," he said.

"I still have the three hours. I am seventy-eight and I would rather be owed than paid, and now we are two men who both think that, and one of us learned it from the other in November."

At the car, at the end, Woo did the thing he did in October, which is to give away something nobody has asked him for.

"Somebody was asking about that lane in January," he said.

Georgij stopped with his hand on the door.

"At a lunch. Six of us, one of those things the port authority does in the new year." Woo was frowning at a bollard. "A man asked me whether I had ever used the Ulsan feeder and what I thought of the woman who runs the association there. He asked it the way you ask about a house you are thinking of buying."

"What did you say."

"I said she writes to me every month and I have never once needed her, and that she is honest and that the lane is too small. He was extremely interested in the word honest and I did not think anything of it at the time."

"His name."

"I do not have it and I did not ask for it, because he was not asking me for anything and there was no reason to." Woo was still frowning. "That is January. Nothing had happened on that lane yet. It was still moving in January."

Georgij stood at the car door for slightly too long.

"Chairman."

"Yes."

"Who else was at the lunch."

Woo told him, and it took about fifteen seconds, and there were five names in it.

He got in the car with all five of them and did not write any of them down until he was past the bridge.

He worked the other thing out somewhere on the bridge and then sat still with it for about four minutes.

The clause was signed in December. It began to be used in January.

In January there was nothing wrong on that lane. Nobody on it had been hurt, nobody had complained, and there was nothing whatever about that woman to interest a man at a port authority lunch.

So he was not looking for somebody who had been hurt.

He was looking for the person who was about to be. And to do that in the first week of the year he had to know what had been signed in a room in Seoul in December, and he had to know it inside a fortnight of the signing.

Georgij put the notebook away at Yeongjong and did not take it out again in the car.

That is a very short list as well.
