Book Two · Chapter 53: The one I did not say in May
Lot Fourteen · Version 1.4 · EN

Day Three Hundred and Five · Tuesday 4 August

On the seventeenth of June he had written down that Mr Yeom would be told. Not that week, and not by a man with something in his coat, and not in the same month if he could help it.

He had helped it. It is August.

And the thing that had made the whole of it impossible in June has gone, because there is nothing left in that house to ask for. He has the name. He got it from a woman with a book in pencil and a card with four lines on it and a small room at the back of a restaurant, and none of it came out of Mr Yeom.

I can go and give him something and want nothing, for the first time since I met him. That is the only reason today is possible, and it is not a virtue, it is a calendar.

———

Mr Yeom was in the same room as in May, with the same table, and he did not look surprised at all.

"I wondered whether you would come back," he said. "I have been wondering it since about the end of May, and then in July I stopped, and here you are in August."

"I am sorry about the gap."

"Do not be. A man who comes back in a fortnight wants something." He was already reaching for the dishes. "Sit down. You will eat, because I am not going to sit here with a plate in front of me and a foreigner watching me use it."

Georgij sat down, and ate, and it was very good, and he let ten minutes go before he started, because a man of sixty-three who has made lunch is owed ten minutes.

"I have come to tell you three things," said Georgij, "and to ask you for nothing at all, and I am going to say that at the start rather than at the end, because at the end it sounds like a technique."

"Then say the first one."

"The telephone call four years ago. The man who told you it was good to see you again, and you had never met him."

Mr Yeom put down what he was holding.

"You have found him."

"I have met him. On Saturday, for two hours, in a room he took for the whole evening." Georgij kept both hands on the table. "That is not the thing I came to tell you. The thing I came to tell you is that the telephone call was not strange."

"Say what that means."

"You have carried it for four years as a peculiar minute in a working life. A man rang, said a warm thing that made no sense, and asked you for a favour on behalf of somebody who would be grateful." He did not hurry it. "It is a method and nothing else. It has been used on a florist in Hyoja-dong, on a woman who has kept a restaurant book in pencil for twenty-two years, on a solicitor in Yeouido eleven years ago, and on you. And out of the four of you, you are the one it was used on least."

The room did what rooms do.

"That is not what I expected you to say," said Mr Yeom.

"No. It is worse than the thing you were braced for, and you should have it in the size it actually is."

"Say why it is worse."

"Because it means you were not asked a favour by a stranger who happened to have your number." Georgij looked at him properly. "You were chosen, and worked at for one minute by a man who is extremely good at one minute. He needed only the one, because you do favours for people who ask you nicely. He knew that before he dialled."

Mr Yeom sat with both hands flat on either side of his plate for a while.

"I have told myself for four years that I did a stupid thing on a Tuesday," he said. "You are telling me that somebody had a look at me first and decided a Tuesday would do it."

"Yes."

He had known on the way over that this was what he was bringing. A man is owed the real size of it.

"Then say the second thing, and do not stop to be careful with me, because I am sixty-three and I would rather have it in one go."

"The name you would not give me in May," said Georgij. "I have it."

Mr Yeom did not move at all.

"From where."

"From a woman who kept a booking book, six weeks ago, and she gave it to me because I described the man before I asked and everything I said was right." Georgij did not soften any of it. "She has not slept properly since. She telephoned him the next morning and told him what she had done, and he thanked her, and he asked her about her knee."

"Then it came out anyway."

"It came out anyway."

Mr Yeom looked at the table for a long moment.

"I have kept that name for four years out of a thing I have had since I was a young man," he said. "I have worked for seven people in my life and I have never handed one of them to anybody. I said that to you in this room in May and I was rather pleased with myself about it."

"I know. I remember exactly how you said it."

"And it turns out it did not hold anything up at all." He said it without any self-pity, which made it worse to sit opposite. "The thing went out through a woman with a book. My silence was not a wall. It was a decoration on a house with no door in it."

Georgij took a moment, because this was the one he had thought about most on the way over.

"No," he said.

"Say no properly."

"It held for four years, and it did not hold today, and those are two separate facts and only one of them is about you." He kept his voice exactly where it had been all lunch. "Mrs Bae had a telephone number because he books rooms. You had a name because a man once trusted you enough to give you one. She gave up a booking. You would have had to give up the only thing you have never done in forty years. Those are not the same size and I am not going to sit at your table and pretend they are."

Mr Yeom did not answer that, and then he pushed his plate an inch away from himself, which he had not done in May.

"Then let me do it now," he said. "It is worth nothing now. Ask me and I will say it, and it will cost me the same as it would have cost me in May, and at least it will have been for something."

"No."

"You are refusing a name you already have. That is not generosity, it is arithmetic."

"It is both, and I am going to say the arithmetic first so that you do not have to trust the other part." Georgij put one hand flat. "If you say it to me today, then in five years you are a man who broke it. That is what you will remember, and you will not remember that I already had it, because nobody remembers the part that lets them off."

Mr Yeom looked at him.

"And the other part."

"The other part is that it is the last thing in this business that belongs to anybody," said Georgij, "and I have spent five weeks taking things off people who did not know they were holding them, and I would like there to be one I did not take."

"There was a third thing," said Mr Yeom, after a while. "You said three."

"There is a third thing and it is the one I have been not saying since May."

"Then say it."

Georgij put both hands in his lap, which is not a thing he does.

He had never given anybody a date about himself. He was about to give one away for nothing.

"In May I told you that I work for the woman who owns the two entries. October and February." He said it evenly and did not decorate it. "That was true and it was chosen, and I chose it in your hallway before you opened the door, and I have thought about it every day since."

Mr Yeom went very still.

"Say the rest."

"I am the February one."

The room was quiet in the way it had not been all afternoon.

Mr Yeom did not say anything for a long time, and when he did his voice was not what it had been.

"You sat at this table in May," he said, "and let me tell you about a fee in an envelope."

"I did."

"And you did not say."

"I did not say, and I am not going to tell you it was tactics, because it was not." Georgij made himself finish it. "I do not give anybody dates about myself. It is the one rule I have had since I was nineteen and it has kept me alive in eleven houses. And in May I used it in your dining room on a man who was giving me the truth for nothing, and I have known since about the Thursday afterward that I owed you this sentence."

Mr Yeom stood up.

He went to the window, and stood there, and did not turn round, and Georgij let him have it.

"My father's house," he said, to the glass. "He ran it for thirty years and I have run it since the year he died, and I never once went down to the floor. I have said that in this room as though it were modesty."

"I know."

"And in October of last year a man went out of my building." He still had not turned round. "And in February my register was corrected for money by a man I had trusted for twenty-six years. I found out about one of the four and let it go in ten minutes, and the one I found out about was not you."

"No."

"Then which one did I hear about."

"The boy," said Georgij. "Lot eleven, in October. Byun came to you in February about that one because it frightened him, and it frightened him because of what the buyer wanted. Mine went through in the same seven weeks and he did not mention it, because mine did not frighten anybody."

Mr Yeom put one hand on the window frame.

"That is the worst sentence anybody has said in this house," he said, "and you said it as though it were an ordinary piece of information."

"It is an ordinary piece of information," said Georgij. "That is the whole of what I am trying to tell you about that building."

He came back to the table eventually and sat down and did not touch the food again.

"I am not going to apologise to you," he said. "You would not know what to do with it and I would not know how to make it the right size."

It was an apology with the word left out of it, and it was the largest one the man had.

"No. Thank you."

"So I am going to do the other thing." Mr Yeom put both hands on the table. "I took a position with the people who bought that house in December. It is not a large one and it is not honest work in the sense that it requires me to do any. I sit in two meetings a year and I have a folder sent to me every quarter and I have never once opened one past the first page."

Georgij did not say anything at all.

"I did not ask why they wanted the house," said Mr Yeom. "I have told you that already and I was comfortable about it for four years and I have not been comfortable since May. And you are not going to ask me for it, because you have made a great performance this afternoon of not asking me for anything, and I have watched you do it and I am not a fool."

"I am not going to ask you for it."

"I know. So I am offering it, and you are going to take it, and the difference between those two things is the only thing I have got left that is worth anything to anybody." He said it flatly and completely. "There are four years of quarterly folders in a cupboard in this house and I have read the first page of each one. I am going to read the rest of them, and I am going to tell you what is in them, and I am not doing it for you."

"For whom."

Mr Yeom looked at him.

"For the one who went out with no invoice," he said, "because he did not go out of a building. He went out of mine."

At the door, Georgij stopped, and it was Mr Yeom who spoke first.

"You will not come back either," he said. "Somebody has already told you not to, this month, and you agreed with her."

"A woman with a book in pencil."

"Then she is right and I am not going to improve on her." He had one hand on the frame. "But I am going to say a thing to you, and you are going to have to take it standing in a doorway, because you have taken everything else that way today."

"Say it."

"You were in this house in May and you had a rule and you kept it, and today you came back and broke it on purpose in front of a man who could do nothing for you." Mr Yeom did not look away. "I have been in this trade my whole life. I have never once seen anybody give away the thing that keeps them safe when there was no bill to pay with it."

Georgij stood in the doorway of a house in the suburbs and found he had nothing arranged.

"There was a bill," he said.

"Yes," said Mr Yeom. "I know. I only wanted to hear whether you would say so."
