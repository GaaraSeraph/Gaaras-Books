Book Two · Chapter 47: The one he kept
Lot Fourteen · Version 2.7 · EN

Day Two Hundred and Ninety-Eight · Tuesday 28 July

Sang-hoon sent for him at ten in the morning, to his own office, which he has not done once in eight months.

The room is on the fourth floor and it is not the room a man of that size would be given by anybody who did not know him. It is not large. There is a window that faces a yard rather than a river, a desk that has been used, and two chairs on the visitor's side rather than one. Georgij noticed the second chair on the way in and understood it four seconds later.

The second chair had a man in it.

He was about sixty, in a suit that had been good eleven years ago and had been looked after since. He stood up when Georgij came in, which nobody in this country does for a man who arrives on foot.

"This is Baek Jun-ho," said Sang-hoon.

Georgij did not sit down.

He stood inside the door of a room on the fourth floor and did the arithmetic in front of both of them, and did not pretend he was doing anything else.

"You did not tell me," he said.

"I did not."

"Then before anything else happens in this room, I am going to say the thing out loud, because if I say it afterward it is a complaint and if I say it now it is a fact." Georgij kept his hands at his sides. "There are four people alive who could describe my face in connection with any of this. As of nine seconds ago there are five, and I did not choose the fifth, and the fifth administers that man's money."

Baek Jun-ho did not move at all.

"That is fair," he said, "and I would like to answer it, because it is about me and not about him."

"Then all of it."

"I have known since yesterday that this was going to happen and I have not told anybody, and I have had a night in which to." He said it evenly, in the voice of a man who has spent thirty years saying things in rooms. "I am aware that is not proof of anything. It is what I have."

Georgij looked at him for a moment.

Then he took the second chair.

Sang-hoon did it properly, which is to say he did not perform any of it.

"I went to him on Monday," he said. "In person, to his office, on my own, without an appointment, and I asked him one question and then I stopped talking, and I would like both of you to notice that I did not send anybody."

"The question," said Georgij.

"I asked him whether there was anything he had signed in eleven years that he had never been able to account for."

The room went quiet in a particular way.

"That is my question," said Georgij.

"It is your question. I have been sitting in it since the twenty-seventh of May and I have watched you use it twice, and I decided that a man of my age is allowed to learn something." Sang-hoon did not look pleased with himself. "It was Monday. It was my birthday. I did not want to spend the whole of it being sixty in a restaurant."

Neither of them said anything about that, which was correct.

Baek Jun-ho has administered eleven trusts for a firm in Yeouido for most of his working life, and one of them, for eleven years, is the one behind a property holding company in Gangwon-do.

"I will tell you what a trust of that kind is," he said, "because both of you have an idea of it and one of you has the wrong one, and I do not know which."

"All of it, then," said Georgij.

"It is a set of instructions and a bank account, and nothing else. My job is to do what the instructions say and to be able to show that I did." Baek had his hands on his knees. "I do not know who benefits from that trust, and I am not hiding it from you. I have never been told, and there is no legal reason anybody would have to tell me."

"What the instructions cover."

"The operating costs of a residence. Staff, provisioning, maintenance, utilities, insurance, and medical costs against invoice." He listed it the way a man lists a thing he has read four hundred times. "It is not an unusual instrument. I have three others that look very like it. In two of those the beneficiary is a child, in the third a man who cannot manage his own affairs, and all three families I have met."

"And this one you have not."

"In eleven years I have never met anybody connected with it, been to the property, or spoken to a person who has." Baek said it without any drama at all. "There is a correspondence address and there is a man who signs for the settlor, and I have dealt with him by letter and once, at the beginning, in a room."

Georgij did not move.

"What he was like," he said.

Baek thought about that.

"Extremely pleasant," he said. "He asked me about my practice for twenty minutes before we did any business, and he remembered my answers eleven years later, in writing, in a letter about something else entirely."

"Four years ago the instructions were amended," said Baek. "That is the part Mr Park asked me about, and it is the part I have not been comfortable with. I would like to be exact about the size of my discomfort. It is small, it has never kept me from sleeping, and it has never gone away."

"Say the amendment."

"A flat in Seodaemun was added to the schedule. Rates, service charge, standing utilities. Not rent, because it is owned outright." He kept his hands where they were. "There is nobody living in it. I know that because the utility consumption is what it is, and I see the figures every year, and a flat with a person in it does not use what that one uses."

"And you asked."

"I wrote once, in the ordinary way. I received a very courteous letter explaining that the property belonged to a member of the household who was being cared for elsewhere, and that her affairs were being kept in order against her return." Baek's voice did not change. "That is a perfectly good answer. It is the answer I would have given. I have been giving it to myself for four years."

Georgij put one hand flat on the arm of the chair, which was as much as he permitted himself.

"Against her return," he said.

"Against her return."

Georgij had been waiting for the schedule since the word amended, and now that he had it he did not ask for anything else.

He went the other way instead, which is the way he has gone in every room since June, and it works exactly once per man.

"In July, four years ago, that trust paid a medical invoice," said Georgij. "A house call outside Seoul. The travelling cost three times the attendance and it was settled inside the month."

Baek Jun-ho did not move.

"How do you know that?"

"Because a woman at a settlement counter passed it four years ago and has not had a comfortable hour since the ninth of January, and because it was paid." Georgij did not hurry any of it. "An invoice of that shape, against that address, in that month. There is exactly one account in this country it could have come out of, and you have just spent ten minutes telling me that you are the only man who signs on it."

Sang-hoon looked from one of them to the other and did not say anything.

"You have not asked me a question yet," said Baek.

"No. I am going to say a thing instead, and you will tell me whether it is true, and if it is not true I will have been wrong out loud in front of both of you." Georgij put one hand flat on the arm of the chair. "You kept a copy."

The room did not do anything at all for about four seconds.

"There is a reason under that."

"Because in eleven years you have queried one line, and it was the flat, and it was not that invoice." He kept his voice exactly where it was. "A man who queries nothing and is troubled by one thing does one of two things with it. He forgets it inside a year, or he takes a copy and never mentions it to anybody, and afterward he cannot explain to himself why."

Baek Jun-ho put both hands on the document case beside his chair, which had been there the whole time and which Georgij had not once looked at.

"And the reason you think it is the second one."

"Because you have not forgotten it. You are sitting in a room on a Tuesday morning with a man you had never heard of on Friday."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"I paid it because it was properly rendered and inside the instructions," said Baek, "and because a solicitor who queries every unusual line in eleven years of a trust is a solicitor who does not have eleven trusts." He did not open the case. "And then I did a thing I have never done before or since."

"That is yours to say and not mine," said Georgij.

"I kept a copy."

There was a silence after that which Sang-hoon did not fill. Georgij let it run to the end of itself, because the next part had to be said by the man holding the case.

"You want it," said Baek.

"I do."

"Then there is what I get, and do not tell me that I get to do the right thing, because I have been doing the right thing on that account for eleven years and it has brought me here."

Georgij did not decorate it.

"You get to be told what you have been signing," he said. "I am the only person alive who can tell you. I have known since the second of June and it has been in a locked drawer since. I am going to tell you whether or not you open that case, because you earned it by sitting down."

Baek looked at him for a long moment.

"That is a strange way to negotiate," he said.

"It is not a negotiation. I am saying both halves at the same time so that neither of us has to pretend it is one." Georgij kept his hand where it was. "You have something I need and I have something you have wanted for four years. If I hand you mine first you will hand me yours, and I want both of us watching me do it in that order."

Baek Jun-ho opened the case.

He put it on the desk between the three of them, in a plastic sleeve, and did not hand it to anybody.

It is one page. It has a practice letterhead on it, and a date in July four years ago, and a line for the attendance and a line for the travelling, and the travelling is three times the attendance.

And in the box where the name goes there are six characters.

Georgij looked at it for a long time and did not touch the sleeve.

He has known what is on that page since the second of June. A woman at a settlement counter read six characters, put it through, and went to lunch, and has not stopped saying so since. He has been carrying her sentence for eight weeks.

This is the page.

"It is not a name, a hospital number or an insurance number," said Baek. "I have looked at all three formats for thirty years and it is none of the three, and that is as far as I have ever got with it."

"I know what it is," said Georgij, "and I said I would tell you first, so I am going to tell you before anybody in this room touches that sleeve."

"Then I will have it."

"It is a lot number."

Baek Jun-ho sat very still.

"I do not know what that means."

"It means that when that practice asked who they were attending, they were given a number instead of a name." Georgij put one finger beside the sleeve and did not touch it. "And that the number is the sort a house uses for a thing it is selling, and that the trust you administer paid the bill."

The window in that room faces a yard, and a yard on a Tuesday morning makes the noises a yard makes.

"I would like to sit for a moment," said Baek.

"Take as long as you want," said Sang-hoon.

He took about ninety seconds, and when he came back he came back all at once, which Georgij had seen once before this year, in a room with an exercise book in it.

"The flat," he said.

"Yes."

"The flat in Seodaemun is hers. Not the woman who is being cared for. The other one." Baek had gone entirely grey around the mouth. "Somebody has been having me pay the rates on a flat belonging to a person who was bought, so that the person would go on existing on paper."

A solicitor of thirty years got there out of one page and a utility bill, and he got there before the man who had brought him the page.

"I do not know that yet," said Georgij, "and I am not going to let you have it as a fact from me when it is a reading. I have two documents and a quantity of groceries."

"Do not do that." Baek said it quite sharply, and it was the first time his voice had done anything at all. "I am not a client and I am not a witness and you are not protecting me. I have signed that schedule every year for four years and I would like to be told what I have been signing, in the largest size it might be."

Georgij took that.

"Then the largest size is what you have just said," he said, "and I am not able to prove it, and I believe it."

There was one more thing, and it was Baek who raised it, standing, with the case under his arm.

"The man who signs for the settlor," he said. "You knew who he was before I described him. You had his face before I had finished the sentence."

"I did."

"Then you know something I have wondered about for eleven years, and I am going to ask it once and then let it go." Baek stood with his hand on the back of the chair. "Does he know?"

Georgij thought about the question properly, because it was the question, and because a man who had kept a copy for four years had earned an accurate answer rather than a quick one.

"I do not think he has been told," he said. "I think he has been given a version of it that a kind man could hold. I think he has held it, and that it has been made easy for him to go on holding it for a very long time."

"And you are going to take it off him."

"Yes."

Baek Jun-ho nodded once, the way a man does when something he has half known has been said in a room with other people in it.

"Then I am glad it is not me," he said, and went.

Georgij stayed behind for a moment, and Sang-hoon did not fill the silence, which he has learned to do since May.

"He kept a copy," said Georgij.

"He kept a copy for four years and did not know why." Sang-hoon turned his chair slightly. "I have four decisions I could not account for. He has one page he could not throw away. I do not think those are different things."

"No."

"Then say what you needed from this room, because you have had it since he opened that case and you have not said it aloud."

Georgij put both hands on the back of the chair he had not sat in when he came in.

"I needed the page," he said, "in the original, with a file reference on it, from a man who will say where he got it."

"For him."

"For him." Georgij did not decorate it. "There is exactly one thing on this earth that will move that man, and it is neither fear nor money nor anything that happens to him. It is finding out what the kind version was covering."

Sang-hoon looked at him for a while.

"Then get it in front of him," he said, "and do not do it in a restaurant."
