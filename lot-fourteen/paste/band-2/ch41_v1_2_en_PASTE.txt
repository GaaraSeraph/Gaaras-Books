Book Two · Chapter 41: The one who does not leave
Lot Fourteen · Version 1.2 · EN

Day Two Hundred and Ninety · Monday 20 July

Jang had given him the other word on the Friday, and then Saturday had happened, and the flowers had taken up the whole of a weekend that the word should have had.

He had it written down twice, once in Jang's phrasing and once in his own, because he had learned in June what happens to a fact that gets tidied before it is used.

Certain. Nine on the rota, counted eleven mornings across six weeks, never once ten. The Friday order has fed ten every Friday since the summer four years ago and nine every Friday before it. The grocer has not changed his round, his prices or his handwriting in six years.

Jang had said one thing more on the telephone and had made a point of saying it slowly.

"I am giving you certain about a quantity," he had said. "I am not giving you certain about a person. I have never seen a tenth person, and I want that in whatever you write down, because in a year somebody is going to read your book and not mine."

———

Sang-hoon took a table again rather than a room, and this time Georgij understood it as the compliment it had turned into.

"You have had three weeks," said Sang-hoon.

"I have had three weeks and I have an answer, and it is not the one you asked me for." Georgij sat down without being asked, which he had not done in eight months. "You asked me whether the woman Jang has been watching is the same woman the trust was built for eleven years ago."

"I did."

"I do not know. I have not got one document that touches it and I am not going to guess at it in front of you." He put both hands on the cloth. "And it has stopped being the question."

Sang-hoon put his cup down.

"Say why."

"Because there are two of them in that house."

The restaurant went on being a restaurant, which is the only reason either of them chose it.

"Say how you know that."

"Nine people work that building on a rota. Four cars, two of them carrying two, counted on eleven separate mornings across six weeks by a man who never once went through the gate." Georgij did not hurry any of it. "The grocer delivers a standing order every Friday. It feeds ten. It has fed ten every Friday since the summer four years ago, and it fed nine every Friday before that, and it has never once gone back down."

"That is food and not a person."

"That is what Jang said, in those words, and he made me repeat it back to him before he would hang up." Georgij let that stand for a moment. "It is a quantity in the shape of one person, which arrived in the summer four years ago and has not left since. In the same summer a doctor drove a very long way to a house in Gangwon-do and put a lot number in the box where a patient's name goes."

Sang-hoon did not say anything at all for a while.

"You are telling me," he said eventually, "that the person you have been looking for since May is in that building."

"I am telling you that somebody has been in that building for four years who did not arrive in one of the cars and does not leave in one of them." Georgij put one hand flat on the cloth beside the cup. "I have wanted it to be her since Friday afternoon and I have written that down twice, in two places, so that it cannot quietly become evidence."

"That is a very careful sentence."

"It is the only careful thing I have left. I have been loud for five weeks and I would like one part of this to still be worth something in September."

Sang-hoon sat back.

Then he did the thing that Georgij had not planned for, and had not planned for because it had not occurred to him that the man was capable of it.

"Give it to me," said Sang-hoon.

"Say what you mean."

"I mean the house. I mean the woman at the window and whoever else is in there." He said it quietly and without any performance in it at all. "I have people. Real ones, not men who stand on roads. I have lawyers who can be at a district office at nine in the morning with a welfare application, a doctor and a court officer. I have enough money to make a provincial authority move in a week instead of a year. You have a notebook and a man with a folded map."

Georgij did not answer straight away.

"You would do that," he said.

"I would have it done by Thursday." Sang-hoon turned his glass a quarter turn and left it. "I have sat at that man's table for eight years and given away four things I cannot account for. I would like to take one out of the middle of him, and I would like it to be a person and not a document. I am fifty-nine and I have not wanted anything that specific in a long time."

It was the first entirely honest offer anybody had made Georgij all year, and he had to say no to it, and he took a moment before he did because it deserved one.

"No," he said.

"Then say it properly. Not the polite version."

"If your lawyers walk into a district office on Thursday morning, that building is empty by Friday night." Georgij put one finger on the cloth. "Not the woman. She cannot be moved quickly and a man who has kept her alive for eleven years is not going to kill her in a hurry. The other one goes. The one who did not arrive in a car, who exists on no paper anywhere in this country, who cannot be reported missing by anybody because there is nobody who has her name."

Sang-hoon did not move.

"You are saying I would rescue one and lose the other."

"I am saying you would rescue the one who has a name in a letterbox in Seodaemun and a trust behind her and eleven years of paper." Georgij heard his own voice go somewhere flatter than he had intended and let it. "And the one nobody can prove exists would be in a car within a day. There is not a court in this country that will order a search for a person who has no line, no owner and no date."

The words came out of him in that order because they are the order he has used about himself three times this year, and he heard it happen, and he did not correct it.

Sang-hoon heard it too.

"That is your own sentence," he said.

"It is."

"You have used it about yourself. In a shed, in May, and I did not say anything at the time."

"I know. I have used it three times and I noticed the second time and I went on using it." Georgij put his hands flat. "It is accurate. That is the only reason I have not stopped."

Neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Then what do I do," said Sang-hoon, "because I am not going to sit in a restaurant for the rest of the summer being told about a building."

"You do the thing you are the only person who can do, and it is not the house." Georgij took the folded sheet out of his inside pocket at last and put it on the table without opening it. "Baek Jun-ho has administered that trust for eleven years and eleven others besides. He is a solicitor with a practising certificate, which is more than that man has ever had, and it is the only thing in the entire structure that belongs to somebody who could lose it."

Sang-hoon looked at the folded sheet and did not touch it.

"You want me to frighten a lawyer."

"I want the opposite of that, and I am going to be exact about it, because if you get this wrong it is the same as the district office." He did not raise his voice. "I want to know what he has signed and what he thinks he has been signing. Not to threaten him with it. A man who has approved invoices for eleven years without asking has an explanation he has been living inside, and there is a version of Thursday where somebody sits down with him and finds out what it is."

"And if he goes to Choi Dae-ho the same afternoon."

"Then we find out that he already knew, and that is worth having and it costs us the trust." Georgij let it sit. "I am not going to pretend that is a small risk to hand you. It is the largest one I have asked anybody to carry this year and it is not mine to carry, and I would like you to sleep on it rather than answer me at this table."

Sang-hoon looked at him for a long moment.

"You have got worse at making things sound easy," he said.

"Yes."

"Good."

It was the whole of what he thought of the afternoon, and he had put it in one word so that it could not be argued with.

———

Annie was in the small room when he got back, at his desk rather than at hers, with the twelve stems still in water on the corner where they had been since Saturday.

She had the notebook open, which she does not do, and she had it open at the second page, which she has never done.

"You left it out," she said.

"I did." He stopped in the doorway. "Not on purpose. I would like that to be true rather than clever, so I am saying it before you ask."

"I know it was not on purpose. You have not done anything on purpose since Saturday morning." Annie turned the notebook round. "Sit down and tell me about Park Sang-hoon."

He told her, in order, and it took under four minutes, and she did not interrupt any of it.

"He offered," she said, when he had finished.

"He offered, and he meant it, and he would have had it done by Thursday."

"And you said no to the only offer of real help anybody has made you since October."

"Yes."

Annie put one hand flat on the open notebook.

"Say it out loud, the whole of it, because you have been carrying it since two o'clock and you have not put it down anywhere."

Georgij sat with that.

"There is a woman in a house in Gangwon-do who has been dying by nine days at a time since the spring," he said, "and a man offered me a court officer and a doctor on Thursday morning, and I said no, and the reason I said no is somebody I have never met and cannot prove is there."

"That is the whole of it."

"That is the whole of it. And I would do it again on Thursday, and again the Thursday after that, and I am not going to dress it up as a hard decision made well." He looked at the flowers rather than at her. "I chose the one nobody can see. That is the choice. There is not a second half to it that makes it better."

Annie did not tell him he was right, which he noticed, and which he was grateful for in a way he did not examine.

"You have not slept since Friday," she said instead.

"No."

"Then go up."

He got as far as the door.

"May I say one thing first?"

"You may say anything you like in this room. You have never once needed to ask that and I have never worked out whether you know it."

Georgij stood with his hand on the frame and found that the sentence he had been carrying up from the car was already halfway out of him.

"When he offered," he said, "I did not think about the house first."

Annie waited.

"I thought that if I gave it to him it would be finished. And if it were finished I would have nothing to bring up those stairs in the evening, and I had that thought before I had the one about the person in the building." He heard it land in the room and did not take it back, and then he stopped, which he does not usually manage. "That is as far as that goes tonight."

The room did the thing it does.

"Then that is as far as it goes tonight," said Annie.

She did not move from his desk and she did not close the notebook, and she did not say any of the four things she could have said, all of which he would have believed.

"Go up, Georgij."

He went up.

He did not write anything in either page that night, for the first time since the eleventh of June. In the morning he did not put a line through the gap or explain it. A book with an entry for every single day is a book somebody is writing for an audience.
