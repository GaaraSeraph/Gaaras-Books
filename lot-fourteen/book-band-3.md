# Book Three · Chapter 1: The first thing that arrives
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.6 · EN

## Day Five Hundred and Ninety-Two · Tuesday 18 May

For fourteen days there has been nothing to build.

He still gets up at the same hour and reads the same four papers, and he still goes through the shipping notices and the corporate filings the way he has since the March before last.

It has not been for anything for fourteen days. He has not missed a morning.

The card came to the front door at twenty past ten.

Mrs Seo took it in the hall and brought it up herself.

She has done that once before, on a Tuesday in May of the year before, about a telephone call. She said at the time that she had been answering that telephone for nine years and that nobody had ever asked for him.

Georgij put the pen down.

"There is a card," said Mrs Seo. "It has your name on the front of it and it has it correctly, and it does not have hers on it anywhere."

She did not come into the room.

"The man who brought it was not from a firm that carries things. A man from one of those has a book and a bag and four more to do before twelve, and he had none of it." She put the card down on the corner of the desk where things go. "He did not ask me who I was before he handed it over."

"He would have had to ask somebody that."

"He did not ask me," said Mrs Seo. "And he was not looking at the house the way a man looks at a house he has not seen before."

She went back down and did not say anything else about it.

The card is printed and it is not expensive, and one line of it is in ink.

It gives a name, with the character in front of it that only ever goes in front of a dead man's.

It gives Wednesday the twenty-sixth of May at eleven in the morning, a temple in Jongno, and a hall inside the temple. It says what the day is. The day is the forty-ninth.

The forty-ninth day is counted from the death and the death is the first of them. Choi Dae-ho died on the eighth of April.

It is a rite that a family holds. Somebody has to book the hall and pay for it and stand at the front of it. If there is no family, the person who does those things has taken the family's place.

It is the sort of card a stationer keeps a box of.

At the bottom, where a card of that sort says who is asking, there is one name.

There is no office under it and no address and no telephone number, and there does not need to be. That name has been in the newspapers of this country for two years, in the sort of paragraph that does not explain who the man is, because whoever is reading it is assumed to know.

The name is Gong Doo-hyun. He is the Prosecutor General.

He turned it over. There is nothing on the back at all.

Georgij read it twice and then went and got a notebook, and it was not the current one.

He keeps them. There are four now, and the first is nearly full of things that came to nothing.

He found the page for the twenty-seventh of May last year in under a minute, because every page in there has the date at the top of it and because he knew roughly where it would be.

*Choi Dae-ho. 59. Former prosecutor, out early and nobody says why.*

He wrote that in a garden in Hannam-dong with a glass in his other hand, because Park Sang-hoon had said it in that order and at that speed.

He has been back to that page a great many times since.

Never once for that line.

He put the notebook down on the card and left it there.

There is a man who says why.

He is going to be standing at the front of a hall in Jongno on Wednesday of next week.

And he has sent a card to a house that is on no list, addressed to a man whose name has no public connection to it. The name exists on paper in the Republic of Korea. Not one piece gives anybody this door.

He looked the temple up, because that costs nothing.

It has two halls. The large one is the one that gets photographed, and it is where a man in that office would be expected to hold anything at all.

The card gives the other one. It is behind the large one and it takes about two hundred, and two hundred is a number a man can go down with a pen.

Georgij looked at that for a while and then wrote one line and put the date on it.

*Everybody in that room will be somebody he wanted in it.*

He telephoned Baek Jun-ho at half past eleven and asked one question.

"The office in Jung-gu that forwards," he said. "When they told you he had died, say who told them."

There was a pause on the line of about three seconds.

"I did not ask." Baek said it straight out. "I have thought about that four times since. I had the girl on the telephone for two minutes and she would have told me."

"You had no reason to ask."

"I had every reason and I did not have the wit." He was not making it smaller. "What has happened. I have asked myself the wrong question four times since the call."

Georgij told him what had come to the door and what was on it, and read him the name at the bottom, and did not say anything about what he thought it meant.

Baek Jun-ho did not answer for a while.

"I have been in this trade for thirty-two years," he said. "I have never once been on the same sheet of paper as that man and I never expected to be."

"You are not on it. I am."

"You are on it because he wrote you on it." Something had gone out of his voice. "I am going to say this badly. I wrote to Choi Dae-ho four times and every one of them went through that office, and the last came back marked in a clerk's hand. I have been quietly pleased with myself for writing to him since February."

"Yes."

"And now a man has taken the family's place, and the name at the bottom is his own, not an estate's and not a firm's. The office told me there was no address for anybody at all. I put that in a note on the twentieth of April, and I have read it since and not seen what it left unanswered."

Georgij did not telephone Park Sang-hoon.

Sang-hoon asked in three places in April and heard nothing back, and he does not usually ask anywhere. Going at it again now would tell him that something had arrived, and he would have the rest of it inside a day, and then two men would be carrying it instead of one.

There is a day for that, and this is not it.

* * *

The tray went up at seven the way it has gone up at seven for ten years.

He went down at about eight and told her all of it in order.

Neither of them had said anything about the Sunday and neither of them was going to, and it was in that room the whole time.

"Say the name again," said Annie.

"Gong Doo-hyun."

She put the pen down and left her hand on it.

"Twice in four years somebody who could have moved him has tried to move him, and he is still sitting in that office." Annie had not looked at the card. "One of the two was in this house in the spring of last year."

"I did not know that."

"You did not need it until this evening."

The clock in the hall did the thing it does at the quarter.

"Say what you think it is."

"Three things, and none of the three is written on it." Georgij did not sit down. "The first is that Choi Dae-ho is dead. That is the first time anybody outside this house has said so to us. Until this morning we had it from a girl on a telephone in April who did not know what she was saying."

Annie waited.

"The second is that a man took the family's place." He had his hands behind his back. "Baek was told there was no address for anybody at all. Somebody still booked a hall and put his own name at the bottom of a card, and that is not a thing you do for a man you have had dinner with."

"And the third."

"The third is that it is not an invitation. A man who wants me in a room does not need my name on the front of it. He needs the address, and he had it, and he has let me see that he had it."

The card was still on the corner of the desk where it had been since half past ten.

Annie did not answer for a while.

"There is a fourth and you have not said it," she said.

"No."

"Then I will say it." She had not moved in the chair. "You cannot refuse it. If you go, he gets an hour of looking at you. If you do not go, he gets the fact that you understood the card, and the second is worth more to him and costs him nothing."

"Yes."

"You had that at about half past ten this morning and you have carried it round the house all day, and you brought me the other three first because they are the ones I can do anything about."

She turned the card round on the desk with one finger so that it faced her, and read it, which she had not done until then.

Georgij stood where he was.

"May I go to it?"

"You are asking because of the thirtieth of April."

"I am asking because I do not know the price, and the part of it I do have belongs in this room."

Annie looked at him for a moment.

"I could tell you not to go. I have thought about it for as long as you have been standing there and I am not going to." She did not soften any of it. "A man who wants you in a room and cannot have you finds out what he is dealing with some other way, and I hear about that afterwards. I would rather it happened at eleven in the morning in a hall with two hundred people in it."

"There are going to be men in that hall who have read people for a living for thirty years," said Georgij. "I am going to walk into it wearing the one thing in this country that answers the question before anybody puts it."

"Yes."

"Better that he has it in the first four seconds than that he spends a fortnight getting it wrong."

The machine in the room at the back ran for four minutes and stopped.

Annie picked the pen back up, which is what she does when a thing has been decided.

"Go on the Wednesday," she said. "Come back here and tell me all of it in order, including the parts you have decided are not important."

"Yes, Mistress."

"And Georgij." She was writing again. "You are not to be the last man out of that hall."

* * *

On the eighth of February a letter went out of an office in Yeouido, and Georgij had said in that office what it was.

*There is nothing in the world with his name on it except what he signed himself. That letter is going to be the first thing that arrives at him.*

It went to a solicitor's office in Jung-gu that forwards. It was forwarded.

Choi Dae-ho was dead on the eighth of April.

The card is on the corner of the desk where Mrs Seo put it, and it has his own name on the front of it, in a hand he does not know.

# Book Three · Chapter 2: Thank you for coming
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.10 · EN

## Day Six Hundred · Wednesday 26 May

He was on the road at half past nine for a thing that started at eleven, because he did not know the building and would not go into a room he had not walked round first.

He took nothing into the hall. There was nothing to take.

The coat is the grey one and it was paid for in October of the year before last by somebody else. There are forty men in that service who can read a coat, and it is the first thing about him that any of them will get right.

* * *

The temple is up a lane off a street that has been widened twice, and the lane has not.

The large hall is at the top of the steps and is the one on the postcards. There were two coach parties in front of it at ten past ten.

A woman sells cut flowers out of a bucket at the bottom of the steps, and she has been there long enough to have worn a place in the stone.

The other hall is behind it and down. There is no view of it from the street and there is nothing on the outside of it except the notice for the day.

Georgij went round the outside of it once, which took four minutes, and then went in at twenty to eleven.

There is a table inside the door with a book open on it and a pen beside the book.

A man of about twenty-five sits behind it. He says nothing to anybody and turns the page when the page is full.

Everybody signs it. That is what it is for.

Georgij stood in front of that table for about four seconds.

There are papers in the Republic of Korea with his name on them, and there are books at doors in his own hand. Not one of them points to this house.

Not signing it would have been the only thing anybody in that hall remembered about the morning.

He wrote it, in the hand he writes in, and it took him about two seconds, and he went and stood at the back.

There were about ninety people in a hall that takes two hundred.

He counted them in the first six minutes, in fours, the way he was taught to count a room in the fourth house.

Eleven of them he has read about. Four of them he has been in a room with. One of them sat at a table on the eighteenth of March.

He came in at five to eleven. He saw Georgij at the back, and he went and stood on the other side of a pillar, and he did not come out from behind it again.

Nobody spoke to Georgij at any point in the whole of it.

That is not the same as nobody noticing him, and he knew the difference by the fourth minute.

There is a photograph at the front and it is not a recent one.

The man in it has a full head of black hair and no weight under the jaw.

He is wearing the sort of suit a man wears who has not yet worked out that he can afford a better one.

He is about thirty-five.

Somebody had a choice of twenty-five years of photographs of that man and went back past all of them.

The monk began at eleven exactly and it went on for a long time, and the chanting is not for the people in the room and does not care whether they follow it.

At the part where it is done, they went up in twos and threes and bowed twice.

Georgij went up with the rest and bowed twice and did it correctly. Doing it wrong would have been a thing about him, and he had not come there to be a thing about himself.

He did not put his head down for any longer than the form asks for.

He is not sorry Choi Dae-ho is dead, and he does not arrange his face to look as if he were.

Gong Doo-hyun stood at the front of it from eleven until twenty past one.

He is about sixty and he is not a large man, and there is nothing about him that would make anybody look at him twice in a street.

He thanked them one at a time, in a line, and did not hurry any of them, and the line went out of the door and down the steps for the last forty minutes of it.

Georgij was the last man in the line, because he wanted the whole of it in front of him before he had to be in it.

"Thank you for coming," said Gong Doo-hyun.

He had said it about ninety times by then, and he said it to Georgij in the same voice he had used on the man in front of him, who was seventy and had wept twice.

Georgij bowed the way the man in front of him had.

"You are the only person in this hall who does not know anybody in it."

"Yes."

"There are three others who do not, and they are from the hall, the crematorium, and the office that sent out the notices." Gong Doo-hyun had his hands at his sides and did not do anything with them. "I am going to say one thing to you, and then you may go and stand anywhere you like, or leave, and I will not mind either."

"The one thing."

Georgij did not put anything in front of it.

"He was thirty-five and he was the best man in that building at the thing he did." Gong Doo-hyun said it with no weight on it anywhere. "He took four companies apart on paper and he was right about all four of them, and there was not one of the four that anybody in that service could have done in the time he did it."

Georgij did not move at all.

"The fifth one he did not take apart."

The monk was putting things away at the front and there was a woman folding cloth, and the hall had begun to sound like a room again.

"That is what I wanted to say to you today, and I am not going to finish it here."

"May I say one thing to you?"

"You may say anything you like." Gong Doo-hyun did not step back and did not look at the door. "I am going to remember all of it, and you know that, and you have decided to say it anyway, and I would like to hear what a man says under that condition."

"I have been told twice what he was at thirty-five," said Georgij. "Both times by somebody who was not sorry."

Gong Doo-hyun looked at him for about two seconds.

"Yes," he said. "That will be most of the people you talk to."

He turned to the woman who had been waiting behind Georgij for a minute and a half, and thanked her for coming, and said her husband's name, and asked whether the road up had been bad.

It was the ninety-first time and it was not a shorter one than the first.

The man who had stood behind the pillar went out at the same time Georgij did, because there is one door.

He did not say anything and he did not look up, and at the top of the steps he stopped and put a hand on the rail for a moment and then went on down.

He was at that table on the eighteenth of March and he was one of the four who ate.

Georgij has read six pages about him. He knows what the man does for a living, what he was sold, what it has cost him since, and the name of the firm that is going to buy the rest of it by the autumn.

None of that says why he came today, and there is nowhere to look it up.

Georgij went down the steps past the woman with the bucket and got into the car and did not tell Jang to start it for about a minute.

He worked out in the car that the man had not asked him one question.

Not what he was, not who had sent him, and not what he had to do with any of it.

And not why a man nobody in that hall knew had come up a lane in Jongno on a Wednesday morning.

He has spent two years getting what he needs out of people by asking them something small enough to answer.

The other man did not want anything out of him today, and there is no version of that which is good.

He took the notebook out at the lights on Yulgok-ro.

The line he had written on Tuesday the eighteenth had the date under it.

*Everybody in that room will be somebody he wanted in it.*

It is true, and it is not what he meant by it.

He had read it as a list. A man who fills a hall fills it with people who are useful to him, and the useful ones are the ones who owe something or want something. A hall of ninety is ninety names and about eleven of them worth having.

There is nobody in that hall who owed Choi Dae-ho anything by the end of it.

There is nobody in it who gained by coming, and there are eleven who are in a service that keeps a note of who was seen where.

Choi Dae-ho has been out of that service for twenty-five years and out of the trade since March and dead since April, and there is no version of standing in that hall that pays.

They came when he asked.

The hall was the cheaper of the two. The card was the sort a stationer keeps a box of. Nobody catered it and there was no car for the family, because there is no family.

Georgij went at the money on the way back, because that is what he does, and there is nothing in it.

It was paid for out of one man's own pocket, in a sum a man on a public salary would notice for a month or two.

There is no fund behind it. Nobody was asked to contribute and nothing went on anybody's account.

The only extravagant thing in the whole arrangement is his own name at the bottom of ninety cards.

* * *

He told her all of it in order at about seven, and it took eleven minutes, and he left nothing out of it.

"And what did you get," said Annie.

"Nothing I can use."

She let that stand for a moment.

"Say the rest of it."

"He is going to finish that sentence somewhere else. He has told me that he is going to and he has not said when, and he will not say when, because the not saying is most of it." Georgij had his hands behind his back. "And I am going to go when he does."

"Yes."

"I would like to say the other thing, because I am going to have thought it either way."

"Then say it."

"I have spent two years finding out what men have done," said Georgij. "It has worked every time, and it worked because there has always been something."

Annie did not write anything down.

"I went at that man for four hours today. The hall is cheap and the card is cheap and the money is his own." He did not move anywhere. "And every single thing he did in that building, he did under his own name in front of ninety people."

"And."

"And there is nothing to find, and I have never once been in front of that." He did not soften it. "I did not know until this afternoon that all of it rested on the other man being dirty."

Annie put the pen down.

"Every one of them was," she said.

"Yes."

"That is not the same as it being the reason it worked, and you have just said the difference out loud, and I want it noted that you said it and not me."

Downstairs somebody shut a door and did not shut it hard.

"It is noted," said Georgij.

# Book Three · Chapter 3: At sixty
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.6 · EN

## Day Six Hundred and Twenty-Eight · Wednesday 23 June

It took four weeks, and Georgij spent them getting ready, and none of it was any use.

He read every published thing that man has put his name to since 2011, which is nine speeches, two forewords and an address to a graduating year. There is not a sentence in any of them that he would not say again in a room with the people in it.

He asked Mr Chae what an office of that sort can do to a man without a case, and got a proper answer, and it did not help. Everything on the list was something Gong Doo-hyun was not going to do.

He did not ask Park Sang-hoon and he did not ask Chairman Woo.

A man who telephones two chaebol about a prosecutor has told the prosecutor something, and it is the one thing about himself that is true.

On the second Thursday he wrote one line in the book and did not put anything under it for a fortnight.

*There is nothing behind him. Work out what that means before he asks.*

He had not worked it out.

* * *

The telephone rang in the small room at ten past nine on the Monday, and Mrs Seo answered it in the hall as she does.

She did not come up.

She wrote it on the pad that is kept by the telephone for exactly this, in the hand she writes in, and she brought the page up with the tray at eleven.

*Wednesday, half past three. He gave the address himself and he did not spell it.*

Georgij read it twice.

The first time, a man from that office came to this door and carried it. The second time it was the man himself on the telephone.

The third time it will be Georgij going to him.

Each of the three is politer than the one before.

* * *

Four weeks to the day, and the same day of the week.

Nobody arranges that by accident and nobody mentions it afterwards.

* * *

The building is in Seocho and it is the one everybody has seen, and it looks like what it is.

He was through the door at twenty past three and in the room at half past, and nobody asked him for anything except his name, which he gave.

The office is on a corner and it is not large. There is a desk, two chairs on the near side of it, a shelf of bound volumes that have been used, and a photograph of a woman and two grown children on the sill behind him.

There is nothing on the walls at all.

Georgij reads a room off what is in it. It has worked in every room he has walked into for two years, and in most of them it took under a minute.

He read this one in about six seconds and got nothing.

A man who wants something buys something. There is nothing here that anybody gave him and nothing here that anybody could.

* * *

"Sit down," said Gong Doo-hyun.

Georgij sat down in the chair on the near side of the desk.

"There is a file and I am going to show it to you, because you are going to spend the next year guessing at it otherwise, and I would rather have the year for something else."

He turned it round on the desk and pushed it across, and did not keep a hand on it.

There are eleven pages in it.

The first of them is a photocopy of a page out of a book with about forty names on it.

The fourth name down that page is in a hand Georgij knows better than any other in the world, because it is his.

"That is where it starts," said Gong Doo-hyun. "You wrote it yourself and you knew what you were doing when you did it."

"Yes."

"The rest is nine pages of things that are all true and prove nothing, and one page that is a question I asked myself in April and have not been able to put down." He was not enjoying any of it. "I want you to see how thin it is. A thin file that does not go away is worse than a thick one, and you will understand why before you leave this room."

* * *

Georgij went through it at his own speed and did not hurry for the man watching him.

Six of the nine are dates. Two are companies with nothing wrong on the face of them. One is a list of five people who lost something between March and August of the year before last, with a line against each of them and no line joining them.

There is not one thing in it that could be put in front of anybody.

The eleventh page has one line on it in a small upright hand, and a date in April.

*If none of it is an offence, what is it.*

A man who wanted a conviction does not write that down. A man who wanted one would have stopped at the ninth page and gone to find a tenth that helped.

"May I say what it is?"

"Say it."

"It is a shape." Georgij put the pages square and did not push them back. "There is no offence in it. There is a shape, and the shape is mine, and you cannot get it any further than that."

"No."

"Then it is not the file that I am here for."

"No."

* * *

Gong Doo-hyun took the file back and put it square with the edge of the desk.

"I know you killed him," he said. "That is not the question I brought you here to answer."

Georgij did not move.

"Then say the question."

"A man in that house bought him at thirty-five. A man in that house finished him at sixty." He did not raise his voice anywhere in it. "He spent twenty-five years being what one afternoon in April made him. You have had two."

* * *

The building did the things a building of that size does at a quarter to four.

"I am not interested in whether you can justify what you did," said Gong Doo-hyun. "You can. I have read it four times and every line of it is true, and that is the worst part of the file and not the best."

He did not put a hand on it again.

"I am interested in what you are going to be at sixty."

* * *

Georgij has been asked a great many things across a desk.

He has never been asked one he could not answer with something true.

* * *

"May I ask you about the year after?" he said.

"Which year."

"Two thousand and three."

Gong Doo-hyun sat back for the first time since Georgij had come in.

"He came to the same place on a Thursday for about a year," he said. "He talked the way we all talked. He was still better at it than anybody at that table and he still knew what a bad indictment looked like, and he told us so, and he was right."

"And then?"

"And then he stopped coming, and there was no week in which it happened."

"There was no afternoon where he decided. It worked, and then it worked again." He said it without any weight on it. "By the third year the man who used to say what a bad indictment looked like did not say it any more. Nobody at that table was writing indictments."

Georgij did not say anything for about four seconds.

"That is what I brought you here for," said Gong Doo-hyun, "and you asked me for it yourself, and I would not have got it in front of you any other way."

* * *

"Next to her," said Georgij, and it was an answer to a question that had been asked six minutes earlier.

"That is who. I asked what."

Georgij did not answer.

"Say one thing you will be at sixty that does not have her in it."

Georgij had his hands where the man could see them and had not moved them since he sat down.

"I cannot."

Gong Doo-hyun did not write it down and did not need to.

* * *

"I know what I will do if somebody points anything at her."

"So did Choi," said Gong Doo-hyun.

* * *

Neither of them said anything for a while.

It is the first time in two years that a sentence has been put in front of Georgij that he could not take apart, and it took the man in the chair three words and no papers at all.

He did not try. Trying would have been an answer and there was not one.

Gong Doo-hyun did not follow it up.

He had brought a man in to find out one thing. He found it out at twenty to four, and there was nothing to put in the rest of the hour, and neither of them got up.

* * *

"You may go," said Gong Doo-hyun. "I am not going to ask you anything and I am not going to tell you not to do anything, and if you were expecting either of those you have misread the afternoon."

Georgij stood up.

"I would like to say one thing to you, and then I will go."

"Say it."

"You buried a man that nobody else would have buried," said Georgij. "I have been in a great many rooms this year and I have not seen anything like it."

Gong Doo-hyun looked at him for about two seconds.

"That is the second time you have said a true thing to me that costs you nothing." He had already picked up the file. "I would like you to notice that I have written both of them down."

* * *

He was on the expressway at twenty past four and did not say anything to Jang for eleven minutes.

There are two men in this country who have looked at what he does and wanted nothing for it. One of them wrote a letter in February in his own hand and posted it from a box that is not near his house.

The other one has eleven pages and a question, and he cannot be paid, and he is not going to get tired, and there is nothing in the world he wants.

Georgij worked out on that road what that leaves.

He did not write it down.

* * *

He told her all of it in order at about eight, and it took nineteen minutes, and he left nothing out of it.

Annie did not interrupt any of it, and she does not.

"Say the last part again."

"So did Choi."

"Yes." She let it be in the room for as long as it needed. "And what did you say to that."

"Nothing. There was nothing to say to it that would not have been a worse version of what I had already said."

"Then say what he is," said Annie.

"An honest man with a file and no case, who is not going to be bought and not going to get tired."

* * *

Annie put the pen down.

"That is what he is to you." She had not moved in the chair. "Say what he is to me."

* * *

Georgij did not answer for a long time.

"Nothing yet," he said.

Annie picked the pen back up.

# Book Three · Chapter 4: Sixteen
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.10 · EN

## Day Six Hundred and Forty-Five · Saturday 10 July

There was a car on the drive at twenty past ten that had not been booked through the house.

Georgij noticed it from the landing window, and what he noticed was not the car.

It was that Mrs Seo was already at the front door before it stopped, which she does not do, because the door gets answered when it is knocked on.

* * *

He went down.

There was a boy in the hall.

* * *

He is about one metre seventy-two and he has grown into most of it. He is wearing a suit that fits, which means somebody paid a tailor and not a shop, and shoes that have been worn in on purpose.

He was standing in the middle of the hall with his hands at his sides.

He had been in the house for perhaps forty seconds.

In that time he had looked at two doors off the hall, at the staircase, at the glass wall at the end and the hill behind it, and at the two people in front of him.

He had done all of it without moving his head more than he had to.

Then he stopped and stood still, because he had finished.

* * *

It took him about nine seconds.

Georgij stood on the bottom step and did not say anything for longer than he meant to.

* * *

"This is Lee Jun," said Mrs Seo.

She said it the way she says the second sitting is at seven.

"He is sixteen. He came from a house in Yangju and he has an account of himself in a folder, and I have read it, and it is in order."

"Thank you."

"The room at the top of the back stairs is made up and it has been since Thursday." She had her hands folded in front of her. "I was told on Thursday."

Georgij heard the whole of that sentence.

Mrs Seo has never once told him a thing she was not told to tell him, and she has never once left one of them out either.

She went back down the passage to whatever she had been doing before the car turned in.

* * *

"Good morning," said Georgij.

"Good morning, sir."

The boy said it in the register a boy of sixteen uses on a man he has been told about and has not met.

It was correct, and it was the third thing he had got right in a minute and a half.

"You counted the doors."

"Yes, sir."

"So did I. I did it standing where you are standing, and it took me the same time." Georgij did not soften any of it. "You may stop doing it in this house. It will take you about four months to stop, and nobody is going to mind while you are still doing it."

Lee Jun did not say anything to that.

He had been given a piece of information he had no use for, and he could not tell whether it was a test, and he did what he has learned to do, which is nothing.

* * *

There was one bag on the step behind him and the driver had already gone.

It is a good bag and it is not full. There is nothing in this house that came out of it and there is not going to be.

A boy of sixteen who has lived somewhere for two years has photographs, or a ticket stub, or something a person gave him.

There is none of that in it, and that is a decision, and somebody made it before the car turned in.

* * *

The folder was on the table in the hall where a folder goes.

Georgij read it standing up, and it took eleven minutes, and neither of them sat down.

It is a name, a registration, and a guardianship order made two years ago and renewed once.

There is a training contract with a firm in Yangju that trains house staff for people who can afford to have staff trained, and a certificate for the year he finished, seventeen months late and top of nine.

There is a health record with four entries in it and a dentist twice a year.

There is a line in the guardianship order for the person who applied, and it is a solicitor in Jung-gu acting on instruction, and there is no instructing party named anywhere in the folder.

Georgij read that page twice.

Everything in it is correct.

Somebody has spent two years making a person legal, one document at a time, and has done it properly and has not put a name on any of it.

* * *

"Do you know who paid for the training."

"No, sir."

"Do you want to."

Lee Jun took slightly longer than he had taken over anything else.

"No, sir."

"That is the first thing you have said this morning that you decided yourself."

* * *

The boy looked at him then, properly, for about a second, and what went across his face was not gratitude.

It was that he had been caught, and that he had not expected to be caught in this house, and that he was now going to have to work out what kind of house it is.

* * *

Georgij took him up to the room at the top of the back stairs and did not carry anything for him.

He showed him the bathroom, the back door, the kitchen, and the two places in the garden where a person can stand and not be seen from any window.

He did not explain the last one and the boy did not ask.

Halfway along the upstairs passage Georgij understood that he was not showing him anything.

Lee Jun was checking it against something he already had.

Somebody gave a training firm in Yangju the arrangement of this house, and they did it long enough ago for a boy to have learned it.

At the door of the room Lee Jun stopped and looked at the collar.

He had been not looking at it for eleven minutes and he had done it well.

"You may look at it."

"Yes, sir."

He looked at it for about two seconds, which was as long as he needed, and then he looked at the floor.

Two years ago that boy had a line in a catalogue and a number over it. He has none now.

The man showing him the room has neither, and has the collar.

"It does not come off," said Georgij. "Nobody in this house will ask you to wear anything and nobody will explain that one to you. If you want to know what it is for, ask me in a year and I will tell you then."

"Yes, sir."

* * *

The boy had his hand on the door and did not open it.

"May I ask one thing, sir?"

"Ask."

"What am I paid?"

He asked it flatly and without any apology in it, and it is the only question he asked all day, and it is the correct one.

A boy who has been sold twice does not need to be told what he is worth. He needs to be told what he is owed, and they are not the same number, and he has worked out that they are not.

Georgij did not have the answer.

He has never been paid anything and he has never once been asked, and it took him about two seconds to find the door out of that.

"Mrs Seo pays the household and she pays it on the last working day of the month." He said it in her voice and not in his. "Ask her on Monday. She will have expected it and she will not think less of you for it."

"Yes, sir."

* * *

Georgij went down the back stairs and stood in the passage for a moment.

He is twenty-seven.

At sixteen he was in the sixth house, or the seventh, and he has never had to work out which because there has never been a reason to.

He worked it out standing in that passage. It was the seventh.

There is a second one and it is older.

On the fourth of October he took a buyer apart with one true sentence, and the boy that buyer had paid for went back into the next month’s catalogue. That is the whole of what he did for him. It was not a good outcome and it was a better one.

A woman four rows away bought him in November.

Georgij has never been able to decide whether that is a thing he did or a thing that happened afterwards, and he has had two years.

He did not do anything with either of them.

* * *

Annie was at the desk at half past two and had been since eleven.

"There is a boy in the room at the top of the back stairs," said Georgij.

"Yes."

"His folder is in order. All of it. Two years of it."

"It is."

Georgij stood where he stands.

"On the fourth of October I asked you what had happened to lot eleven. I asked again on the seventh." He said it without any weight on it at all. "You did not answer either time, and I stopped."

"You did."

* * *

"I have carried that boy for two years," said Georgij.

He did not put anything round it and he did not look away.

"You could have put it down for me on any day of it."

"Yes."

"Why did you not."

* * *

Annie put the pen down.

"Because you would have thanked me," she said.

Georgij did not say anything at all.

"And then it would have been about you."

* * *

In the room at the back a machine ran for four minutes and stopped.

"Yes, Mistress."

"That is not agreement. Say whether it is."

Georgij thought about that for about four seconds, which is a long time for him.

"No," he said.

* * *

Annie looked at him.

"Good."

She has had a great many people say yes to her, and she can tell the two apart from the other side of a room.

"He is not a present and he is not a debt. He was fourteen and he was for sale twice." She picked the pen back up. "What you do with him from here is yours, and I am not going to have an opinion about it, and you may take that as the whole of what I owe you on this."

"I do not know what I am going to do with him."

"No."

"I know two things. One of them is this house and the other one is the trade." Georgij did not move. "There is not a third."

Annie did not offer him one.

* * *

Georgij went and saw about the second sitting.

At seven the tray went up the way it has gone up at seven for ten years, and there was an extra place laid at the kitchen table, and nobody had asked Mrs Seo to lay it.

Lee Jun sat where he was put and ate what he was given and said four words in an hour, and all four of them were to Mrs Seo.

He has been in the house for nine hours.

He has not once been in a room without knowing where the door is.
