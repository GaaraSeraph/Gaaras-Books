# Book Three · Chapter 1: The first thing that arrives
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.5 · EN

## Day Five Hundred and Ninety-Two · Tuesday 18 May

For fourteen days there has been nothing to build.

He still gets up at the same hour and reads the same four papers, and he still goes through the shipping notices and the corporate filings the way he has since the March before last.

It has not been for anything for fourteen days. He has not missed a morning.

The card came to the front door at twenty past ten.

Mrs Seo took it in the hall and brought it up herself.

She has done that once before, on a Friday in May of the year before, about a telephone call. She said at the time that she had been answering that telephone for nine years and that nobody had ever asked for him.

Georgij put the pen down.

"There is a card," said Mrs Seo. "It has your name on the front of it and it has it correctly, and it does not have hers on it anywhere."

She did not come into the room.

"The man who brought it was not from a firm that carries things. A man from one of those has a book and a bag and four more to do before twelve, and he had none of it." She put the card down on the corner of the desk where things go. "He did not ask me who I was before he handed it over."

"He would have had to ask somebody that."

"He did not ask me," said Mrs Seo. "And he was not looking at the house the way a man looks at a house he has not seen before."

She went back down and did not say anything else about it.

The card is printed and it is not expensive, and one line of it is in ink.

It gives a name, with the character in front of it that only ever goes in front of a dead man's.

It gives Wednesday the twenty-sixth of May at eleven in the morning, a temple in Jongno, and a hall inside the temple. It says what the day is. The day is the forty-ninth.

The forty-ninth day is counted from the death and the death is the first of them. Choi Dae-ho died on the eighth of April.

It is a rite that a family holds. Somebody has to book the hall and pay for it and stand at the front of it. If there is no family, the person who does those things has taken the family's place.

It is the sort of card a stationer keeps a box of.

At the bottom, where a card of that sort says who is asking, there is one name.

There is no office under it and no address and no telephone number, and there does not need to be. That name has been in the newspapers of this country for two years, in the sort of paragraph that does not explain who the man is, because whoever is reading it is assumed to know.

The name is Gong Doo-hyun. He is the Prosecutor General.

He turned it over. There is nothing on the back at all.

Georgij read it twice and then went and got a notebook, and it was not the current one.

He keeps them. There are four now, and the first is nearly full of things that came to nothing.

He found the page for the twenty-seventh of May last year in under a minute, because every page in there has the date at the top of it and because he knew roughly where it would be.

*Choi Dae-ho. 59. Former prosecutor, out early and nobody says why.*

He wrote that in a garden in Hannam-dong with a glass in his other hand, because Park Sang-hoon had said it in that order and at that speed.

He has been back to that page a great many times since.

Never once for that line.

He put the notebook down on the card and left it there.

There is a man who says why.

He is going to be standing at the front of a hall in Jongno on Wednesday of next week.

And he has sent a card to a house that is on no list, addressed to a man whose name exists on one piece of paper in the Republic of Korea. That one exists because a compliance officer needed something in the second week of October two years ago.

He looked the temple up, because that costs nothing.

It has two halls. The large one is the one that gets photographed, and it is where a man in that office would be expected to hold anything at all.

The card gives the other one. It is behind the large one and it takes about two hundred, and two hundred is a number a man can go down with a pen.

Georgij looked at that for a while and then wrote one line and put the date on it.

*Everybody in that room will be somebody he wanted in it.*

He telephoned Baek Jun-ho at half past eleven and asked one question.

"The office in Jung-gu that forwards," he said. "When they told you he had died, say who told them."

There was a pause on the line of about three seconds.

"I did not ask." Baek said it straight out. "I have thought about that four times since. I had the girl on the telephone for two minutes and she would have told me."

"You had no reason to ask."

"I had every reason and I did not have the wit." He was not making it smaller. "Say what has happened."

Georgij told him what had come to the door and what was on it, and read him the name at the bottom, and did not say anything about what he thought it meant.

Baek Jun-ho did not answer for a while.

"I have been in this trade for thirty-eight years," he said. "I have never once been on the same sheet of paper as that man and I never expected to be."

"You are not on it. I am."

"You are on it because he wrote you on it." Something had gone out of his voice. "I am going to say this badly. I wrote to Choi Dae-ho four times and every one of them went through that office, and the last came back marked in a clerk's hand, and I have been quietly pleased with myself about that letter since February."

"Yes."

"And now a man has taken the family's place, and the name at the bottom is his own, not an estate's and not a firm's. The office told me there was no address for anybody at all. I put that in a note on the twentieth of April, and I have read it since and not seen what it left unanswered."

Georgij did not telephone Park Sang-hoon.

Sang-hoon asked in three places in April and heard nothing back, and he does not usually ask anywhere. Going at it again now would tell him that something had arrived, and he would have the rest of it inside a day, and then two men would be carrying it instead of one.

There is a day for that, and this is not it.

* * *

The tray went up at seven the way it has gone up at seven for ten years.

He went down at about eight and told her all of it in order.

Neither of them had said anything about the Sunday and neither of them was going to, and it was in that room the whole time.

"Say the name again," said Annie.

"Gong Doo-hyun."

She put the pen down and left her hand on it.

"Twice in four years somebody who could have moved him has tried to move him, and he is still sitting in that office." Annie had not looked at the card. "One of the two was in this house in the spring of last year."

"I did not know that."

"You did not need it until this evening."

The clock in the hall did the thing it does at the quarter.

"Say what you think it is."

"Three things, and none of the three is written on it." Georgij did not sit down. "The first is that Choi Dae-ho is dead. That is the first time anybody outside this house has said so to us. Until this morning we had it from a girl on a telephone in April who did not know what she was saying."

Annie waited.

"The second is that a man took the family's place." He had his hands behind his back. "Baek was told there was no address for anybody at all. Somebody still booked a hall and put his own name at the bottom of a card, and that is not a thing you do for a man you have had dinner with."

"And the third."

"The third is that it is not an invitation. A man who wants me in a room does not need my name on the front of it. He needs the address, and he had it, and he has let me see that he had it."

The card was still on the corner of the desk where it had been since half past ten.

Annie did not answer for a while.

"There is a fourth and you have not said it," she said.

"No."

"Then I will say it." She had not moved in the chair. "You cannot refuse it. If you go, he gets an hour of looking at you. If you do not go, he gets the fact that you understood the card, and the second is worth more to him and costs him nothing."

"Yes."

"You had that at about half past ten this morning and you have carried it round the house all day, and you brought me the other three first because they are the ones I can do anything about."

She turned the card round on the desk with one finger so that it faced her, and read it, which she had not done until then.

Georgij stood where he was.

"May I go to it?"

"You are asking because of the thirtieth of April."

"I am asking because I do not know the price, and the part of it I do have belongs in this room."

Annie looked at him for a moment.

"I could tell you not to go. I have thought about it for as long as you have been standing there and I am not going to." She did not soften any of it. "A man who wants you in a room and cannot have you finds out what he is dealing with some other way, and I hear about that afterwards. I would rather it happened at eleven in the morning in a hall with two hundred people in it."

"There are going to be men in that hall who have read people for a living for thirty years," said Georgij. "I am going to walk into it wearing the one thing in this country that answers the question before anybody puts it."

"Yes."

"Better that he has it in the first four seconds than that he spends a fortnight getting it wrong."

The machine in the room at the back ran for four minutes and stopped.

Annie picked the pen back up, which is what she does when a thing has been decided.

"Go on the Wednesday," she said. "Come back here and tell me all of it in order, including the parts you have decided are not important."

"Yes, Mistress."

"And Georgij." She was writing again. "You are not to be the last man out of that hall."

* * *

On the eighth of February a letter went out of an office in Yeouido, and Georgij had said in that office what it was.

*There is nothing in the world with his name on it except what he signed himself. That letter is going to be the first thing that arrives at him.*

It went to a solicitor's office in Jung-gu that forwards. It was forwarded.

Choi Dae-ho was dead on the eighth of April.

The card is on the corner of the desk where Mrs Seo put it, and it has his own name on the front of it, in a hand he does not know.

# Book Three · Chapter 2: Thank you for coming
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.8 · EN

## Day Six Hundred · Wednesday 26 May

He was on the road at half past nine for a thing that started at eleven, because he did not know the building and would not go into a room he had not walked round first.

He took nothing with him. There was nothing to take.

The coat is the grey one and it was paid for in October of the year before last by somebody else. There are forty men in that service who can read a coat, and it is the first thing about him that any of them will get right.

* * *

The temple is up a lane off a street that has been widened twice, and the lane has not.

The large hall is at the top of the steps and is the one on the postcards. There were two coach parties in front of it at ten past ten.

A woman sells cut flowers out of a bucket at the bottom of the steps, and she has been there long enough to have worn a place in the stone.

The other hall is behind it and down. There is no view of it from the street and there is nothing on the outside of it except the notice for the day.

Georgij went round the outside of it once, which took four minutes, and then went in at twenty to eleven.

There is a table inside the door with a book open on it and a pen beside the book.

A man of about twenty-five sits behind it. He says nothing to anybody and turns the page when the page is full.

Everybody signs it. That is what it is for.

Georgij stood in front of that table for about four seconds.

There is no paper in the Republic of Korea with his name on it that he put there. What exists, somebody else needed and somebody else made.

Not signing it would have been the only thing anybody in that hall remembered about the morning.

He wrote it, in the hand he writes in, and it took him about two seconds, and he went and stood at the back.

There were about ninety people in a hall that takes two hundred.

He counted them in the first six minutes, in fours, the way he was taught to count a room in the fourth house.

Eleven of them he has read about. Four of them he has been in a room with. One of them sat at a table on the eighteenth of March and did not eat.

He came in at five to eleven. He saw Georgij at the back, and he went and stood on the other side of a pillar, and he did not come out from behind it again.

Nobody spoke to Georgij at any point in the whole of it.

That is not the same as nobody noticing him, and he knew the difference by the fourth minute.

There is a photograph at the front and it is not a recent one.

The man in it has a full head of black hair and no weight under the jaw.

He is wearing the sort of suit a man wears who has not yet worked out that he can afford a better one.

He is about thirty-five.

Somebody had a choice of twenty-five years of photographs of that man and went back past all of them.

The monk began at eleven exactly and it went on for a long time, and the chanting is not for the people in the room and does not care whether they follow it.

At the part where it is done, they went up in twos and threes and bowed twice.

Georgij went up with the rest and bowed twice and did it correctly. Doing it wrong would have been a thing about him, and he had not come there to be a thing about himself.

He did not put his head down for any longer than the form asks for.

He is the only man in that hall who is not sorry Choi Dae-ho is dead, and he did not arrange his face to look as if he were.

Gong Doo-hyun stood at the front of it from eleven until twenty past one.

He is about sixty and he is not a large man, and there is nothing about him that would make anybody look at him twice in a street.

He thanked them one at a time, in a line, and did not hurry any of them, and the line went out of the door and down the steps for the last forty minutes of it.

Georgij was the last man in the line, because he wanted the whole of it in front of him before he had to be in it.

"Thank you for coming," said Gong Doo-hyun.

He had said it about ninety times by then, and he said it to Georgij in the same voice he had used on the man in front of him, who was seventy and had wept twice.

Georgij bowed the way the man in front of him had.

"You are the only person in this hall who does not know anybody in it."

"Yes."

"There are three others who do not, and they are from the hall, the crematorium, and the office that sent out the notices." Gong Doo-hyun had his hands at his sides and did not do anything with them. "I am going to say one thing to you, and then you may go and stand anywhere you like, or leave, and I will not mind either."

"Then say it."

Georgij did not put anything in front of it.

"He was thirty-five and he was the best man in that building at the thing he did." Gong Doo-hyun said it with no weight on it anywhere. "He took four companies apart on paper and he was right about all four of them, and there was not one of the four that anybody in that service could have done in the time he did it."

Georgij did not move at all.

"The fifth one he did not take apart."

The monk was putting things away at the front and there was a woman folding cloth, and the hall had begun to sound like a room again.

"That is what I wanted to say to you today, and I am not going to finish it here."

"May I say one thing to you?"

"You may say anything you like." Gong Doo-hyun did not step back and did not look at the door. "I am going to remember all of it, and you know that, and you have decided to say it anyway, and I would like to hear what a man says under that condition."

"I have been told twice what he was at thirty-five," said Georgij. "Both times by somebody who was not sorry."

Gong Doo-hyun looked at him for about two seconds.

"Yes," he said. "That will be most of the people you talk to."

He turned to the woman who had been waiting behind Georgij for a minute and a half, and thanked her for coming, and said her husband's name, and asked whether the road up had been bad.

It was the ninety-first time and it was not a shorter one than the first.

The man who had stood behind the pillar went out at the same time Georgij did, because there is one door.

He did not say anything and he did not look up, and at the top of the steps he stopped and put a hand on the rail for a moment and then went on down.

He was at that table on the eighteenth of March and he was one of the four who ate.

Georgij has read six pages about him. He knows what the man does for a living, what he was sold, what it has cost him since, and the name of the firm that is going to buy the rest of it by the autumn.

None of that says why he came today, and there is nowhere to look it up.

Georgij went down the steps past the woman with the bucket and got into the car and did not tell Jang to start it for about a minute.

He worked out in the car that the man had not asked him one question.

Not what he was, not who had sent him, and not what he had to do with any of it.

And not why a man nobody in that hall knew had come up a lane in Jongno on a Wednesday morning.

He has spent two years getting what he needs out of people by asking them something small enough to answer.

The other man did not want anything out of him today, and there is no version of that which is good.

He took the notebook out at the lights on Yulgok-ro.

The line he had written on Tuesday the eighteenth had the date under it.

*Everybody in that room will be somebody he wanted in it.*

It is true, and it is not what he meant by it.

He had read it as a list. A man who fills a hall fills it with people who are useful to him, and the useful ones are the ones who owe something or want something. A hall of ninety is ninety names and about eleven of them worth having.

There is nobody in that hall who owed Choi Dae-ho anything by the end of it.

There is nobody in it who gained by coming, and there are eleven who are in a service that keeps a note of who was seen where.

Choi Dae-ho has been out of that service for twenty-five years and out of the trade since March and dead since April, and there is no version of standing in that hall that pays.

They came because they were asked, and the man who asked them is the one man in the Republic of Korea who is not able to give any of them anything.

The hall was the cheaper of the two. The card was the sort a stationer keeps a box of. Nobody catered it and there was no car for the family, because there is no family.

Georgij went at the money on the way back, because that is what he does, and there is nothing in it.

It was paid for out of one man's own pocket, in a sum a man on a public salary would notice for a month or two.

There is no fund behind it. Nobody was asked to contribute and nothing went on anybody's account.

The only extravagant thing in the whole arrangement is his own name at the bottom of ninety cards.

* * *

He told her all of it in order at about seven, and it took eleven minutes, and he left nothing out of it.

"And what did you get," said Annie.

"Nothing I can use."

She let that stand for a moment.

"Say the rest of it."

"He is going to finish that sentence somewhere else. He has told me that he is going to and he has not said when, and he will not say when, because the not saying is most of it." Georgij had his hands behind his back. "And I am going to go when he does."

"Yes."

"I would like to say the other thing, because I am going to have thought it either way."

"Then say it."

"I have spent two years finding out what men have done," said Georgij. "It has worked every time, and it worked because there has always been something."

Annie did not write anything down.

"I went at that man for four hours today. The hall is cheap and the card is cheap and the money is his own." He did not move anywhere. "And every single thing he did in that building, he did under his own name in front of ninety people."

"And."

"And there is nothing to find, and I have never once been in front of that." He did not soften it. "I did not know until this afternoon that all of it rested on the other man being dirty."

Annie put the pen down.

"Every one of them was," she said.

"Yes."

"That is not the same as it being the reason it worked, and you have just said the difference out loud, and I want it noted that you said it and not me."

Downstairs somebody shut a door and did not shut it hard.

"It is noted," said Georgij.
