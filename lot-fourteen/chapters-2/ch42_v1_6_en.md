# Chapter 42: The line above it
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.6 · EN

## Day Two Hundred and Seventy-Nine · Thursday 9 July

He had given K two days and had nothing to show for either of them.

---

Annie had pointed him at the places that keep no book, and he had taken the direction seriously enough to spend Tuesday and Wednesday inside it. A bathhouse with private rooms keeps a till roll and a laundry count, which he established in one telephone call to a linen supplier who had no reason to lie about towels. A golf course keeps tee times and a roster, and the only way to read the roster is to join the club, which he was not going to do for four names on a page. A boat keeps nothing at all until it leaves a harbour, and then the harbour keeps a record of the boat and not of the men on it.

---

The boat had held him most of Wednesday, because it was the first of the three that opened at all. Then he saw the shape of it properly. To read a departure record he had to know the boat, and to know the boat he had to know the man, and the man was what he was looking for.

---

He wrote the two days down in the notebook without dressing them up.

---

*Two days on K. Three kinds of place, no document anywhere that puts a name next to a date. Nothing.*

---

There was nothing else on the desk, so he read his own four lines again, and this time he looked at them as a document rather than as a source.

---

Four entries, one under another, in one hand, one pen, one sitting. He had taken them off pages that were months and years apart and set them down as a list. The list was tidy, and it was his, and it was the only version of Sang-hoon's calendar he had looked at since the first week of June.

---

Two weeks ago he had written down Jang's rota and Jang's grocery numbers in the order Jang had given them to him. He had put the reason beside them, which was that the order a thing is noticed in is worth more than a tidy one imposed afterward. He had written that about another man's work and had not once turned it round.

---

He sat still for a while with his hands flat on either side of the page.

---

*K has no date because I did not copy one, which is not the same as there having been none.*

---

He telephoned Sang-hoon before he had finished thinking it through, because another hour would only have made it easier to say.

"I would like to see the calendar itself," said Georgij. "Not my copy of it."

"Say what is wrong with your copy."

"I made it. In the first week of June I sat with your book at a table in your outer office and took four lines out of it, and I left everything those four lines were sitting next to." He put one hand flat on the four copied lines. "K has no date in my copy. I no longer know whether it had one in yours."

---

The line was quiet for long enough that he could hear a door somewhere on the other end of it.

"How long have you had that thought?"

"Since about ten this morning."

"Five weeks," said Sang-hoon. "That is not a complaint. That book has been on my own desk for eight years and I have read those four lines more often than I have read anything else in it, and I have never once read what was above them either. Two of us made the same mistake and only one of us was hired to make it."

---

Georgij took that without putting anything on top of it.

"I will send it this afternoon," Sang-hoon said. "You may have the pages those four sit on. Not the book. There are things further back in it that are mine and are not this, and I would like them to stay that way."

"Understood."

"There is nothing stopping you. That is the part I would like you to notice, because I have noticed it."

"I have noticed it."

"Good. My man will wait in the hall and take it back tonight. He does not know what he is carrying and I would rather he went on not knowing."

* * *

The book came at four, brought by a man in a grey suit who sat down in the hall with his hat on his knees and refused tea twice.

---

It was one long book and not a set of year volumes. Georgij had not known that, because a copyist does not see the binding of a thing while he is looking at a line in the middle of it. Sang-hoon dated an entry when he thought the entry worth dating and left it alone when he did not. The hand was the same on every page and had got smaller over the years rather than looser.

---

Georgij folded two pieces of paper and set one at each end of the section he had been given, so that his own hand would have to lift something to go past them.

---

He went backwards first, because the pages carried no year. Sang-hoon wrote a year at the top of a page only when one had ended and another had started, and there were eight of those in the whole book. Georgij found the one he needed and came forward again, counting, and the counting took him under a quarter of an hour.

---

Then he found the note.

---

*K. Late.*

---

Above it, in the same hand and half a line higher, an entry dated the twenty-fourth of February. Below it, three lines down, one dated the twenty-sixth.

---

He read the three dates twice before he let himself write anything, because he had spent this whole year learning what happens to a man who is pleased with a page too early.

---

Then he did the rest of it, and the rest of it took a minute. S was on the page for the twentieth of March, with the time written beside it. His own copy said March, four years ago, and no day at all, and he had rounded H and Y the same way and had not thought about it once.

---

Between the last possible day for K and the evening of S there were twenty-two days. Between the first possible day and the same evening there were twenty-four.

---

He telephoned Sang-hoon at half past five, with the book still open and the man in the grey suit still in the hall.

"The twenty-fourth, twenty-fifth or twenty-sixth of February, four years ago," said Georgij. "K is one of those three days. S is the twentieth of March. Whatever K was, it was three weeks before it and not three years."

---

Sang-hoon did not answer for a while.

"Say the three days again."

"The twenty-fourth, the twenty-fifth, the twenty-sixth."

"Then it was not a dinner." Something moved on the other end, a chair or a drawer. "I have been telling you for six weeks that I made four decisions at that man's table. Three of them were at his table. That one was in my own office, at the end of a day, and he came to me, which he had never done before and did not do again."

"What you remember about it."

"Twenty minutes, standing up, with his coat still on. I wrote three words afterward and did not date them because there was nothing to date." He stopped and came back to it. "That is why it says late. It was late in the day. I have read that word for six weeks as an hour in an evening and it was never an evening at all."

"What did he ask you?"

"He asked whether I was going to the spring sale on the river." Sang-hoon said it the way a man says a thing he has just heard properly for the first time. "I said no. I had not been in years and I had no reason to go, and I told him so, and he thanked me and put his hat on and left, and I have never once counted that as a conversation."

---

Georgij put one finger on the twenty-fourth of February and left it there.

"You are going to tell me he did not want me in that room," said Sang-hoon.

"No," said Georgij. "He did not need you out of it. He needed to be able to tell somebody else, with certainty, that you would not be in it."

---

The silence after that was a different shape from the one before it.

"Three weeks later," said Sang-hoon, "I sat down at his table and made the first of the three I do know about."

"Yes."

"And the sale on the river that spring is the one you have been counting from."

"It is."

* * *

He brought it up to her at nine, and she had the door open before he reached it, which meant Mrs Seo had told her he had been on the telephone twice.

"Say it in the order you got it," said Annie.

---

He did, and it took under three minutes, and she did not interrupt any of it. She was standing at the window when he started and was still standing there when he stopped.

"Twenty-two days," she said.

"Twenty-two at the shortest and twenty-four at the longest. I cannot make it exact, and the three days stay three days until something else closes them."

"You do not need it exact." She came away from the window. "Say what you think you have."

"The first of the four, dated, with a place to start counting from. And an evening in a spring that already has an auction in it, and a doctor in the July after it, and a woman who has been at a window ever since."

---

Annie sat down on the edge of the desk with her back half to him and looked at the open book.

"That is what you have. Now say what he actually gave away, because you told Park Sang-hoon what that man wanted and you have not once said what he got."

---

Georgij thought about that.

"That he would answer," he said.

"Yes." She put one hand flat on the page and did not turn it. "A man walks into an office at the end of a day with his coat on and asks a question he already knows the answer to. He does not want the answer. He wants to know whether he gets one. It costs Park Sang-hoon nothing to say no to a sale he was never going to attend, and that is the entire point of asking it, because a thing that costs nothing is what you take first."

"Then K is not a decision."

"K is the measurement. The three you have been calling decisions came afterward, and they came afterward because that one went well." She lifted her hand off the page. "He has spent four years unable to account for the smallest of the four. It is the only one of them that was ever about him."

---

Georgij reached past her for the notebook and wrote it down, and she watched him do it.

"You have written that sentence twice," said Annie.

---

He looked. She was right, and the second one stopped halfway through a word.

"I know when you last slept properly," she said. "You do not, which is the part I mind. Finish the line and then stop."

---

He finished the line. Then he went round the desk and down onto the carpet at the end of it, with his back against the drawers.

She stayed where she was on the edge of it while he closed the book and put the two folded pieces of paper in his pocket.

"He should hear it from a person and not a telephone," said Georgij. "I will ask him for an hour this week, Mistress."

"Then it keeps until he gives you one." She reached down without looking and put two fingers on his wrist and took them away again. "Give the man in the grey suit his book and send him home. He has been sitting in my hall for five hours with his hat on his knees, and somebody in this house ought to notice."

---

She put two fingers on his wrist and then sent him to be decent to a stranger, and only one of those was an instruction.
