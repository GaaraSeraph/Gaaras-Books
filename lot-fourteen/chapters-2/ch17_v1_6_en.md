# Chapter 17: The face on it
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.6 · EN

## Day Two Hundred and Six · Monday 27 April

He came in at about half past seven with the notebook and did not sit down on the floor.

"I have nine lines," he said. "Eight of them are worth nothing. I am going to read you all nine anyway, because if I read you the one you will hear it differently."

"Read them."

---

He read them.

Sixty or a little less. Not tall. Ate nothing at a lunch, not one thing. Pleasant, quiet, the sort of man who has been in rooms. Introduced in a way that did not carry a name. Telephoned in the first week of March. Knew what post that office receives. Asked for two copies, one to send and one for the file. Was extremely interested in the word honest.

And then the ninth.

"At the end of the lunch he stood up and shook Mr Ok's hand and said that he was glad they had finally met."

"They had never met before," said Georgij.

---

Eight of the nine lines described a man. The ninth described what he does.

---

Annie was standing at the window when he began and she was still standing at it when he finished, and nothing whatever happened to her face.

That is not the same as nothing happening.

---

He has watched her hear four hundred things in this room since October. He knows the six or seven small things that occur when she is surprised, when she is bored, when she has already had it from somebody else that afternoon and is letting him say it anyway.

None of the six or seven happened.

She went entirely still in the way that a person does not go still by accident, and she held it for about two seconds too long, and then she turned round.

"You know him," said Georgij.

"Yes."

---

There it was, and it was the first time she had ever answered that question with a word.

"Since when."

"Long before you. That is the only part of it that is not going to be useful to you." She came away from the window. "Ask me the next one properly, because you have been building up to it since the fourteenth of March and I want it in a full sentence."

"Will you give me his name?"

"No."

---

Georgij put the notebook down on the desk.

"Then I am going to argue with you about it," he said, "and I would like to say before I start that I know I am going to lose, and that I am doing it anyway."

"Do it anyway."

"In December it was that I was better without it, and you were right and it worked and I have said so." He kept his hands at his sides. "In March it was that I would get it exactly right, and that getting it exactly right in front of a man who reads arrangements is the same as signing my name. That was also right and I have behaved as though it was."

"Go on."

"Both of those were about protecting the work." He did not hurry any of it. "There is no work left to protect. He has spent me. He put a page in front of a woman in Ulsan in March, and I have spent six weeks taking her apart with my own hands, and I have made a very great deal of noise doing it. Whatever I was on the second of March, I am not it now."

---

Annie did not interrupt him.

"And there is a second half," said Georgij. "It is not an argument and I am going to say it anyway."

"Say it."

"On Thursday I am going to a yard in Ulsan for the third time. She has asked one question since January and it is the same question every month for twenty years, which is whether anybody is going to answer her." He looked at her. "I can give her the whole of it except one word. And the one word is the only part she would actually want."

---

The clock in the hall did the thing it does.

"That is the worst argument you have ever made in this room," said Annie, "and it is the first one that has ever been difficult to listen to, and I want you to understand that those are two different sentences."

"I know."

---

She sat down at the desk.

"Now I am going to answer you, and I am going to do it properly, because you have earned that and because I am not going to hide behind December for the rest of my life."

"He does not act," she said. "Not once, not ever, not in twenty-four years. He has never given an instruction that anybody could repeat, and he has never asked anybody for anything they could refuse. What he does is put two people in the same room who each have half of something, and then he goes home."

"I know that. I worked that out on a bridge on the ninth of April."

"You worked out that he does it. You have not worked out what it costs him." She put both hands flat. "It costs him nothing at all. He can do it eleven times a year for the rest of his life and there is no version of it that is ever illegal, ever provable or ever even impolite. That is not a man with a method. That is a man who has found a way to live."

"And the name."

"The name changes exactly one thing," said Annie, "and it is not what you think it is. It does not tell you where he is, because he is at four addresses and none of them is his. It does not tell you what he owns, because he owns nothing that is in his own name and neither do you and you of all people should be able to see the shape of that."

---

Georgij did not say anything for a moment.

"What it changes is you."

"Say it."

"You have walked past him twice," said Annie. "Once in October and once in March, and both times you did the correct thing, and both times the correct thing was invisible because you did not know he was there. The day you have his name, everything you do about him is a decision. You will not go at a company because it is his. You will be careful at a dinner because he might be at it. You will read a guest list differently." She did not raise her voice at any point. "And he reads guest lists for a living."

"So the name is the thing that makes me legible."

"The name is the thing that makes you legible. It is the only thing in this that has ever been able to. You have spent eight weeks being the one man in Seoul he cannot see, and it has been entirely accidental." She let that sit. "I am not going to spend it on a Thursday afternoon in a yard in Ulsan so that a woman who tried to blackmail me can feel answered."

---

Georgij stood there and did not have anything.

"That is the argument," said Annie. "It is better than yours and we both know it, and I am sorry, and being sorry does not move it."

---

He heard the word and did not react to it, because reacting to it would have made her regret saying it, and she says it perhaps twice a year.

"May I have one thing instead?" he said.

"Ask."

"Not the name. One true thing about him that I can use, that does not tell me where he is or what he owns."

---

Annie thought about that for a while, and it was a real pause and not a performance.

"He eats," she said.

---

Georgij looked up.

"He eats a great deal, and he enjoys it, and he does it at his own table and nowhere else." She said it very precisely. "He does not eat in front of people he is working on. It is not a discipline and it is not a superstition, it is simply that he cannot do both at once, and he has never in his life noticed that it shows."

"That is not one true thing," said Georgij. "That is a rule about a room."

"Yes."

"It means that Mr Ok was being worked on at a lunch in January and did not know it."

"It means rather more than that." Annie picked up the notebook and turned it round and read the ninth line again. "It means that every man in this city who has ever sat down to a meal with him and watched him eat is a man he was not working on. And I can think of five of those and you have met two of them."

---

She had given him a test he could run on his own memory, and no way at all to check the answer.

---

Georgij stood absolutely still.

"You are not going to tell me which two."

"No. You will get there in about four days and it will be yours when you do." She put the notebook down. "That is the difference between a name and a thing you can use, and I have just given you the second one, and it is worth more and you know it is."

---

He went at it on the stairs afterwards and did not get anywhere, which is correct. A thing a man gets on a staircase is a thing he would have got sitting down.

Two men he has eaten with, out of everybody he has eaten with since October. There have been nine of those meals and he can name every one of them without looking at anything.

He did not write the list tonight. He will write it on Wednesday, on paper, with dates, in the order the meals happened, because that is how a list stops being a feeling.

And one of the nine was the eighteenth of November, and he already knows what he is going to find when he gets to it, and he is going to write it down in its place anyway.

---

He picked up the notebook and did not open it.

"Thank you, Mistress."

"Do not thank me for that. I have been sitting on the whole of it since December and I have just given you the smallest piece I could get away with, and both of us are aware of it."

"Yes."

"Say it, then. You are permitted to."

"You are still holding it," said Georgij.

"I am still holding it."

"And on the day you stop holding it, it will be because it has stopped being safe and not because I asked."

"Yes."

---

She looked at him.

"Does that make you angry?"

---

There it was, and it is the first question she has ever asked him that he had to think about before he could answer it.

"No," he said, in the end. "It makes me tired, and I have not been tired since the second of March, and I would rather be angry. Anger is quicker."

---

Annie did not say anything to that for a moment.

Then she got up and came round the desk and stood in front of him. She put her hand flat on the side of his neck above the collar, which she has done perhaps three times since October and never once in that room.

"Go to bed," she said. "You are going to Ulsan on Thursday and you are not going to say one untrue thing to that woman. You are going to manage it because you have not got the name and cannot give it to her."

"That is not why you kept it."

"No," said Annie. "But it is going to be true on Thursday anyway."
