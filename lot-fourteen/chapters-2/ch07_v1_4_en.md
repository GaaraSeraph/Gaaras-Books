# Chapter 7: Not the first
*Lot Fourteen* · Version 1.4 · EN

## Day One Hundred and Sixty-Six · Wednesday 18 March

Mrs Jeon had the receipt book shut when he came in, which was the whole of her greeting and was enough.

"He rang on Monday night," she said. "At ten past eight. He talked for four minutes and he did not once ask me a question, which I think means he had decided before he picked up the telephone."

"He would have."

"He told me what he was going to say to whoever called him, and then he said it to me first so that I would know it was not going to be flowery." She looked at her hands. "It was not flowery. I have written it down."

---

"Then here is yours."

---

She did not make him ask twice and she did not stretch it out.

"A lot cannot leave this building without a bill unless somebody signs a release for it. One page. It says what is going out and who has authorised it, and it does not say who is receiving it, because the receiving is what the bill is for and there is no bill."

"So I get the man who let it out of the building. Not the man who took it home."

"You get the man who let it out. Nobody at this desk has ever had the other one, on that evening or any other."

---

"Three times in twenty-three years," she said. "That is all. Two of them are old and one of them is the one you are asking about."

"And the name on all three."

"Hwang," said Mrs Jeon. "He was Mr Hwang then and he is Mr Hwang now, and in between he was Mr Hwang at three other houses."

---

Georgij had read that name once before.

It was in six point at the bottom of a page, under four sentences about method, in the small room at the house on the second of March. He had read the four sentences twice, because they were the best thing in that book. He had not read the name at all.

---

"He was here as a young man," she said. "Then he went away for eight years and did other houses, and he did them well enough that people still talk about two of them. He came back in the autumn."

"And he is the new man."

"He is the new man. He is going to put me out of this building and I am going to tell you the truth about him anyway, because you have not asked me to say anything unkind and I would not."

---

"He is the most honest person I have ever worked for. He does not take anything. He does not have a favourite. He came back to a house whose records were a disgrace and he has spent five months making them not a disgrace, and he is proud of it, and he has every right to be."

---

Georgij did not write any of that down.

"He tidied the catalogue," he said.

"He tidied everything. That was the first thing he did." She said it flatly and without any idea what she was handing over. "Permanent references, one series, the whole thirty years in one sequence. Before him you could not find a lot from four years ago without knowing which of the four systems it had been entered under. Now a girl of nineteen can find anything in the place in about a minute."

---

"And the back page."

"The back page is his as well. He put the retained ones together in one list because they were scattered and because a thing you cannot find is a thing that goes wrong later." Mrs Jeon put her palm flat on the shut receipt book. "He is not a man who would use it. He would not know how. He made it findable because findable is correct."

---

Georgij stood in the first-floor corridor afterwards for about as long as it takes to put a cup down.

A man had come back to a house that was in a mess. He had spent five months doing his job better than anybody had ever done it in that building. In doing it he had built the thing that is now pointed at the woman who owns him.

There is no fault anywhere in that sentence. That is what makes it the worst one he has had all month.

---

"One more thing and then you should go," said Mrs Jeon through the glass. "He keeps everything. Every note, every release, every letter that has ever come into this house about anything. He is proud of that too."

"Thank you."

"I have not done you a kindness. I have been paid, properly, by somebody who told me the price out loud before he agreed to it, and I would like that written down somewhere as well."

* * *

Mrs Seo met him in the hall at ten past two, which she does not.

"She has been in the small room since eleven," she said. "She asked whether you were back, twice, and she has not asked for anything else."

---

The envelope was on the desk and had been opened along the top with something proper, not a finger.

Annie was standing behind the desk with her arms folded and she did not say anything at all. She turned it round so that it faced him and moved one finger about an inch, which meant read it.

---

He read the date first.

The eighteenth of March. He looked at that for a second and a half before he read a single other word on the page, and the whole afternoon was already different by the time he got to the salutation.

---

The letterhead is an association of six independent shippers on one lane out of Ulsan. It has existed for twenty years. Underneath the name of the association there is a telephone number and no fax number and no address for electronic mail.

The letter is typed. It is signed by hand, and under the signature it says *Chair*, which is the only title the woman has.

---

> I have written to six people on the first of every month for twenty years. I am writing to you on the eighteenth because I no longer have a first to wait for.
>
> I know what stands against your name in the records of a house near the river in Seoul. I know it twice. I know what the words on the back page mean, and I know that neither of them can be read by anybody who does not already know what he is looking at.
>
> I am not going to sell it and I am not going to give it to a newspaper. Neither of those puts one container on my lane. I have thought about it for four days and I am telling you the truth about it so that you do not have to spend a week working out what I intend.
>
> Since January my members have moved nothing. I have not been told why. Nobody has refused me anything, because nobody has had to. I am fifty-eight and I have run one lane since my brother died, and I have never in that time been unable to find out what was happening to it.
>
> I want an hour. I want the six of them in the room, and I want somebody to answer them. Not me. Them.
>
> I will hold this until the twenty-sixth of March.
>
> If nobody comes, I will write again on the first, as I always do.

---

Georgij put the letter down on the desk in exactly the position it had been in, square to the edge, which took him a moment longer than it needed to.

---

"Who gave it to her," said Annie.

---

There it was, and he had known it was coming since the second paragraph, and knowing had not made it any easier to say.

"I do not know."

---

He has answered every direct question she has ever asked him. He has declined to answer twice and said so out loud both times. He has never once stood in that room and had nothing.

"Say what you do know."

---

"She has the meaning and not just the page." He stayed on his side of the desk. "Every buyer on that list got a copy of that back page in March. All of them looked at four references and a phrase and put it in a drawer. Somebody sat down with that woman and told her what she was holding."

"Go on."

"She has never held anything outside this trade. She has no reason to know anybody who could explain it to her, and no reason for anybody who could explain it to go and find her in Ulsan." He did not stop. "And it arrived in the fourth week of the worst quarter of her life. Not in January when she could still wait, and not in June when it would be over."

---

"So somebody chose her."

"Somebody chose her, and chose extremely well." Georgij looked at the letterhead. "She is the one person in this who will use it properly and will not be believed to be anybody's. She has a constitution that lets her speak for six firms. She has twenty years of writing letters that nobody answered, which is the most credible thing about her. And she has never met whoever did this and never will."

---

"I said something about her on the seventh," said Annie.

"You said she was not somebody who was going to be told to be patient."

"I did not think she would be this quick." She looked at the letterhead and not at him. "What do you make of her."

---

"There is not one untrue sentence in it." Georgij read the last line of it again. "She has not made what she has sound bigger. She has not pretended to be willing to do something she is not willing to do. She has not asked for money in order to look reasonable. She has told you what she will do and on what date."

"And that is the dangerous part."

"That is the whole danger. A man who lies leaves you something to catch him at." He put one finger on the corner of the page. "She has left nothing at all, and she has done it deliberately, and she has done it because she is certain she is right. She is also right."

---

Annie looked at him for a moment when he had finished saying that.

"What," said Georgij.

"Nothing," said Annie.

---

Annie unfolded her arms and put one hand on the back of the chair.

"How long did you need."

"Four days. Six at the outside."

"For what, precisely."

"Whoever took that lot home. With the fourth name I could have made the whole page dull inside a fortnight." He said it without any weight on it. "I got the near end this morning at eleven."

---

Annie did not say anything to that, which was the correct thing to do and cost her something, because she is not a person who leaves a silence where a sentence would be kinder.

---

"I am not going to answer it," she said.

"No."

"Say why, so that I know you have got there and not just agreed with me."

---

"Because an hour in a room is the confirmation." He turned the letter a quarter turn so that it lay square. "There is nothing on that page that says the veto is yours. There is a phrase in a book and a name she has attached to it herself, and until somebody responds to it, that is all she has. The moment a woman from your house sits down in front of six shippers in Ulsan, everybody in that room knows she was right."

"And if I do nothing."

"Then on the first of April she writes again, and she writes to six people, and she has been doing that for twenty years without once being told to stop."

---

"So it is the same box as the book," said Annie.

"It is exactly the same box. Everything you touch confirms it. That is now true twice and it is going to go on being true, because it is not a coincidence. It is the shape of what is being done to you."

---

She went to the window.

"Then somebody who is not out of this house is going to have to go to Ulsan," she said, and she did not turn round, and Georgij did not answer that, because it was not a question and because she already knew what he was.

---

He went up at about six and got the notebook out of the coat and turned back to the fifth of March.

He had read the association's constitution that Thursday, in the middle of three days of other people's paper, and he had made a note about it in his own hand.

*Mostly nothing. One clause about who may speak for the members in a dealing with a carrier.*

---

He sat and looked at that for a while.

He had held the only piece of standing that woman possesses, two weeks ago, in a room in this house, and he had written *mostly nothing* next to it and turned the page.

He did not tear it out and he did not correct it. He wrote the date underneath and put the notebook back in the coat.
